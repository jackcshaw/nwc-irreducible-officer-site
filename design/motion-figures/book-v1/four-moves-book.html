The four moves

Four judgments that stay with a person

AI can draft, search, compare and recommend. Four judgments still belong to the person who will answer for the work.

Each move opens a chapter with two pages: what the move asks, the failures it guards against, what an educator can ask, and the evidence worth keeping.

Each failure mode then gets a spread. The left page says what goes wrong and what faculty can ask. The right page holds a short figure that shows it happening.

The order matters. A learner who never framed the problem has nothing to calibrate against and nothing to own.

The essay was written for the National War College. Judgment Lab adapts the same moves for higher education and high school as designs to test. The examples in the figures are constructed.

The four movesSection IV

Move 1 of 4

Frame the problem.

Decide what problem you are solving, why it matters, and what would count as a good answer. Do it before AI hands you a structure.

“The framing is where the judgment lives.”

Section IV, Purpose as the Irreducible Human Act

A tightly bounded prompt makes the hard choices for the learner, and that kind of structure is where AI works best. Ask learners to set the frame first.

Frame the problem · in practice

Guards against

. The first plausible frame becomes the student’s frame.

. A request for help transfers criteria, structure, or meaning.

Ask

What problem did the student choose to solve?

What did the AI decide for the student?

Keep as evidence

A one-page frame: the problem, key assumptions, criteria for success, the evidence standard, and the role AI played.

Pilot step 1

Unaided frame. Students frame the problem without AI, scored on completion.

The four movesSection V

Move 2 of 4

Calibrate the tool.

Learn where AI tends to be strong and where it tends to fail on this kind of task. Then decide what to accept, check, revise, or refuse.

“Students need to predict, with reasonable accuracy, when AI performs well for a given type of task and when it does not, and calibrate their use accordingly.”

Section V, Appropriate Reliance as a Teachable Competency

Calibrate the tool · in practice

Guards against

. The student accepts confident output without knowing whether the task fits the tool.

Ask

What evidence justified relying on the system here?

What counts

  1. TrustA feeling about the system
  2. UseAI appeared in the work
  3. Reliance decisionA choice you can question
  4. The choice fits the task, risk and evidence

Keep as evidence

Which outputs the learner accepted, modified, rejected, verified, or kept AI away from, with a reason for each.

Pilot step 2

AI challenge. Students ask AI to attack their frame and record what they accepted, rejected and changed.

The four movesSection VI

Move 3 of 4

Refuse the garden path.

A garden path is work a fluent template answer can finish. Set problems where the template answer is wrong, or where several frames hold up and the learner must defend one.

“If instructors do not decide which friction matters, AI will decide by default.”

Section VI, Friction as Developmental Design

Let AI remove

Formatting, search, repetitive drafting, clerical assembly.

Protect

Initial framing, failed synthesis, seminar challenge, revision that changes the argument.

Refuse the garden path · in practice

Guards against

. The model connects material before the student has built the map.

. Shared systems and defaults narrow the range of frames available to the class.

Ask

Can the student explain why these connections matter?

What frames are missing across the seminar?

Keep as evidence

A diagnosis of a polished, misframed AI assessment: the hidden frame, the assumptions behind it, the missing evidence, and the point where it fails.

Pilot steps 3–4

Misframed assessment, then diagnosis and revision. The flaw sits in the frame, where it is hardest to see.

The four movesSection VII

Move 4 of 4

Own the decision.

AI can shape the work that leads to a decision. A person answers for the decision itself.

“AI can compress the work before a decision. It cannot own what follows.”

Section VII, Accountability Is Structural

Learners should be able to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why the recommendation is still theirs.

Own the decision · in practice

Guards against

. The model’s recommendation makes agency feel distributed or easier to defend.

. Polished analysis stands in for owned reasoning.

Ask

Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?

Can the student independently explain the judgment in plain speech?

Keep as evidence

A short oral defense: what AI got wrong, what it made easier, where reliance fit and where it was refused, what changed between the first frame and the last, and what evidence would change the recommendation.

Pilot step 5

Oral defense. This is where ownership of the frame shows, or fails to.
