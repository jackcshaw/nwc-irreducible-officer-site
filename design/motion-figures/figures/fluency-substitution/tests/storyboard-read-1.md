# Fluency substitution: storyboard cold read, round 1

Reader: first-time viewer, an educator who hasn't read the essay. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed). I read this before opening any source. The post-read fact check at the end was added afterwards.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1, the task (0–6 s).** A task card appears at the top right and a red dot labelled "Student" at the left. Caption: "A student gets a task that needs a decision." Clear. The dot is a student and the task asks for a yes or no. The card and the dot are far apart, but with nothing else on stage that doesn't matter.

**Beat 2, the AI drafts (6–15 s).** The dot pulses, a sheet tagged "AI'S DRAFT" appears, and a grey paragraph writes itself. With the caption, I read the pulse as "asks". Grey with a band plus the tag teaches me "grey = AI" well. The paragraph: "Any course must weigh escalation against the ally losing trust. Cost and readiness elsewhere matter too. A measured, flexible approach, adjusted as conditions change, offers the best path forward."
- My honest first reaction: this is thin, not impressive. It's 29 words, and "a measured, flexible approach… best path forward" is so obviously a hedge that I don't believe it would fool a war-college faculty member. The figure needs me to feel the paragraph is persuasive, and I didn't. More on this under Problems.

**Beat 3, kept as their own (15–23.5 s).** The dot runs along the lines, then swaps "course" for "option" and "path" for "way" in red. A wipe turns everything ink, and the tag becomes "THE STUDENT'S PAPER". The mechanics are clear: the AI's authorship vanishes and the label claims the paper.
- What I read into it, though, is a student skimming (0.5 s a line is far faster than reading) and then swapping synonyms. That's the classic move for disguising copied text. To a teacher it looks like academic dishonesty, not a sincere student mistaking good prose for their own thinking.
- I may still be reading the paragraph when the edits and the wipe start (19–21.7 s). The colours change under my eyes mid-sentence. That's minor, because the words don't change.

**Beat 4, read it with us (23.5–33 s).** Four underlines (escalation, the ally losing trust, cost, readiness elsewhere), then a dashed empty box labelled "MATTERS MOST". Caption: "Read it with us: it weighs four risks and never says which matters most."
- This is the clearest beat. The empty dashed box reads instantly as "blank, nobody filled this in".
- Small snags. "Us" made me ask who is reading with me: the narrator? Faculty? Also, cost isn't really a "risk", and the paragraph half-ranks already: escalation vs. trust is the main weighing, and the other two "matter too". A sharp reader will object that it does say something about priority, just not between the top two.
- The slot is on screen from 30.5 to 34.0 s, about 3.5 s, before the next caption arrives and it fades. That's short for the one new symbol the rest of the figure depends on. It comes back in beat 7, so it's recoverable.

**Beat 5, another student decides first (33–41 s).** Paper A shrinks to the top left. A second red dot, "Another student", writes in red on a blank sheet: "Send the forces. Losing the ally's trust is the bigger risk." Clear. Red = the student's own words is now firmly established (the edits were red too).

**Beat 6, two polished papers (41–50.5 s).** The AI continues paper B in grey ("Escalation can be managed with clear limits and open channels. Cost and readiness elsewhere are real but secondary."). Then a wipe, a shrink to the lower left, and the faculty labels. Caption: "They ask an AI to draft the rest. Faculty receive two equally polished papers."
- This is the busiest beat: a pulse, the tag, the AI text composing, a wipe, a move and three labels, under a two-sentence caption. I would be reading the caption while the AI text composes. By the time I look, it has been wiped and shrunk. So I'd miss the one place the figure shows an AI working inside the student's frame ("real but secondary"), which I think is quietly important.
- "They" briefly read to me as "both students". It's singular they, but straight after "Another student" it's ambiguous.
- "Equally polished" collided with what I'd just learned. Beat 4 taught me paper A fails on its face, and paper B opens with a plain decision. So the papers aren't alike to me: I can already see which one decides. I ended this beat thinking "faculty could tell these apart by reading them."
- The same wipe that felt like the wrongdoing in beat 3 ("keeps it as their own") happens here to the good student, with no comment. I wasn't sure whether hiding the AI's part is bad or beside the point.

**Beat 7, papers aside (50.5–59.5 s).** The papers dim, the question appears at top centre, two dots appear on the right in line with their papers, and an empty slot sits at the end of each row. The two-row table layout reads well: paper, student, answer, slot. Dimming as "set aside" works, with the caption's help. The dots have lost their labels, but row position makes clear which student is which.

**Beat 8, the first student answers (59.5–69.5 s).** Two phrases on dimmed paper A turn grey again, lift off and float into a speech panel: "Well… weigh escalation against the ally losing trust… a measured, flexible approach." The slot pulses and stays empty. The meaning is clear: the student can only repeat the AI's words.
- I had a flicker of "is the student reading off the paper?", because words travel from the paper that was just set aside. The caption resolves it.
- The returning grey is the figure being omniscient (faculty can't see the grey), which is fine as narration. A viewer could take it to mean "faculty can spot the AI phrases", though.
- The answer is a stammered fragment. It makes the first student look unprepared rather than fluent, which is the opposite of the failure the title names. Real fluency substitution would sound smooth under questioning: "I'd take a measured approach that balances escalation against the ally's confidence." Said in a seminar, that might even sound wise.

**Beat 9, the second student answers (69.5–79 s).** In red: "Send them. Losing the ally's trust is the bigger risk. Trust is hard to rebuild." The slot turns solid red: "Ally's trust". Clear.
- Comparing the two answers, I noticed the second student also mostly repeats their own paper. Their first two sentences are nearly verbatim. The only new material is "Trust is hard to rebuild", which is thin and which I wouldn't have spotted as "not on the paper" without the storyboard's Notice line. In real time, both students recite their papers. One paper had a decision in it and one didn't.
- The answer finishes arriving at 75.4 s, and reading 15 words takes me about 5 s, so it's tight before beat 10. Beat 10 doesn't move the picture, so the answer stays readable.

**Beat 10, the faculty check (79–85.7 s, then rests).** "FACULTY CHECK: Can the student independently explain the judgment in plain speech?", then "THEN ASK: What evidence would change your recommendation?" The figure rests here, so nothing is rushed. The follow-up is a new idea with no picture, and it is the question I think actually separates owning from reciting. It's never shown being asked or answered.

## 2. The point, in my words

An AI can write a paper that sounds like a considered judgment without making one, so to find out whether a student made the call, put the paper away and ask them in plain words what matters most and why.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Put your paper away. What would you do, which risk drives that, and what would make you change your mind?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Partly. Without the animation it says: two papers; one student echoes phrases from their paper and leaves the "matters most" box empty; the other answers plainly and fills it; under it, a check about explaining in plain speech. The table layout (paper, student, answer, slot) reads well at a glance.

What it loses:
- **The AI disappears.** No text in the final frame mentions AI: not the task card, the question, the row labels, the answers or the check. Grey is decoded only by the "AI'S DRAFT" tags, which are gone by the end, and paper B shows no trace of AI at all. As a poster or print view, it reads as a generic oral-defense story (one student can't explain their paper, one can). It doesn't say "AI fluency stood in for a decision".
- **Red reads as a red pen.** Without beats 3 and 5 to teach that red means "the student's own", a teacher's reflex is red = corrected or wrong, and the good student's answer and slot are the red ones.
- **The papers give it away.** Paper A is readable in the still and visibly doesn't decide, while paper B opens with "Send the forces." The still shows a difference on the page, so it doesn't show why setting the papers aside was needed.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Confusion about what the test detects (the main problem).** The figure shows the difference between the students on the page before the oral question is asked:
   - Beat 4 has me find paper A's missing decision just by reading it.
   - Paper B states its decision in its first sentence.
   - In beat 9 the second student mostly recites their own paper back.

   So the oral check looks like it detects "did the paper make a call?", not "does the student own the reasoning?". A teacher could leave with "require a clear thesis statement", or worse, "a student who used AI passes as long as the AI picked a side". The hard case is the one the title promises and the figure never shows: an AI draft that does commit to a recommendation, kept by a student who can't defend it. "Equally polished" in beat 6 also contradicts what beat 4 just taught. The AI's draft feels too hollow to fool anyone, so I never felt the danger. The follow-up ("What evidence would change your recommendation?") is what would separate owning from reciting, and it appears only as text at the end.
2. **Unfair to the first student, and it shifts the failure into dishonesty.** A skim at 0.5 s a line followed by two synonym swaps reads as disguising copied work. The stammered "Well… weigh escalation…" reads as unprepared. The concept, as the page copy states it, is a student who sincerely mistakes polished language for their own reasoning, and that student would be fluent when questioned, not stammering. Related: beat 3 frames the wipe (AI authorship hidden, paper relabelled as the student's) as the wrong act, then the good student does the same in beat 6 with no comment. A teacher who cares about AI disclosure will get mixed signals about whether disclosure is the issue.
3. **The final frame loses the AI** (see section 4). For the poster, print and reduced-motion views this is the whole figure, and "AI" appears nowhere on it.
4. **Beat 6 is too full.** It carries two ideas and about five motions. The AI's continuation of paper B composes while I'm reading the caption, then is wiped and shrunk before I can read it. That continuation is the only place the figure shows AI helping inside a student's frame (it ranks cost and readiness as "secondary" because the student set the frame). Missing it means the "good use of AI" half of the story goes by unseen. "They ask an AI…" is also briefly ambiguous (one student or both?).
5. **The beat 4 slot is too fast.** The empty "MATTERS MOST" slot is the figure's central symbol. It is on screen about 3.5 s before the next caption arrives and it fades. That's recoverable, since it returns in beat 7.
6. **Decisiveness can pass for judgment.** The question presumes one risk must matter most, so a student who reasons that the risks are genuinely balanced would fail the test as drawn. The second student's reason ("Trust is hard to rebuild") is thin. Paper B also contains an AI claim the student never examined ("Escalation can be managed with clear limits and open channels"), and the figure treats it as harmless. A PME faculty member will notice that confidence isn't the same as owned judgment.
7. **The captions slightly overclaim the picture.** "Four risks": cost and readiness are costs rather than risks, and the paragraph half-ranks by putting escalation vs. trust first and the others as "matter too". "Read it with us": who is "us"?
8. **Colour.** Red as the "decided" colour collides with the red-pen reflex for teachers, and the failing student is red too. The animation teaches the rule by beat 5, so this mostly bites in the still.
9. **Narrow screens.** At 390 px the stage scales to about 0.37, so every word in the picture (all 20 units or smaller) falls under 11 px and is hidden. The animation becomes dots, bands and boxes, and the beat list carries everything. That's acceptable on a desktop-first site, but the phone animation conveys almost nothing alone.
10. **Sourcing.** The figure shows no numbers. Claims I'd want backed:
    - "Epistemic confinement" (Chow et al., 2026), quoted in the page copy;
    - the three essay quotations ("tone of analytic maturity", "retreating to the artifact's language", the Section IX follow-up question);
    - "Failure mode 2 of 7" (where is the list of seven?).
