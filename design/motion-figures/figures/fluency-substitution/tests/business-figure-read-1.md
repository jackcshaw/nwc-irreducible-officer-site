## 2. The point, in one sentence

Two people can hand in the same polished, AI-assisted work and both talk through it convincingly. You only find out whose reasoning was their own when you take the draft away and change a fact.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"For the last recommendation my team signed off on, could the person who wrote it tell me what would change their mind? And when I change that fact, do they rework the answer, or do they give me the draft's answer again?"

## 4. The final frame alone

**Wide (1280×900): yes.** The table holds the whole story: the task, the struck line and the red note in the "not handed in" row, two identical beige drafts in "what the manager receives", and two rows of answers in which the first analyst is all beige except for a red "still" while the second is all red. Alongside sit the definition, both check questions and the source line. Read cold, it says: same output, but only one of them owned the reasoning, and you see it when the facts move.

**Narrow (1280×720 and 390): only partly.** The note that explains why the second analyst differs is gone from the last frame. You see which answers were the AI's words and which were the person's own, but not the reason (writing down what was new first). At 390 the source line is off-screen too.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The first analyst's answer to the changed fact is a straw man, and the second analyst's "rework" is the AI draft's own fallback.** Told that an older patent already covers reading through clothing, the first analyst says the key advance is "*still*" reading through clothing, so claim it broadly. That ignores the fact outright. No competent professional who had "read closely" would say it, and the red "still" (their only words of their own) makes them look stubborn rather than dependent. Worse, the draft they read closely already said "with the battery design as a fallback". So the second analyst's answer, "Then what's new is the battery design. Lead the claims with that", is the draft's idea, and a sceptical viewer can argue that the *second* analyst is the one falling back on the draft. The red/beige colouring rests on wording, not on whose idea it was. The essay line quoted below the figure ("revises under challenge rather than retreating to the artifact's language") would be better served if the first analyst *retreated to the draft's fallback language* without being able to say why the battery design is new, while the second could.
2. **Beat 7 already separates them, which undercuts "the second line separates them."** The manager asks "what do you claim, *and why*?" The first analyst repeats a claim with no reason. The second gives a reason ("No one has done that"). An attentive manager has caught the difference at the first check, yet the page text says "Both analysts in the figure explain the claims clearly without the draft. The second line separates them." Either give the first analyst a plausible why of their own (still taken from the draft), or soften the copy.
3. **At 1280×720 a desktop reader gets the stacked phone layout**, with no dot, no side-by-side comparison and one merged tile where the caption says "two identical drafts". The "not handed in" row (the second analyst's note) disappears from beat 7 to the end. In that layout the end state doesn't show *why* the two analysts differ. The beat list's "On screen" text still describes the wide version (a red dot, columns, "a thin line pairs each line"), so in the narrow layout it describes things that aren't there.
4. **Autoplay depends on the controls being fully in view.** On my first attempt I centred the picture in the window. At 1280×900 that left the control bar about 6 px below the fold, and at 390 it was off-screen. The figure sat on its poster frame with "Play" for 115 s and never started. A reader who stops scrolling with the picture centred will see a title card and may not realise it's an animation. At 390 the poster frame also shows the name card overlapping faint task text.
5. **Beat 5 draws the second draft at about 1.5× the first**, so "word for word" can't be checked by eye. The underline pairing reads as emphasis, not as a match.
6. **The end of beat 3 is dense.** Strike, key, lift and relabel all land in about 2 s, and the red "Cut one sentence from the AI's draft" label is tiny.
7. **The draft underline in beat 9 lasts about 2 s** and clears before the beat ends. It carries the evidence that the first analyst's words are the draft's, so it should hold, ideally into the final frame.
8. **There's a 5 s gap in beat 7** between the caption "both explain the claims clearly" and the first answer appearing.
9. **Small things:** the clock reads 0:08 at the start of the name card and then jumps to 0:00. The key says "An analyst's own work" in the wide layout and "Analyst's own work" in the narrow one.

Fairness to the AI: fine. Its draft is competent and even contains the right fallback, and the only weak line is mild puffery. Fairness to the people: the first analyst is shown doing real editing work (good), but see problem 1. There's no second confound between them: the dot reads both drafts at the same pace, the outputs are identical, and seniority isn't mentioned. The changed fact is aimed squarely at the premise the second analyst wrote down. That is the design, but it's worth knowing that the test favours whoever wrote that premise.

## 7. Business-edition questions

**Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work here?** Partly. What happens is universal: AI drafts the recommendation, you edit and agree, the manager asks you to defend it without the doc, then a fact changes. "The manager asks", "the call", "What would you recommend now?" and "what the manager receives" all land well for a manager. The setting is the barrier. A patent law firm is about as far from a nonprofit programme team or a company's ops analyst as you can get, and "claim it broadly", "as a fallback", "an older patent covers" and "lead the claims with" are legal moves the reader has to translate. The study justifies the setting, but the reader does the work of carrying it back to their own job.

**Would someone outside patent law follow it?** Yes, at the level that matters. The intro defines claims ("the sentences that set what an invention protects"), and the second analyst's note spells out novelty ("what's new... no one has done"), so "an older patent covers it" reads as "it's no longer new". I didn't need to understand claim scope or why a battery design is a sensible fallback to get the point. I did need it to judge whether the first analyst's answer was plausible, which is why problem 1 jumped out.

**Is anything unfair to the AI or either person, or is there a second difference between them?** See problems 1 and 2 and the fairness note under them. The main risk is that the first analyst looks obtuse rather than dependent, and the second analyst's "own" answer is the draft's idea. There's no hidden seniority or effort confound.

**Does the figure contradict what its cited research found?** Not as I read the page's own summary. The study found AI raised the quality of work delivered, and that on a later test without AI juniors split, with some better and some worse. The figure shows identical AI-assisted output followed by a split when the AI is taken away. Three gaps are worth stating. (a) The study's people were *patent lawyers*, and the figure calls them *analysts*, a role that doesn't draft claims at a law firm. (b) The split was among *juniors*, and the figure doesn't say either analyst is junior. (c) The study's test without AI was reviewing and correcting an application. The figure's test is a spoken what-if. The page says the study "measured scores and didn't record reasons" and presents the figure's mechanism as "its answer to the question the study leaves open". That is the right level of claim, if it's accurate (checked below).

**Is the research source line clear, without overclaiming?** Mostly. "Illustration based on Autor et al. (2026). The analysts, the invention and the older patent are constructed" in the intro, and "Illustration based on Autor et al., NBER (2026)" in the figure, are clear. One sentence overreaches slightly: "The two analysts in the figure illustrate the split the study found." The study found a spread of *scores* among juniors on a different task, not two people with identical output who diverge on a changed fact. "illustrate one way the split could arise" would be safer. The in-figure source line is easy to miss in the narrow layouts (under the controls, below the fold at 390).

**Military or classroom wording still on the page.** There's none in the example, captions or check. There are three military references, all honest attributions:
- Directly under the intro: "See this with: Workplace · PME" and "PME (professional military education): the example from *The Irreducible Officer*, the National War College essay." This is prominent: at 1280×720 it sits just above the figure while it plays. It glosses PME but says nothing about the edition the reader is on.
- The note under the reviewer's check: "It comes from *The Irreducible Officer*, an essay written for military education..."
- Go further: "The original essay, written for military education: *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II."

"Example task" is the only phrase with a faint classroom feel, and it's fine for a workplace.

## 8. Fact check against the paper (done after the read above)

I checked against the NBER PDF of w35720 ("September 2026, Revised October 2026", PDF created 7 October 2026, 48 pages) and the earlier version on the NBER page, `w35720.rev0.pdf` (3 September 2026).

**Supported as written:**
- The title and the authors Autor, Rodchenko, Martin, Iscenko, Strand, Pearl and Ferere. It is pre-registered (AEA RCT Registry AEARCTR-0015823), a three-month randomised trial, with "133 practicing patent lawyers at eleven U.S. intellectual property law firms" and a "custom AI patent drafting assistant" (abstract, word for word).
- "91 of whom completed the full protocol" (p. 1). The raters were "blinded patent attorneys at an independent law firm".
- Every abstract quote matches: 0.34 SD (p = 0.03) at 10 days and 0.38 SD (p = 0.01) at 90 days, "with larger gains among junior lawyers", 0.32 SD (p = 0.04), seniors 0.45 SD (p = 0.02), juniors "no average gain" and bifurcated, "springboard... cushion".
- "Our experiment does not answer why the effects appear positive for some lawyers and negative for others" is on p. 2, as cited.
- Juniors are "fewer than seven years" of experience (p. 23), and "The junior subgroup is smaller still" (limitations, p. 30).
- "The study was conducted by Google", "Funding for the direct costs of this experiment was provided by Google", and "All firms in the experiment have ongoing patent-drafting relationships with Google LLC" (front matter and p. 1).
- "October 7, 2026 version" matches the PDF's creation date. The paper itself only says "Revised October 2026", and the PDF was modified again on 9 October.

**Not supported, or overstated:**
1. **"The study measured scores and didn't record reasons."** This is too strong. The study also recorded time on task, post-task perception surveys, monthly AI-use surveys and self-reported on-the-job drafting. The current version says its Supplementary Materials (not in the public PDF) report "post-experiment interviews with treated participants and an LLM-based qualitative analysis of redlining submissions" (p. 29). The 3 September version printed that analysis in full (its §4.3 and §5.3). It found that treated seniors "paired their redlines with rationale-rich annotations naming the legal principles behind each edit", and it floated the idea that sustained AI use "may train users to articulate the 'why' and 'how' of an edit". Juniors often "diagnosed high-level problems without executing the corresponding redlines", but that was "equally common among unassisted control juniors". The authors flag all of this as exploratory, not preregistered. A safer line: *"The study's tested results are scores. Its look at reasons, a handful of interviews and an exploratory reading of the redlines, is qualitative, so the figure's account of the difference is an illustration, not a finding."* The September observation about seniors writing down the "why" actually fits the figure's mechanism, and it could be cited carefully as consistent with it.
2. **"The two analysts in the figure illustrate the split the study found."** The split was among *junior* lawyers' scores on an unassisted *redlining* task. The paper says the poor scores rose, the mediocre ones fell, the good (second-quintile) ones rose, and the top quintile didn't change. For seniors, AI-first practice went with *better* unassisted judgment. The figure never says the analysts are junior, so a business reader can take away "drafting with AI first erodes judgment", which the study found only as a split among juniors, not on average and not for experienced people. Fix: make both analysts early-career, or change the wording to "illustrate one way that split could arise".
3. **"After three months, every lawyer reviewed and corrected an existing patent application without AI."** The abstract does say "all subjects redlined", but only 91 of 133 submitted. Footnote 17 says Firm 3 finished before the task existed and Firms 4 and 7 registered no submissions. The no-AI rule couldn't be fully verified either: 15 of the 91 redlines were flagged for possible AI use, and excluding them pushes the full-sample 0.32 SD below conventional significance, though the senior effect holds at the 10 percent level (p. 3). "The lawyers who completed the study" is accurate. Whether to add the robustness caveat is an editorial call.
4. **The study's people were patent lawyers, but the figure calls them "analysts".** Analysts don't draft claims at a patent firm, so a reader who follows the citation finds a different role. The setup's "the analysts... are constructed" covers it legally, but "Illustration based on Autor et al." invites the reader to map one onto the other.

**The paper supports the figure on its core claim.** "Higher average scores reflect lower prevalence of weak work, not higher prevalence of excellent work". With AI, juniors' drafting scores "equaled or exceeded those of untreated seniors", yet their unassisted redlining was close to the control mean. And "this pattern is not evident in either subjects' AI-assisted performance or their own assessments of their work" (pp. 2 and 30). That is the figure's point that the delivered output can't tell you who has the judgment. The authors' own tentative mechanism is *time on task*: juniors worked faster and showed no unassisted gains (p. 30). The figure deliberately holds reading time equal (the dot reads both drafts at the same pace) and proposes a different mechanism, writing the reason down first. That isn't a contradiction, but the page should say its mechanism differs from the one the authors float.
