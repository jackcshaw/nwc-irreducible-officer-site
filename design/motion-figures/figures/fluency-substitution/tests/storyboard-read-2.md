Reader: a first-time educator who hasn't read the essay. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` (Intent removed). Nothing else was opened before this report was written. October 27, 2026.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1, the task (0–6 s).** A task card ("Example task: recommend whether to send forces to reassure a threatened ally") and a red dot labelled Student. Clear. I know who the student is and that they have to make a yes-or-no call.

**Beat 2, the AI drafts (6–14.7 s).** A grey paragraph writes itself under "AI'S DRAFT". I can read it: weigh escalation against trust, on balance trust is the bigger risk, send the forces with limits, then "Either choice carries real consequences." It reads like a decent answer. I'd agree with it. I don't get through all 33 words before the next caption arrives, but the dot's reading in beat 3 gives me time to finish.

**Beat 3, the student agrees (14.7–23.7 s).** The dot reads along each line, strikes the last sentence, the sentence fades, and the label becomes "First student's paper · drafted with AI". I understand this student read carefully and made one real edit. The edit is fast: a 0.6 s strike and a 0.4 s fade. Because the caption has already told me a line will be cut, I'm watching for it and catch it. Someone looking at the caption instead could miss which line went. My reaction: this student did nothing wrong that I can see. That seems deliberate.

**Beat 4, another student writes first (23.7–32.2 s).** The first paper moves left. A second red dot, "Another student", writes a red note: "Send them. The ally's trust matters most, and troops show we mean it." Clear. This student decided before using AI.

**Beat 5, the AI writes it up (32.2–43.2 s).** A grey draft appears and turns out to be word for word the first student's paper. The dot reads it at the same pace. Then the note slides behind the sheet and the label changes. Two moments here where I wasn't sure what I was seeing:
- **Same words.** When the AI produces exactly the paper it gave the student who wrote no note, my first thought was "the AI ignored the note." Is the figure telling me something about AI (it gives everyone the same thing), or is this a device so the two papers can be literally identical? I settled on device, but only after beat 6's caption said "identical".
- **The note disappears.** It slides behind the sheet in 0.6 s and no caption mentions it. I read it as the note being dropped, not as "now hidden where faculty can't see it." I only understood it was still there because the storyboard says so. On screen, I'd probably have missed it.

**Beat 6, what faculty receive (43.2–49.2 s).** Two identical grey papers under "What faculty receive", both tagged as drafted with AI, plus a key: grey is AI, red is a student's own words. Clear, and the strongest moment so far. Nothing on the page separates the two students.

**Beat 7, papers aside: why? (49.2–64.2 s).** The papers dim. Faculty ask "What do you recommend, and why?" The first student answers in grey with the paper's own sentence, and that sentence lights up on the paper. The second answers in red, in their own words. The caption says "One uses the paper's words." What I take from it: the first student is quoting the AI, and that's the tell. But both students give the same call for the same reason, and both answers are fluent. The only difference is which words they used, and the colour makes that difference loud. So I left this beat thinking the test is whether a student paraphrases. A faculty member in a real room can't see the colours. All they'd see is a word-for-word match with the paper.

Small stumble: "Send them. Their trust matters most." With no other antecedent on screen, "their" first reads as the forces' trust. The note said "the ally's".

**Beat 8, one fact changes (64.2–75.2 s).** "What if the ally doesn't want our troops on its soil?" The caption says the first student "repeats the paper's balancing line." It tells me what's coming before I see it, so for about 7 s I already know the outcome. "Balancing line" sent my eye to the "On balance…" sentence, which is the one beat 7 already used. The actual reply is the other sentence, "Any course must weigh escalation against the ally's trust." The highlight on the paper fixes that, but the paper is small and dimmed. Once I see it, I get it: asked a new question, the student falls back on the paper's hedge and makes no call.

**Beat 9, the second student reworks the call (75.2–83.2 s).** In red: "Then troops would cost their trust. I'd send aid and hold exercises instead." Clear and satisfying. Same reason, new decision. This is the beat that separates the two students. A PME reader might ask where those exercises happen if troops aren't welcome on the ally's soil.

**Beat 10, faculty check (83.2–89.9 s).** "Can the student independently explain the judgment in plain speech?" then "Then ask: What evidence would change your recommendation?" I hesitated here. In the picture, both students explained the judgment in plain speech when asked why: the first student's answer is a plain, clear sentence. What separated them was a changed fact, and that move isn't in the check or the follow-up. The follow-up is a different question from the one the picture just demonstrated.

## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same polished, AI-drafted paper with the same disclosure tag, and only questioning them without it, especially changing one fact, shows which of them actually made the decision and can rework it.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Suppose this one thing were different. What would you recommend now, and why?" Then listen for whether the student reasons from their own reason to a new call, or repeats the paper.

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. The 2 × 3 table reads: same paper, same first answer, different answer when a fact changes. The key tells me grey is AI wording and red is the student's own. On its own it says that the paper and the disclosure tag can't show who decided, and a changed-fact question can.

What it loses:
- **The note.** Nothing in the still shows that the second student decided first, so it gives no hint why that student could adapt. It shows how to detect the difference, not where the difference came from.
- **The careful first student.** Without beat 3's close read and cut, the first student just looks like a parrot. The animation works hard to make them sincere, and the still drops that.
- **The demonstrated move.** The caption band asks for "plain speech" and "what evidence would change your recommendation". The picture above shows a changed-fact question. "Change one fact" isn't written anywhere in the still.
- **Legibility.** The two papers are at 66% scale (about 13 px at 960 wide) and dimmed to 70%. By my estimate, grey text on the grey band drops from about 5:1 to under 3:1 contrast. This still is also the reduced-motion and print view.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Beat 7 teaches a paraphrase test (confusion, fairness).** The colours and the caption "One uses the paper's words" make wording provenance the first visible difference between the students. Faculty can't see colours. A student who paraphrases the AI passes. A student who sincerely agrees with clear wording and repeats it gets marked. The picture says the first question already separates them, while the rest of the figure implies only the changed fact does. I'd expect some teachers to leave with "check whether they use the AI's words," which is weaker than the figure's real point.
2. **The faculty check doesn't match the picture (caption vs picture).** The check asks whether the student can explain the judgment in plain speech, and at Q1 both students did. The follow-up asks what evidence would change the recommendation, but the picture shows faculty changing a fact themselves. The move the figure demonstrates ("set the paper aside, ask why, then change one fact") appears only in the page copy, not on the final frame or in beat 10.
3. **The hidden note is fast and uncaptioned (too fast, ambiguous visual).** The 0.6 s slide behind the sheet carries an important idea: the only difference is now out of sight. The caption doesn't name it, and the final frame doesn't show it. Viewers may think the note was thrown away. Combined with the AI producing a word-for-word identical paragraph from a different input, a viewer could read it as "the AI ignored the student's note."
4. **The essay quote doesn't match the paragraph shown (caption vs picture).** The "From the essay" passage ends: "A paragraph that balances every consideration may never identify which risk actually matters most." The figure's paragraph does name the risk ("losing that trust is the greater risk") and makes a call. The hedge the quote describes shows up in the first student's spoken reply in beat 8, not in the paper. A teacher reading the quote under the figure may ask what's wrong with the paper.
5. **Is the first student a straw man? (fairness to students).** Someone who read closely and agreed that trust is the greater risk could plausibly reason "then don't send troops." The figure presents the retreat to the paper's language as what happens, without signalling that this is the risk the essay names, not a typical result. Experienced teachers may push back. Relatedly, the second student's paper keeps AI-added content they never wrote or defend ("with clear limits to contain escalation"). The figure treats owning the call as owning the whole paper.
6. **"Balancing line" points at the wrong sentence (ambiguous caption).** The word "balance" appears in the "On balance" sentence, which beat 7 already used. The beat 8 reply is the "weigh escalation" sentence.
7. **Captions announce outcomes (minor).** Beats 7, 8 and 9 tell me what a student will say before they say it. The rule that the caption comes first keeps reading load low, but it takes away the viewer's chance to judge the answer themselves, which is the skill the figure is teaching.
8. **Small wording and grammar issues (minor).**
   - "Send them. Their trust matters most": "their" reads as the forces.
   - "Another student" vs "second student".
   - "Hold exercises" may draw a PME objection if troops aren't welcome on the ally's soil.
   - Red marks the dots, name labels, the note's label and the strike, but the key defines red only as "a student's own words".
9. **Legibility and narrow screens (accessibility, estimate only).**
   - The dimmed 66% papers in the final frame look below 3:1 contrast.
   - At 390 px, the stage scales to about 0.37, so nearly all picture text falls under the 11 px cutoff and is hidden. The storyboard doesn't say what the stage shows then (empty sheets and bands?). The beat list carries the content, which is acceptable for a desktop-first figure, but the spec should say what remains on the stage.
10. **Sourcing to confirm (factual).** The figure shows no numbers. The quotes I'd check:
    - Section II: "tone of analytic maturity", "balanced paragraphs, caveats in the right places…"
    - Section VIII: "revises under challenge rather than retreating to the artifact's language"
    - Section I: disclosure doesn't show whose judgment the work holds
    - Section IX: the oral defense asks what evidence would change the recommendation
    - Chow et al. (2026): "epistemic confinement"

Not a problem: the AI is treated fairly. Its draft is good, and the figure places the failure in the student's ownership, not the tool's quality. Overall pacing (about 90 s, generous holds, at least 1.5 s every beat) felt unhurried apart from the two fast moves noted above (beat 3's cut and beat 5's hidden note).
