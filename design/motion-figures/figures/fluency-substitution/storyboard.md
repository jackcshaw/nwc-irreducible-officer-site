# Fluency substitution: storyboard

Motion figure 2 of 8 · slug `fluency-substitution` · storyboard round 2, revised after storyboard cold read 1 · October 27, 2026

Starting point: `concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.2, "Strip the fluency" (8 s, not built). This version keeps its mechanism: take the fluency away and see what judgment is left. Faculty do the taking away, by setting the paper aside, asking why, and then changing one fact. Both students use an AI and hand in the same polished paper, so the difference can only show off the page. Frame capture's shared runtime, conventions and test lessons apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** A student can read an AI's polished recommendation closely, agree with it, and hand in a paper identical to one from a student who made the same call first and had the AI write it up. On the page the two can't be told apart. Off the page they can. With the paper set aside, the first student explains in the paper's own sentences. When one fact changes, the first student falls back on the paper's balancing line, while the second keeps their reason and changes the call in plain words.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "Both students used an AI and handed in the same polished paper: send the forces, because losing the ally's trust is the greater risk. One read the AI's draft carefully, cut a weak line and agreed with the rest. The other wrote the judgment down first in plain words and had the AI write it up. With the papers put away, both could say why, but one said it in the paper's sentence. Then the question changed: what if the ally doesn't want our troops? The first student answered 'any course must weigh escalation against the ally's trust'. The second said troops would then cost the trust they were after, so send aid and hold exercises instead. So I'd ask whether the student can explain the judgment in plain speech, and then change a fact or ask what evidence would change the recommendation."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from the site's Core Failure Modes table): *Can the student independently explain the judgment in plain speech?* The final frame adds, smaller, a follow-up from Section IX's oral defense: *What evidence would change your recommendation?*

**Essay passage** (Section II, "What the Problem Looks Like"):

> **Fluency substitution** is easier to miss. AI produces the tone of analytic maturity (balanced paragraphs, caveats in the right places, the measured voice of a considered judgment) and the student mistakes well-ordered language for well-owned reasoning. Researchers studying AI's effects on student cognition have introduced the concept of epistemic confinement to describe this condition: an illusion of competence while operating entirely within AI-constructed analytical boundaries, where the student believes they are thinking independently while the frame doing the work was never theirs ([Chow et al., 2026](https://ojs.aut.ac.nz/pjtel/article/view/246)). In strategy, fluency substitutes for deciding. A paragraph that balances every consideration may never identify which risk actually matters most.

Related:
- Section I: "Disclosure tells faculty that a student used AI. It does not tell them whether the judgment in the work is theirs." Both papers carry a "drafted with AI" tag.
- Section I: "Or the machine may have handed her a frame she never examined, and she dressed it in the vocabulary of someone who had."
- Section VIII, frame evidence: "The student who answers those questions under questioning, and revises under challenge rather than retreating to the artifact's language, has owned the frame. The one who cannot has not." (beats 7–9)
- Section IX, step five (oral defense): faculty ask "what evidence would change the recommendation." (beat 10 follow-up; beat 8 asks a concrete version)

**Misreadings to design against.**

1. *Polished writing is the problem.* Both papers are the same polished paragraph.
2. *Using AI is the failure.* Both papers are drafted with AI and say so.
3. *The first student was lazy or copied.* The student reads at the same pace as the second student, makes a real editorial cut, and answers fluently and sincerely. There are no synonym swaps and no stammer.
4. *Faculty only need to read more carefully.* The papers are word-for-word identical.
5. *Disclosure solves it.* Both papers disclose AI use.
6. *The check rewards articulate students.* The first student is the more fluent speaker. What separates them is what happens when a fact changes.
7. *Decisiveness is judgment.* Both students make the same call. The second student's reason is what carries over to the changed case.
8. *Changing your mind is the test.* The second student keeps the reason and changes the call because the reason now points elsewhere. The caption says so.
9. *The AI wrote a bad paragraph.* It reaches a defensible call with a sensible caveat.
10. *The grey answer means the student is reading off the paper.* The papers are dimmed and set aside, both students speak at the same pace, and the caption says the first student "uses the paper's words".
11. *This is frame capture again.* Both students reach the same call inside the same frame. What differs is whether the reasoning is theirs.

**Changes after storyboard cold read 1** (`tests/storyboard-read-1.md` and the orchestrator's notes):
- *The paper gave the difference away.* Both papers now make the same call, name the same risk as decisive, and are word-for-word identical. The "Read it with us" beat and the "matters most" slots are gone, because the page no longer shows the difference.
- *The off-page test.* Faculty ask why without the paper, then change one fact. The second question separates the students, in Section VIII's terms.
- *Fairness to the first student.* The reading pace matches the second student's, there are no synonym swaps or stammer, and the one edit cuts a weak sentence. Answers are fluent.
- *Same treatment for both AI uses.* Each student's AI draft is grey, each student reads it closely, and each kept paper is labelled "drafted with AI".
- *No wipe.* The AI's words stay grey on both papers to the end, so nothing reads as hiding authorship.
- *AI in the final frame.* Grey paper text, the tags, the first student's grey answers and a small colour key.
- *Smaller fixes.* "Four risks" is gone. The second student's AI draft holds a moment before it is read, and its caption names that student, so "they" isn't ambiguous.

**Considered and left out.**
- *Asking "What would change your recommendation?" on stage instead of a concrete fact.* The second student would have to supply both the condition and the revision, about 25 words. It stays as the follow-up line under the check.
- *Revealing the second student's note at the end,* as frame capture revealed what each paper rested on. The red answers already carry it, and the still stays less crowded.
- *An early beat on the essay's balance line.* The papers now decide, so the balance line appears where the first student falls back on it (beat 8).

**Why this scenario.** The lab's fluency case is a paragraph about the essay itself, which a viewer can't judge without reading the essay. The figure builds a strategy recommendation in the essay's own terms ("which risk actually matters most") and keeps frame capture's PME setting with a different task, so the two figures don't read as the same student. The changed fact tests the link between the reason (the ally's trust) and the course (troops), which only the student who made that link can rework. The task, the paragraph, the note and every answer are constructed, and the task card says "Example task".

**Conventions this figure would add** (to `CONVENTIONS.md` only after the cold reads):
- *A speech panel is what a person says without the paper.* Its words keep their origin colours: a grey band for the AI's sentences repeated, red for the person's own words.
- *A source brightens when someone repeats it.* The repeated sentence on the set-aside paper returns to full strength while it is said, then dims again.
- *Set aside is a dim.* A product dims to 70% when faculty ask without it.
- *A disclosure tag* ("DRAFTED WITH AI", grey mono) stays on a product made with AI through the final frame.
- *Drain only when the point is invisibility.* Frame capture drained the AI's grey to show its origin vanishing from the product. Here the AI's words stay grey because origin isn't hidden.
- *A colour key in the final frame* when the still must carry the colour code alone.
- *Real words when the words are the point.* Frame capture drew writing as bars; this figure sets real sentences because fluency is in the wording.

## 2. Scenario

A war-college student must recommend whether to send forces to reassure a threatened ally. The student asks an AI for a draft. It writes a polished paragraph that weighs escalation against the ally's trust and concludes, on balance, that the forces should go, with clear limits. The student reads it closely, cuts one weak sentence and agrees with the rest. A second student first writes a plain note, "Send them. The ally's trust matters most, and troops show we mean it.", then has an AI write it up and reads that draft just as closely. The two papers are identical, and both are tagged as drafted with AI. With the papers set aside, faculty ask each student why. Both answer, the first in the paper's words. Then faculty ask what if the ally doesn't want troops on its soil. The first student repeats the paper's balancing line. The second keeps the reason and changes the call: send aid and hold exercises instead.

*Sources.* The task, the paragraph, the note and every answer are constructed for this figure, and the task card says "Example task". *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, describes the AI's "balanced paragraphs, caveats in the right places, the measured voice of a considered judgment". Section VIII names the student who "revises under challenge rather than retreating to the artifact's language". Section I notes that disclosure doesn't show whose judgment the work holds. The follow-up question is from the oral defense in Section IX. The figure shows no numbers.

To test it, set the paper aside, ask why, then change one fact. Section VIII of the essay looks for the student who "revises under challenge rather than retreating to the artifact's language". In the oral defense in Section IX, faculty ask what evidence would change the recommendation.

**From the essay** (*The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, What the Problem Looks Like), with its link to Chow et al. (2026):

> Fluency substitution is easier to miss. AI produces the tone of analytic maturity (balanced paragraphs, caveats in the right places, the measured voice of a considered judgment) and the student mistakes well-ordered language for well-owned reasoning. Researchers studying AI's effects on student cognition have introduced the concept of epistemic confinement to describe this condition: an illusion of competence while operating entirely within AI-constructed analytical boundaries, where the student believes they are thinking independently while the frame doing the work was never theirs ([Chow et al., 2026](https://ojs.aut.ac.nz/pjtel/article/view/246)). In strategy, fluency substitutes for deciding. A paragraph that balances every consideration may never identify which risk actually matters most.
