What a good answer says

A good answer says where the check went and why. This is the figure's reasoning from the study's results; the study itself didn't test checking, a time budget, or what the work was for. Task B's deciding details were in the interviews, and the spreadsheet only looked complete. Graders rated the AI users' recommendations higher whether or not they were right, so a read-through wouldn't show a miss. That's where the one check belongs, and it starts from the interviews. Task A is the kind of work the study found inside the frontier, and the figure reads it as having no hidden facts: a weak segmentation shows when read. As a first draft it can go without a check. When the segmentation goes straight into the launch plan, it is still inside the frontier, so the call is still to rely on it, but now someone has to read it carefully before it's built. Moving the one check would leave Task B, the risky task, to a read-through; asking for a week to check both is defensible, and it slips the launch a week. Changing one condition and asking again shows whether the student has a reason for the call.

Evidence

Dell'Acqua et al., Navigating the Jagged Technological Frontier, HBS working paper 24-013 (2023). In the field experiment, 758 BCG consultants were split between two sets of tasks and randomly assigned to work without AI, with GPT-4, or with GPT-4 plus a prompt-engineering overview. On the tasks inside the frontier, consultants with AI finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster and at more than 40% higher quality. On the task chosen to fall outside it, consultants without AI were right about 84.5% of the time, with GPT-4 alone 70.6%, and with GPT-4 plus the overview 60%: in the working paper's words, with the two AI groups pooled, "19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions." Graders still rated the AI users' recommendations higher in quality "regardless of the correctness of their answer."

About the tasks

The tasks inside the frontier were 18 product-development questions for a fictional footwear company. Consultants generated ideas for a new shoe, segmented the market by users, listed the steps to launch it, and wrote a press release and a persuasive memo. On the task built to fall outside the frontier, consultants told a fictional clothing company's CEO which of its brands to invest in, working from a spreadsheet of financial data and interviews with company insiders. The spreadsheet was designed to look complete, but the deciding details were in the interviews, and the authors designed the task so that GPT-4 would get it wrong. In the study each consultant worked one set of tasks; the choice of where to spend one check, and the launch-plan condition, are this figure's. The task wording on screen is paraphrased. The frontier moves as models change; this is where it sat in 2023, with GPT-4.

From the essay

The distinction between trust and reliance matters here. Trust is a subjective disposition, a feeling of confidence that a system will perform well. Reliance is the observable act of accepting its output and acting on it. Appropriate reliance is harder and more discriminating: accepting support when it is warranted, refusing or verifying when it is not, and being able to give an account of the difference (Raees & Papangelis, 2026).

The Irreducible Officer, Section V, Appropriate Reliance as a Teachable Competency

The educational response is pattern recognition: learning to identify what kind of task is in front of you, where models tend to be strong, where they tend to fail, and what independent checks are required before the output governs the work.
