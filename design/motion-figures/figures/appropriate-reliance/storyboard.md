Starting point: `concepts-v0/reliance.html`, "Make the call" (`concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.8, built). This version keeps its mechanism: the reader makes a reliance call on two look-alike tasks from the Dell'Acqua et al. study before anything shows which side of the frontier each sits on, and the study's results then split the two. The v0 version cost nothing to check, so "always check" could never lose. Here the reader has time to check only one task, so the call is where to spend that check. The figure runs at teaching pace on the shared runtime, with the reader's choice gating the timeline. It shows warranted acceptance as a result, and it adds a second call after one condition changes, so "what would have changed the call" is something the reader watches happen. The v0 quality-prediction step and its "+17.9%" and "+25.1% quality" figures are cut; they aren't in the source spine. The shared runtime and `CONVENTIONS.md` apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** Appropriate reliance means putting your checks where the task needs them, and being able to say why. Checking costs time, and two tasks can look alike and get equally finished AI answers, so the answer can't tell you where to check. What the task is and what a miss would cost can. Sometimes the right call is to rely without a check, because the task is the kind the AI does well and a miss would be cheap and visible, and that frees the check for the task that needs it. Change one condition and the same task can need a check of its own. Faculty ask for that account: the evidence for relying, and what would have changed the call.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "I had time to check only one of two tasks from a 2023 study of 758 consultants using GPT-4: segmenting a footwear market and picking a segment, or picking a clothing brand from a spreadsheet and insider interviews. Both AI answers looked finished, and both were going into a first draft for my team. The first task sat inside the jagged frontier: with AI, consultants finished more tasks, faster, at higher quality. The second was chosen to fall outside it: with AI they were right less often, and graders still rated their work higher, so reading the answer wouldn't catch a wrong pick. The reason was in the task. The brand pick depended on interview details that cut against the spreadsheet, while the segmentation is idea work you can judge by reading. So the evidence suggests relying on the first and spending the check on the second, by working it from the interviews myself before comparing with the AI's answer. Checking the first would have left the second unchecked. Then the company was going to build the segment I picked. Same task, same side of the frontier, but a miss would get built, so it needed a check too, and I'd have to make time for it. That's what I'd ask a student: what evidence justified relying here, and what would have changed the call? Then I'd change one condition and ask again."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from `PLAN.md` and the site's Core Failure Modes table): *What evidence justified relying on the system here, and what would have changed the call?* The final frame adds, directly under it and at the same size, the move the figure makes: *Change one condition and ask for the call again.*

**Essay passages** (Section V, "Appropriate Reliance as a Teachable Competency"):

> The distinction between trust and reliance matters here. Trust is a subjective disposition, a feeling of confidence that a system will perform well. Reliance is the observable act of accepting its output and acting on it. Appropriate reliance is harder and more discriminating: accepting support when it is warranted, refusing or verifying when it is not, and being able to give an account of the difference ([Raees & Papangelis, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.23896)). A student who trusts AI generally has learned almost nothing transferable. One who has developed a disciplined account of when and why to rely, across task types, risk levels, and domains, has developed something real.

> AI improved performance on some tasks and degraded it on others, and the boundary was not obvious in advance. Tasks that looked similar from the outside differed in whether AI helped or hurt, a finding Dell'Acqua et al. describe as the jagged technological frontier ... The educational response is pattern recognition: learning to identify what kind of task is in front of you, where models tend to be strong, where they tend to fail, and what independent checks are required before the output governs the work.

> Users who can only inspect an AI answer are still downstream of the frame. Users who can change the task, revise the context, reset the criteria, and design the checks are exercising the judgment the assignment is meant to build.

**Misreadings to design against.**

1. *AI is bad at judgment work, so don't use it.* The first suggestion is to rely on Task A, with the study's inside-the-frontier gains beside it. Task B's suggestion ends "then compare", so the AI's answer is still used. In the changed condition the AI's answer to Task A stays in use, with a check.
2. *Check everything to be safe.* The reader has time for one check, so checking Task A leaves Task B unchecked, and the note under Task A says so. Checking has a cost, so warranted acceptance has a payoff.
3. *A check means reading the AI's answer again.* Beat 3 defines a check on screen: work it yourself, then compare. On Task B that starts from the interviews, before the AI's answer can anchor you.
4. *Checking the AI's answer would have fixed Task B.* The AI groups had the same sources and still scored 60–70%. The suggestion for Task B is to work it from the interviews yourself, which comes closest to the group without AI, and it is labelled as what the evidence suggests, not as a finding.
5. *You can tell from the answer.* The two answers are drawn identically and get their "READY" tags at the same moment, and graders rated the AI users' work higher whether or not it was right.
6. *The frontier is about the subject (shoes or clothes), or fixed forever.* Both tasks are retail picks whose second lines begin "pick the …". The reason given is what each answer rested on, which applies to any task. "2023" and "GPT-4" are on screen from beat 1, and the panel says Task B was chosen to fall outside.
7. *The frontier alone decides.* Beat 10: same task, same side of the frontier, and the call flips.
8. *A first draft is always safe to rely on.* Task B carries the same first-draft condition and still needs the check, because a read-through wouldn't catch a wrong pick.
9. *It's a quiz with an answer key.* Nothing before a call shows a side, a reason or a suggestion. That covers the poster, the first frame, the reduced-motion view, print, the hidden summary and the beat list. The suggestions are the same for every reader and come with their reasons. There is no score.
10. *Warranted means the AI will be right.* Relying on Task A accepts an occasional weak pick, because it is cheap and the team will see it. The figure says what the evidence suggests and doesn't say the AI's answer was right.

**Conventions this figure would add** (to `CONVENTIONS.md` in Step B):
- *A gate.* The timeline pauses at a fixed moment until the reader makes a call or plays on without one. The figure is `render(t, calls)`: captions never branch, and only the reader's red marks and the live-region text depend on the calls. The no-call path is also the video path.
- *Question before answer.* On an interactive figure, every view that doesn't play shows the question first: the poster, the first frame, reduced motion, print, the hidden summary and the beat list. The answer is one deliberate step away.
- *A budgeted call.* When checking is the choice, the reader gets a fixed amount of it ("TIME FOR ONE CHECK"), so a call has a cost. What the reader didn't check is labelled as reliance, in red, because they decided it.
- *"What the evidence suggests."* Recommended calls are filled navy tags under a key with that name. They are reasoning from the evidence and are labelled that way; the same tags show for every reader.
- *A changed condition adds a row,* so the final frame shows both conditions and both calls.
- *"Start over"* replaces "Restart" on an interactive figure and clears the calls.

**Why this scenario.** The study's own tasks make the reveal evidence rather than the author's opinion: what happened to 758 consultants tells the reader how their call would have fared. Figure 4 reserved these tasks for this figure. The two tasks share a sentence shape and an ending ("pick the …"). They are the smallest set that separates the frontier from the stakes and gives checking a cost.

- *A "check both" option in round 1.* It would undo the budget. Making time for a second check appears in round 2, where the condition justifies it.
- *v0's quality prediction* ("Whose recommendations did graders score higher?"). It quizzes the reader on a result rather than a reliance call. The finding appears as a beat.

## 2. Scenario

You have time to check one of two tasks from Dell'Acqua et al.'s 2023 field experiment with 758 BCG consultants using GPT-4. Task A: segment the footwear market by user and pick the segment that matters most. Task B: use a spreadsheet and insider interviews to pick the brand a clothing company should back. An AI gives both answers in the same finished form, and both will go into a first draft for your team. A check means working the task yourself, then comparing with the AI's answer. You choose which task gets your one check, and you rely on the AI for the other. Then the frontier is drawn between the tasks, the study's results split them, and the figure shows what each answer rested on and what the evidence suggests. Then one condition changes: the company will build the segment you pick. You decide whether to rely on Task A now, and the figure shows the call that the evidence and the new condition suggest. The figure ends on the faculty check.

*Sources.* The tasks are paraphrased from the working paper's task descriptions; their wording on screen is the figure's. The study figures are from the source spine's Jagged Frontier entry (Dell'Acqua et al., 2023, HBS working paper 24-013): inside the frontier, consultants with AI finished 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster and at more than 40% higher quality; on a task chosen to fall outside it, consultants without AI were right about 84.5% of the time and the two AI groups 60% and 70%, and graders still rated the AI users' recommendations higher in quality "regardless of the correctness of their answer". That the right answer on Task B depended on interview details that cut against the spreadsheet is from the working paper's task design, not the spine. The AI's answers are drawn as lines, not text. The conditions (a first draft for your team, time for one check, the company will build what you pick) are added for the exercise; the study didn't vary them. *The Irreducible Officer*, Section V, defines trust, reliance and appropriate reliance, and describes the jagged frontier and pattern recognition across task types.

**Interaction.**
- *Gate 1 control.* One radio group, legend "Your one check goes to", with options "Task A" and "Task B" placed over each card's "Check this one" pill. Arrow keys move between them. Choosing one puts the red dot in that pill and "YOU RELY ON IT" beside the other card's pill (the dot grows in over 0.26 s, removed under reduced motion). The reader can change it until they press Show what happened.
- *Gate 2 control.* One radio group on card A's second row, legend "If the company builds what you pick: Task A", options "Rely on it" and "Check it".
- *Buttons.* At a gate, the caption band shows **Show what happened** (enabled once the open question has a call) and **Play without choosing**. Focus is visible on everything.
- *Resuming.* Show what happened jumps the playhead to 1.5 s before the gate beat ends (only a hold remains, so nothing visibly skips) and plays. Play without choosing, or the main Play button at a gate, plays on with no call for that gate. Play without choosing at gate 1 also passes gate 2.
- *Scrubbing.* The scrubber can move past a gate at any time; a gate without a call renders as no call. Moving back to a gate opens its control again with any earlier call in place.
- *Start over.* Clears every call and plays from 0.
- *Screen readers.* The SVG and the caption band are `aria-hidden`. A polite live region announces each gate ("You have time to check one of the two tasks.
