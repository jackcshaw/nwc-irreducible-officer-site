# Appropriate reliance: storyboard cold read, round 2

Read October 28, 2026, from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only. I didn't open `storyboard.md`, `storyboard-read-1.md`, any other figure's folder, the plan, the brief or the essay before writing sections 1–7. Section 8, the fact check, was done afterwards against the source spine.

**Reading conditions, said plainly.** A storyboard can't hide its answers the way the figure can. The Scenario paragraph says "the study's results split them", the Sources paragraph says Task B depended on interview details, and the cast table and suggestions table give every tag before Beat 1. I made my gate 1 call after reading the two task lines in the Scenario, before the Sources paragraph. My gate 2 call is contaminated, because I'd already seen the suggestions table. I also already knew the BCG jagged-frontier study, so the sides didn't surprise me the way they would surprise a true first-timer. I've written what I chose, and separately what I think a first-time educator would do.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · Two tasks (0–16.5 s).** Two cards come in, one after the other. Task A: segment the footwear market by user and pick the segment that matters most. Task B: use a spreadsheet and insider interviews to pick the brand a clothing company should back. The source line at the top says 2023, 758 BCG consultants, GPT-4. Making both second lines start with "pick the …" works: they look like siblings. The one visible difference is that Task B names two sources. The caption ("…a field experiment with 758 consultants") repeats the source line, so I'd be reading the same fact twice while I'm trying to read the tasks.

**Beat 2 · The AI answers both (16.5–23.5 s).** The same grey band, the same two grey lines and the same READY pill land on both cards at the same moment. It's clear and quick. Drawing the answers as lines instead of text is the right call: it removes the urge to proofread them, so "they look equally finished" is something I see rather than something I'm told.

**Beat 3 · One check, gate 1 (23.5–36.5 s).** Each card gets "IF IT'S A FIRST DRAFT FOR YOUR TEAM", and the bottom band says "TIME FOR ONE CHECK · A CHECK: WORK IT YOURSELF, THEN COMPARE". Then a "Check this one" pill appears on each card, with a red "YOUR CALL". The rules are clear before I'm asked. Defining a check as "work it yourself, then compare" matters, because it makes a check expensive and makes the budget believable.

- **My call: Task B.** It asks me to reconcile two kinds of material, numbers and interviews. A fluent synthesis can paper over a conflict between them, and I wouldn't see that from reading the answer. Task A is open-ended segmentation: a weak segmentation shows on its face, and there's no hidden fact in it to miss. (I also knew the study, which pointed the same way.)
- **What a first-time educator might do.** It's a fair gate, and I think a fair share of teachers would choose A. "Pick the segment that matters most" reads like the higher-judgment task, and "use a spreadsheet" sounds like the data work people expect AI to do well. Others will choose B because "AI is bad at numbers". That's the right answer for the wrong reason, and Beat 7 corrects it, which is good.

**Beat 4 · The jagged frontier (36.5–46 s).** An uneven navy line draws down the gap between the cards, labelled INSIDE over A and OUTSIDE over B. My red dot stays in place, so I can see my call against the sides right away. Feedback arrives seven seconds after I commit, which is good. The caption says "where AI stops being reliable", which generalises from one model in one year (see problem 8).

**Beat 5 · Inside the frontier (46–56 s).** Card A's slot gets "12.2% more tasks · 25.1% faster · 40%+ higher quality", under a title that correctly says these are averages across the inside tasks. It's clear, but it's the least necessary beat: Beat 4 has already told me the side, and these numbers confirm it without surprising me.

**Beat 6 · Outside the frontier (56–71 s).** The scene dims and a panel rises: Without AI 84.5%; With AI, two groups, 60% and 70%. A bracket under the two shorter bars reads "Rated higher in quality, right or wrong." Then the panel shrinks into card B. This is the surprise of the figure, and the bracket on the shorter bars is the best single image in it. "Two groups" is unexplained on screen. I wondered what separated them, and only the page copy answers that (one group also got a prompting overview). A short gloss under the label would stop the question.

**Beat 7 · What each answer rested on (71–82.5 s).** "Segment the footwear market by user" gets an underline and the tag "IDEA WORK · JUDGE IT BY READING". "Insider interviews" gets an underline and "THE RIGHT ANSWER DEPENDED ON INTERVIEW DETAILS / THAT CUT AGAINST THE SPREADSHEET". This matched my reason, so it felt like confirmation. For the reader who chose A because "picking what matters most is judgment", this is where the figure is weakest (problem 3). The figure made Task A look like a pick, and now it calls Task A idea work. The real difference is that Task B had a hidden fact a fluent answer could miss and Task A had none. The tags suggest that but don't say it.

**Beat 8 · What the evidence suggests (82.5–94.5 s).** Navy tags appear: Task A "RELY ON IT", with "A CHECK HERE LEAVES TASK B UNCHECKED"; Task B "WORK IT FROM THE INTERVIEWS, THEN COMPARE". My red marks matched both. This beat taught me two things. First, reliance is an allocation: relying on A is what pays for the check on B, and the cost note makes that concrete without scolding anyone. Second, the check on B starts from the interviews, before the AI's answer can anchor you. That's a practical procedure I could hand a student. Juxtaposing red and navy, with no ticks or crosses, is the right tone.

**Beat 9 · One thing changes, gate 2 (94.5–104.5 s).** A second row appears on card A only: "IF THE COMPANY BUILDS WHAT YOU PICK", with "Rely on it" and "Check it". The caption asks: "Now the company will build the segment you pick. Would you rely on Task A?"

- **My call: Check it**, though with the suggestions table already read I can't claim it was clean. My real answer was "check it, and ask for more time so Task B keeps its check". The two buttons can't say that. "Check it" could mean moving my one check off Task B, which Beat 8 just told me was the wrong trade, or it could mean adding a second check, which the rules said I didn't have time for.
- **What a first-time educator would do.** Most will press "Check it". The question's form ("Now one thing changes. Would you still…?") signals a flip, and checking is the safe, virtuous-sounding button. A careful reader who stays inside the stated rules ("time for one check; B needs it more") will press "Rely on it", and that reader will be shown as wrong.

**Beat 10 · The call flips (104.5–114.5 s).** The frontier pulses, INSIDE brightens, and a navy "CHECK IT · MAKE TIME FOR A SECOND CHECK" appears. The caption: "Same task, same side of the frontier. Now a miss gets built, so check it." The idea, that risk changes the call while the task stays the same, is right and worth teaching. But the answer resolves the dilemma by dropping the budget that defined gate 1, and "TIME FOR ONE CHECK" is still printed along the bottom of the stage (problem 1). The pulse adds nothing the caption doesn't already say.

**Beat 11 · The faculty check (114.5–124.5 s).** "What evidence justified relying on the system here, and what would have changed the call?", and then, at the same size, "Change one condition and ask for the call again." It's a good ending, because the figure has just modelled that move. The teaching sequence works: the teacher asks for the warrant (Beats 5–7), then asks what would flip it (Beats 9–10).

No caption is scheduled to change before it can be read. The pacing rule (3 words a second, with the change waiting until the caption has been read once) shows in every beat.

## 2. The point

Where a scarce check should go depends on whether reading the AI's answer could reveal a miss, not on how finished the answer looks; and when the cost of a miss goes up, the call changes even though the task and the tool haven't.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before you saw any results, what in the task itself, not the answer, told you where your one check belonged? And what would have to change before you'd move that check, or ask for time for a second one?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It mostly carries the idea. The frontier between the cards, the inside numbers under A, the bars and the bracket under B, the two pattern tags, the three navy suggestions with their key, and the faculty check together tell the whole story to someone who never saw the motion. The bracket on the shorter bars carries the most weight and reads well on its own.

Two things weaken it as a still:

- **Contradiction.** "TIME FOR ONE CHECK" sits along the bottom while "MAKE TIME FOR A SECOND CHECK" sits on card A. A reader of the printed page sees the rule and its breach side by side, with no explanation.
- **Shared vocabulary.** Card A stacks the "Check this one" pill, a navy "RELY ON IT" tag, the cost note, a "Rely on it" pill, a "Check it" pill and a navy "CHECK IT · …" tag: five imperative labels in about 160 units of height, and two of them say "rely". Fill style is the only thing that separates an option from a verdict. In the no-call still, with no red marks to anchor it, I had to reread card A to tell which labels were buttons and which were answers.

**Does the no-choice version hold the answer back?** In the picture, yes.

- **Poster, social card and first frame:** Beat 3 settled, with the question showing and no sides.
- **Reduced motion:** opens on the live question. Beats 4–9 come out only after the first reveal, and that reveal arrives together with the second question, so gate 2 is still asked before it's answered.
- **Print:** puts the question on page 1 and the answer on page 2. That's as good as print gets.
- **Hidden summary and live region:** both are split at the gate.
- **The gap is the page around the figure.** The "A good answer says…" paragraph, the Evidence block and "About the tasks" all name Task A as inside and Task B as outside. The storyboard doesn't say whether they're gated, collapsed, or printed with page 1. A teacher who scrolls before choosing, or prints, gets the answer first (problem 2).
- **Preview video:** it holds each question for about 6–7 s. That's enough to read the question but short for a real prediction unless the viewer pauses.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Gate 2 rewards reflexive checking, and it does that by dropping the rule from gate 1.** "Check it" is the safe button, and the question's framing signals the flip, so a reader who doesn't think scores 2 for 2. The pattern is "check the one that looks harder; check whenever asked". The reader who honoured "TIME FOR ONE CHECK" and relied on A to keep B's check is shown as wrong, and the answer is "make time", which is not a choice they were offered. "Check it" is also ambiguous: a reader who meant "move my check off B", the trade Beat 8 just warned against, is marked as matching. That cuts against the page's own standard ("A student who checked everything hasn't shown that judgment"). Possible fixes:
   - Offer three options at gate 2: "Rely on it" · "Move my check here" · "Ask for time to check both". The suggestion would be the third, with the note "Moving the check leaves Task B unchecked again".
   - Or put the budget change in the condition itself, so nobody wins by ignoring the rules ("…the company will build the segment you pick. You can ask for one more day.").
   - In either case, change the bottom band after gate 2 (for example to "TIME FOR ONE CHECK → ASK FOR A SECOND"), so the final frame doesn't contradict itself.
2. **The page copy gives the answer before the call.** "A good answer…", Evidence and "About the tasks" are written as visible page sections, and each names the sides. Put them behind the same gate as the beat list, or below a clearly marked "after you've made your call" divider. Say what print does with them. This is the only route I found by which the no-choice views give the answer away first.
3. **The figure calls Task A "idea work" after making it look like a pick.** On screen, Task A ends "pick the segment that matters most", which the storyboard deliberately makes look like Task B. Beat 7 then calls it "idea work you can judge by reading". A teacher who checked A because choosing what matters most is a judgment call will feel the task was relabelled after the fact. The distinction that actually carries over is this: Task B had a fact hidden in its materials that a fluent answer could miss, and Task A had none, so reading shows a weak segmentation but not a wrong brand pick. Say that in the caption, for example "Task A has no hidden fact to miss. Task B turned on interview details, and reading the answer wouldn't show the miss." That also settles an objection a sharp reader will raise: the figure uses graders' quality ratings as the warrant for relying on A (40%+) right after showing that quality ratings missed wrong answers on B. Reading-based quality is a fair measure exactly when the work is judged by reading. Saying so turns the apparent contradiction into the lesson.
4. **Options and verdicts share words.** "Check this one", "RELY ON IT", "Rely on it", "Check it" and "CHECK IT" all sit on card A. After each reveal, fade the option pills the reader didn't choose (or all of them on the no-call path) to about 40%, or mark the navy tags as verdicts with a leading word, so the still doesn't depend on fill style alone.
5. **"Show what happened" overclaims, especially at gate 2.** Nothing happened at gate 2: the page itself says the study didn't vary the condition. The same goes for the key "WHAT THE EVIDENCE SUGGESTS" on the row-2 tag, because that call comes from the stakes, not from Dell'Acqua. Something like "Show the call" for the button, and "WHAT THE EVIDENCE AND THE STAKES SUGGEST" (or just "SUGGESTED CALL") for the key, would match the page's own honesty note.
6. **It's too long, mainly at the front.** See the length section below.
7. **Playing past gate 1 without a call also skips gate 2.** A teacher who didn't know enough to call gate 1 is the one who most needs gate 2, which needs no study knowledge. Keep gate 2's pause even after a skip at gate 1.
8. **"Where AI stops being reliable" (Beat 4) generalises.** The frontier described is GPT-4's in 2023, and the page copy says so, but the caption says "AI". Something like "…where this AI stopped being reliable" is fairer to current tools and to the study.
9. **Smaller points.**
   - "Would you rely on Task A?" should read "on the AI's answer to Task A".
   - "Two groups" needs a three-word gloss on screen.
   - The new condition is on card A only, and a reader may ask whether a build decision raises Task B's stakes too.
   - At 390 px, Task B's two-line pattern tag and the "WORK IT FROM THE INTERVIEWS, THEN COMPARE" tag will wrap to three or four lines in a half-width column. I doubt card A's column, with two rows of buttons plus the evidence list, fits in 844 px. The storyboard allows scrolling at a gate, and the site is desktop-first, so this is low priority.

## 6. The orchestrator's questions

**What I chose and why.** Gate 1: Task B, because reconciling a spreadsheet with interviews can hide a conflict that reading the answer won't show. Gate 2: Check it, meaning "and ask for time", which the buttons couldn't express (see Beat 9 for the caveat).

**Did the reveal teach me something?** Yes, though not the sides, because I already knew the study.

- Beat 8 taught me something new: reliance is an allocation that pays for the check elsewhere, and a check should start from the source material before reading the AI's answer.
- Beat 6's bracket would be the main surprise for a first-timer.
- Beat 10 taught little, because it was predictable from the question's form.

**Does "always check", or another blanket strategy, still win without thinking?**

- **Gate 1:** "always check" can't be played, because the choice is forced. "Check the one that looks harder" or "AI is bad with spreadsheets" both win it without the reasoning the figure wants. Beat 7 does correct the reason, which helps.
- **Gate 2:** yes, "always check" wins outright, and the careful reader who kept to the budget loses. That's the biggest fix (problem 1).

**Does the no-choice version carry the idea without giving the answers away first?**

- The picture does: the poster, reduced motion, print order and beat list all ask before they answer.
- The final still carries the idea, apart from the "one check" against "second check" contradiction and the crowded verbs on card A.
- The surrounding page copy is the leak (problem 2).

## 7. Length

At 124.5 s on the no-call path, and 115.8 s plus thinking time when interactive (realistically two and a half minutes or more), it's at the upper limit. The gates justify more length than a passive figure would get, because the reader controls the pace there. The risk is concentrated in two places:

- **The first 30 seconds,** before the first interaction. Beat 1 alone is 16.5 s, and its caption repeats the source line.
- **The 60 seconds from gate 1 to the Beat 8 suggestions.** Every beat here earns its place except Beat 5.

What I'd cut, aiming at roughly 105–110 s on the no-call path (under 100 s plus decisions when interactive):

- **Merge Beats 4 and 5,** so the frontier draws and then A's three numbers land in one beat: about −7 s. Beat 5 confirms the side Beat 4 has just shown.
- **Rework Beat 1's caption** so it doesn't repeat the source line (for example "Two tasks from one field experiment.") and bring card B in sooner: about −3 s.
- **Drop the frontier pulse in Beat 10.** The caption already says "same side of the frontier": about −3 s.
- **Trim Beat 6's full-size hold** from 5.0 s to 3.5 s: −1.5 s. This is the beat I'd protect most. Don't cut the dim or the bracket.

I would not cut Beat 8 or the faculty check.
