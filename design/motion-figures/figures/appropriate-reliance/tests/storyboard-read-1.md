# Appropriate reliance: storyboard cold read, round 1

Tester: cold reader, first-time educator. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only. Date: October 28, 2026.

**How I read it.** The read tool returned the whole file in one piece, so I couldn't physically stop at the gates. For each gate I made my call using only what the earlier beats put on screen, and wrote it down before thinking about later beats. Where hindsight could have leaked in, I say so. I wrote sections 1 to 5 before opening the source spine. The fact check at the end came afterwards.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · Two tasks (0–16 s).** Two task cards and a line saying "758 BCG consultants". The tasks look like siblings: both end in "pick the …". Task B names its materials (a spreadsheet and insider interviews) and Task A names none. I read this as "here are two consulting tasks," and nothing yet tells me why there are two. Sixteen seconds is fine because there's a lot of text.

**Beat 2 · The AI answers both (16–24.5 s).** Identical grey lines and a "READY" pill on both. The message is clear: the two answers look the same. One thing I noticed, and it matters later: I can't read either answer. They're just lines.

**Beat 3 · Gate 1 (24.5–37 s).** "If it's a first draft for your team", then three choices per task.

*My calls:*
- **Task A: Check it first.** I can't see the answer, I know nothing about how good AI is at market segmentation, and my default as a teacher is to verify. "First draft for your team" pulled me a little towards Rely on it, since the team is a second check, but not enough.
- **Task B: Check it first.** It names sources (a spreadsheet, interviews), so there's something to check the pick against. I also half-suspected the opposite of the study's finding: a spreadsheet sounds like the kind of structured work AI is good at, and "the segment that matters most" sounds like judgement. A reader who acts on that hunch would rely on B and check A. That reader gets the biggest surprise from this figure, which is good.

*Note:* my calls didn't separate the two tasks at all. Only "Rely on it" forces you to tell them apart, and a cautious educator will rarely pick it.

**Beat 4 · The jagged frontier (37–50.5 s).** A jagged line between the cards, with A inside and B outside. The definition, "where AI stops being reliable", is clear and gets its own beat, which helps. The READY pills pulsing together restates beat 2 neatly. What I wondered: how does anyone know which side a task is on? On screen it looks like a fact about the task types. Nothing says the study *designed* Task B to fall outside, or that this was GPT-4 in 2023.

**Beat 5 · Inside the frontier (50.5–62.5 s).** A big panel: 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, 40%+ higher quality. Then it shrinks onto card A. Clear. Two things: the big numbers get about 2 s before they start shrinking, and once they're parked on card A they read as *Task A's* numbers, although the panel title says "tasks inside the frontier" in general. Only 1.8 s after the panel lands, the scene dims again for beat 6. It's easy to miss where the numbers went.

**Beat 6 · Outside the frontier (62.5–75.5 s).** Without AI 84.5%; with AI 60% and 70%; a bracket: "Rated higher in quality, right or wrong." This is the beat that taught me something. AI didn't just fail to help, it made people worse *and* made the worse work look better to graders. That second half is the real hook for teachers, because it's exactly the trap in grading. It gets about 2 s at full size before it shrinks, which is too short for the most surprising line in the figure. It does stay on the card afterwards, and the caption carries it. "TWO GROUPS" raised a question (why two?) that the figure never answers.

**Beat 7 · What each answer rested on (75.5–87.5 s).** Task A: "IDEA WORK · YOU CAN JUDGE IT BY READING." Task B: "THE RIGHT ANSWER TURNED ON THE INTERVIEWS." I understood the first. The second left me asking *why* the interviews defeated the AI. Did it not read them? Did they contradict the spreadsheet? Was the signal subtle? Without that, I can't carry the pattern to a new task. "Turned on the interviews" names where the answer lived but doesn't say what made it hard. The Notice also says the reason "was on screen before the call", which made me feel I should have spotted it. I don't think a first-time reader could have, and the underline lands as hindsight.

**Beat 8 · The calls as a first draft (87.5–99.5 s).** Task A: Rely on it is WARRANTED; my Check it first reads "SAFE, SLOWER". Task B: Check it first and Don't use it are both WARRANTED.

*Did the reveal teach me something?* Partly. On A I learned that relying would have been fine, but "SAFE, SLOWER" let me off the hook. It doesn't say I was wrong, and I left thinking checking is always fine. On B I felt vindicated, but on reflection I shouldn't have. The consultants in the AI groups had the same spreadsheet and interviews and could have checked, and they still landed at 60–70%. The figure marks "Check it first" warranted on B without showing that checking works there. "Task B needed a check against the interviews, or no AI" is stated as a result.

**Beat 9 · One thing changes (99.5–111.5 s).** "If the company builds what you pick", on Task A only.

*My call:* **Check it first.** Higher stakes, and I was already checking. Here, though, I hit a real snag: check *what*? Task A names no sources. The option's description, "Test it against the task's sources before using it", has nothing to point at. And beat 7 just told me Task A is work "you can judge by reading", so reading it should already be the check. I picked Check it first by reflex, not because I knew what the check would be.

**Beat 10 · The call flips (111.5–123.5 s).** Check it first is WARRANTED; Rely on it reads "A MISS GETS BUILT". For me nothing flipped: I checked both times and was marked right both times. The flip only exists for a reader who relied on A at gate 1. The caption, "Same task, same side of the frontier. Now a miss would get built, so check it first", is the clearest sentence in the figure.

**Beat 11 · Faculty check (123.5–133.5 s).** "What evidence justified relying on the system here, and what would have changed the call?" plus "Change one condition and ask for the call again." Good move line. "Here" is vague with three rows on screen, though it most likely means Task A, row 1.

**Captions changing before I'd read them:** none on paper. The timing is careful. The risks are the two big-panel windows (beat 5's numbers, beat 6's bracket), about 2 s each before they shrink, and the 1.8 s hold at the end of beat 5.

## 2. The point, in one sentence

Whether to rely on an AI answer depends on the kind of task and on what a miss would cost, not on how finished the answer looks, because on some tasks AI makes people less accurate while making their work look better.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before you use this AI answer: what does being right depend on, would you spot a miss just by reading it, and what happens if it's wrong? If one of those changed, would your call change, and why?"

## 4. The final frame alone (also reduced motion, print, poster)

**Does it carry the idea?** For a careful reader, yes. The strongest single element is card A's two rows: same task, two conditions, two different WARRANTED tags. That's the lesson in one glance. Card B's bars and bracket carry "worse, but rated better".

**What it says to me:** two look-alike tasks got the same polished answer. On one, relying was fine. On the other, AI made people wrong and graders didn't notice. And on the safe one, raising the stakes still calls for a check.

**Where it falls short:**
- **It reads as an answer key.** With no choice made, the mechanic that does the teaching (predict, then compare) is gone. What's left is a table of verdicts. The faculty check partly restores the question, but the WARRANTED tags answer it before a student can try.
- **It's very dense.** Two task texts, two pattern tags, two AI answers, three option rows with nine notes and tags, two evidence blocks, frontier labels, a source line and a two-line caption. There's no clear reading order: do I start at the tags, the evidence or the verdicts?
- **Poster and social card:** 13-unit mono labels will be unreadable at thumbnail size, and the card shows every verdict to anyone who sees the share before opening the figure.
- **Reduced motion opens on the final frame,** so a reduced-motion user sees every WARRANTED tag before reaching the beat 3 gate in the list. The gate is spoiled for exactly the readers who can't use the animation. The visually hidden figure summary does the same for screen-reader users: it gives the warranted calls before the figure starts.
- **Print** gives a teacher the answer key. For class use, a teacher would want the gate still (beat 3, no tags) to hand out, and the final frame to reveal.
- **The no-call play-through** (the preview video) still works as a "pause and think" because each gate holds its full length with "YOUR CALL" in red. Without a commitment, though, the reveal teaches facts, not calibration.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **"Check it first" is never wrong, so the figure can't tell a reasoned call from a blanket rule.** Across all three rows, checking is marked SAFE, SLOWER, then WARRANTED, then WARRANTED. A reader who always checks (likely most cautious educators, as I was) is marked right everywhere and never sees a flip. The flip at beat 10 only teaches readers who relied on Task A at gate 1. The faculty-check rationale ("shows whether a student has a reason for the call or only a sense that the answer looked right") doesn't hold for the always-checker. The setup's definition (accept when warranted, check or refuse when not) implies that checking a warranted answer is under-reliance, but "SAFE, SLOWER" doesn't say so. *Type: confusion / weak lesson.* Options: show what checking costs on A (for example, tie it to the 25.1% speed result), or add a condition that flips the call *towards* relying.
2. **"Check it first · WARRANTED" on Task B isn't the study's result, and the study leans against it.** The AI groups had the same materials and could have checked, yet were right 60–70% of the time against 84.5% without AI. The storyboard's own Evidence note says the study "didn't test checking", but on screen the tag sits next to the study's bars, and beat 8's caption ("Task B needed a check against the interviews, or no AI") reads as a finding. A teacher could pass on "just check it and you'll be fine", which is the overconfidence the bars refute. *Type: factual claim / unfair to students.*
3. **"The right answer turned on the interviews" names where the answer lived, not why the AI missed it.** Without the mechanism (I'd guess the interviews held something the spreadsheet didn't show, but the figure doesn't say), the pattern doesn't travel to a teacher's own task. Beat 7's framing that "the reason is in the task text, on screen before the call" is hindsight: on screen nothing says the study *chose* Task B to fall outside the frontier. *Type: confusion / unfair to the reader who relied on B.*
4. **Task A's check is undefined, and "judge it by reading" can't be tried.** The AI's answers are grey lines, so the reader is told Task A is judgeable by reading but can't read it. At gate 2, "Check it first" on Task A has no sources to check against ("Test it against the task's sources"), and beat 7 has just implied that reading *is* the check. So what does checking add once the company builds it? *Type: ambiguous visual / ambiguous option.*
5. **The frontier looks timeless.** On screen it's "an AI". GPT-4 and 2023 appear only in the page's Evidence block. A 2026 faculty reader will ask whether Task B is still outside the frontier. Saying "in 2023, with GPT-4" on screen would be fairer to AI and more honest about how the frontier moves. *Type: unfair to AI / sourcing.*
6. **The no-choice views spoil the gate.** Reduced motion opens on the final frame, the visually hidden summary states the warranted calls before the figure starts, and the poster shows every verdict. *Type: accessibility / design contradiction.*
7. **The inside-frontier numbers look like Task A's.** 12.2%, 25.1% and 40%+ are for "tasks inside the frontier" in general, but they park on card A as if they were measured on that task. *Type: caption/picture mismatch, minor.*
8. **Two short windows.** Beat 5's numbers and beat 6's "Rated higher in quality, right or wrong" each get about 2 s at full size before shrinking, and beat 5 holds only 1.8 s before the next dim. The content persists, but the figure's most surprising line gets its big moment for two seconds. *Type: too fast, minor.*
9. **"TWO GROUPS"** raises a question (why two AI groups, and what was different?) that the figure never answers. *Minor.*
10. **"Here" in the faculty check** is vague with three rows on screen. *Minor.*
11. **The red dot.** Red usually signals "wrong" to teachers. A red dot next to a navy WARRANTED tag could read as a correction mark even when the reader was right. *Minor.*
12. **Phone fit.** Two side-by-side columns at 390 px, each holding task text, a tag, a chip, three 40 px buttons (six on A) and evidence, plus the caption band and gate buttons, look tight for 844 px. The storyboard already allows scrolling at a gate, and the site is desktop-first. *Risk only.*

## Length (about 124–134 s)

**Verdict:** long but mostly earned. Most of the time is reading, not choreography, and the gates make it feel shorter because the viewer is doing something. The interactive run is realistically 2.5–3 minutes with decision time. That's acceptable for an educator who chose to engage, but it's at the edge. The 133.5 s no-call run (the preview video) is too long for passive viewing.

**What I'd cut (about 10 s, no beats lost):**
- **Beat 5's centre-stage rise and shrink.** The inside-frontier result is the expected one. Write the three numbers straight into card A's slot without dimming. That saves about 4 s, removes one of three dim cycles and fixes the 1.8 s hold. Keep the big panel for beat 6, which is the surprise.
- **Beat 9's large condition.** The caption already states it. Fade the row-2 label straight in, which saves about 3 s and a dim cycle.
- **Beat 4's READY pulse** restates beat 2. Cut it and trim the hold, saving about 2–3 s.
- I wouldn't cut gate 2. It carries "what would have changed the call", which is the faculty check. I'd rather spend the saved seconds fixing problem 1 than remove it.

## Fact check (done after the read)

I checked against `nwc-irreducible-officer-companion/sources/source-spine.md` (the Jagged Frontier entry, line 62). For the two essay references the spine doesn't cover, I checked `the-irreducible-officer.md`.

**In the spine, and matching:**
- 758 BCG consultants, GPT-4, field experiment.
- Inside the frontier: 12.2% more tasks, 25.1% faster, more than 40% higher quality ("40%+" on screen).
- On a task chosen to fall outside it: without AI about 84.5% right, the two AI groups 60% and 70%.
- "19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions": the working-paper wording, as the spine specifies. The spine notes the published version (Organization Science, 2026) says "19% less likely". The page's Evidence block correctly quotes the working paper.
- Graders rated the AI users' recommendations higher in quality "regardless of the correctness of their answer". "Rated higher in quality, right or wrong" is a fair paraphrase.
- HBS working paper 24-013, 2023, and the link.

**Not in the spine:**
- **Both task descriptions.** The spine never describes the tasks: no footwear segmentation, no spreadsheet plus insider interviews, no brand pick. The storyboard says they're paraphrased from the working paper, which I couldn't verify from the spine. In particular, "pick the segment that matters most" and the parallel "pick the …" second lines look built for the figure. Check them against the paper, and check that a segmentation subtask really was among the inside-frontier tasks the 12.2/25.1/40% figures cover.
- **"The right answer turned on the interviews."** That's a claim about the study's mechanism. The spine says only that the task was "chosen to fall outside" the frontier.
- **"Idea work · you can judge it by reading."** The figure's own characterisation, not a study finding.
- **"Check it first · WARRANTED" on Task B, and "SAFE, SLOWER" on Task A.** Nothing in the spine covers checking (the storyboard admits the study didn't test it). Two spine entries cut against treating checking as a reliable fix. Vaccaro et al. (2024) found human–AI combinations did worse than the better of human or AI alone, with losses on decision tasks (g = −0.27), and Task B is a decision task. Kim et al. / Jiang et al. (2025) found that asking a second model "is not an independent check". This strengthens problem 2.
- **84.5%** shows on screen without the spine's "about". That's minor; the Evidence block keeps it.
- **GPT-4** is in the spine but not on screen (problem 5).

**Checked in the essay instead (not in the spine):**
- Section V's quoted ending, "what independent checks are required before the output governs the work": present, line 87.
- Raees & Papangelis, 2026: cited in the essay (line 89; reference line 229). The spine lists the paper without a year.
- The setup's definition of appropriate reliance matches the essay's ("accepting support when it is warranted, refusing or verifying when it is not, and being able to give an account of the difference").
- The essay says of the frontier that "the boundary was not obvious in advance". That cuts against beat 7's Notice, "the reason is in the task text, which was on screen before the call", and supports problem 3. The essay also names "risk levels" next to task types, which supports keeping gate 2.
