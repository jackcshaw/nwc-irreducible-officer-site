## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same signed, AI-drafted recommendation and both stay answerable for it. The paper can't show which of them examined what the AI's estimate rested on; only asking each one why they relied on it brings that out.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

To a student, before the work is graded: "You relied on this estimate. What does it assume, what did you check before you signed, and what would make it wrong?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It carries most of the idea. Two identical grey AI drafts, both signed, each with ANSWERABLE resting on the student's signature. The two questions. Two answers that differ sharply: the first with "The analysis" outlined, the second bracketed in red. The second student's note, labelled NOT HANDED IN. The faculty check underneath. Taken alone it tells me: same paper, same answerability; the difference is only in what each student could say when asked why.

What the still loses:
- The "FACULTY DON'T SEE THIS" and "WHAT FACULTY SEE" framing is gone. Only "NOT HANDED IN" tells you faculty never saw the note.
- The red dot beside "The analysis" isn't explained in the still at all.
- The outline linking "The analysis" in the answer to "The analysis" on the copy survives and helps.

On the phone, the final frame puts the ANSWERABLE tags right beside the signatures, which reads even more clearly.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Ambiguous visual: the red dot beside "The analysis"** (from 1:12.5 to the end, including the final and reduced-motion frames). The split doesn't read: the dot left at the signature doesn't shrink, and the new dot is larger. The key defines red as "a student's decision", so the mark can read as "the student decided here" rather than "the student handed this to the AI". And the second student, who also relied on the estimate, gets no equivalent mark. A teacher could misread it, or skip it as decoration.
2. **Caption 7, "The first leans on the AI", can be taken as being about reliance.** The second student says outright, "I chose to rely on the estimate." A teacher could leave thinking the failure is relying on the AI at all. The figure doesn't mean that; the page text below even says whether the reliance was justified is a separate check. The real difference is between giving the AI as your reason and giving your own reason. "Right or wrong" also needs a second read.
3. **Phone (390) collisions:**
   - (a) "FACULTY DON'T SEE THIS" prints over the faded note text, and the frame label "WHAT FACULTY SEE" prints over "normal.". This lasts all of beats 5–6, 0:39–1:06.
   - (b) When the ANSWERABLE tags first appear (1:17.5–1:21) they sit on top of "The analysis" on both copies, hiding the phrase that was outlined a few seconds earlier.
   - (c) The dot beside "The analysis" hangs over the card's left edge.
4. **Mild drag** at 0:02.5–0:06.4, 0:08–0:10.5, in the typed answers of beats 5–6, and in the 4 s tag hover in beat 8. See section 1.
5. **Beat 2 shows neither a prompt nor an AI.** The dots pulse, then a blank sheet appears. The caption and the "WRITTEN BY AI" key carry it. Minor.
6. **Small things:**
   - The dash marking "no note" in the first student's column is easy to miss.
   - Nothing in the picture says the questions come from faculty; only the caption does.
   - The ANSWERABLE shapes read as tags, not weights.
   - At 1280×900, tabbing to Play scrolls the page just far enough to start autoplay with the top of the stage, including the task card, off-screen.
7. **Fairness:**
   - To students: it seems fair. Both are shown reading closely. The second student also admits a miss ("I should have checked for closures"), so the figure doesn't turn them into a hero. The first student's "It seemed solid" is an honest, plausible answer and isn't mocked.
   - To the AI: also fair. Its estimate is never shown to be wrong. "The AI can't answer for it" is presented as a principle, not a claim about what the model can do.
8. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:** none with numbers. The page's claims about what Section VII asks of students, and what the companion's failure-mode lab says about signed statements of responsibility and sampling, should be checked against those sources. The task, draft and students are labelled as constructed.
