## 2. The point

Two people can sign the same AI-drafted recommendation and both own it, but you only find out whether either has a reason of their own, rather than the AI's, by asking why they relied on it.

## 3. The question to ask afterwards

For a manager reviewing a team's work: "For the last AI-drafted recommendation I signed or approved, could I say what its key number rests on and one way it could be wrong, without quoting the draft?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It mostly carries the idea. On their own you can see:
- two identical copies, both with AI-written text and both signed;
- the second analyst's private note;
- four answers: the same first answer from both, and different second answers;
- "The analysis" outlined in both the copy and the first analyst's answer, which shows the first analyst repeating the AI;
- a red bracket on the second answer, the analyst's own reason;
- "Answerable" on both signatures;
- the definition and the check.

What it says to me: same document, same ownership, different reasons, so ask why before you accept the signature.

Two parts don't explain themselves without the animation. The hollow dashed ring is only decoded by the key "Reason handed to the AI". The smaller first-analyst dot has no key at all, so in the still frame it just looks like a smaller dot. The "Related case" line sits beside a picture of two analysts with no context. A viewer who sees only the figure, for example as a screenshot in a deck, could assume Moffatt is a case about employees signing AI recommendations.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The split dot can read as "less responsible" (ambiguous visual).** After beat 7 the first analyst's dot at the signature is smaller than the second's (at 2× scale, roughly 16 px against 19 px wide; it reads as clearly smaller). It stays that way when "Answerable" lands on it. A quick viewer may take away "half of the first analyst's responsibility went to the AI", which is the illusion the figure argues against. The key names the ring but not the smaller dot. A related stumble: the name promises someone dodging blame, yet both analysts say "I did. I signed it." The laundering is in the reason, not the ownership answer. The prose under the figure resolves this ("Both answer for the call. Only the second can say what it rested on."), but the picture leans on the split dot to make the bridge. Suggestion: keep the first analyst's signature dot full size and let only a copy or ghost of it travel to the AI line. Or key the remaining half explicitly.
2. **Military wording in the page chrome.** "See this with: Workplace · PME" uses "PME" with no expansion. A business reader won't know it means professional military education. The meta description calls the page "A teaching figure", which is classroom wording and would show in link previews. The figure and the main prose are clean. "Go further" names the essay as written for military education, but that is labelled provenance and is fine.
3. **The figure's source line has no context (risk of overclaiming).** On its own, "Related case: Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)." beside two analysts invites the reading that the case is about staff signing AI memos. The page prose is careful to say it isn't. The figure line could carry a few words, for example "Related case, a company's own chatbot: …".
4. **Numbers not in the source spine (factual check).** `sources/source-spine.md` has no entry for Moffatt at all. The page's case figures appear only in the page's own citation: November 2022, the 90-day quote, paras 2/14/15/16/17/27/28/44, $812.02, $650.88 and 14 February 2024. They are attributed to the decision with paragraph numbers (I checked them afterwards; see Fact check). The 97% is declared as constructed, and "7 of 7" is the series count. Strictly, the mechanical check fails until the spine gets an entry.
5. **Nonprofit fit.** A retailer and refund questions are instantly recognisable to anyone in CX, ops or analytics at a company. A nonprofit manager has to translate, for example to a benefits-eligibility or donor-FAQ chatbot. The pattern carries over (an AI-drafted go/no-go memo signed by staff), but nothing on the page signals that it applies outside retail.
6. **Small marks at 390 (mobile).** The half dot, its dashed trail and the grey ring are about 7 px in the stacked view. The trip up the copy's edge is hard to see. In the final frame the source line sits under the controls and is cut off at the bottom of an 844 px screen until you scroll.
7. **The poster before autoplay looks mid-play.** Before the figure is in view it shows 0:10 / 1:38, a part-filled scrubber, and the task card and analyst labels ghosted behind the name card. It then jumps to 0:00.
8. **Polish.** The inline "uncalibrated reliance" link in the check paragraph renders in default browser blue (rgb 0,0,238), unlike every other link on the page (navy). Apostrophes are straight in the setup and check paragraphs ("retailer's", "can't") but curly in the figure ("model's", "doesn't").
9. **"Agency" in the definition** is a little academic for a business audience. "Ownership" is the word a manager would use. This may be fixed wording shared across editions, so it's low priority.

Nothing here seems unfair to the AI: the AI's text is shown neutrally, and the figure never says the 97% is wrong.

## Business-reader questions

- **Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work?** A company reader, yes, strongly. Deciding whether a support chatbot should take refund questions, an AI-drafted memo with an accuracy estimate, a passive "It is recommended…", a manager's "who made this call?": that is a normal week in CX or ops. A nonprofit reader will recognise the workflow but has to translate the setting (Problems, 5).
- **Is either person a straw man, or is there a second difference?** Neither is a straw man. The figure takes care to make them equal: same prompt, same draft, same close reading at the same pace, same signature, and the same first answer, "I did. I signed it." The note is not a second difference. It is the same difference shown earlier: the second analyst asked what the estimate rests on before signing, which is why they can answer later. The first analyst's "It seemed solid" is thin, but it is how many careful people actually answer. Two things a sharp reader may notice:
  - The second analyst admits "I didn't test exceptions." That adds candour. On a refund chatbot, where exceptions are the whole risk, a manager may think the second analyst signed knowing about the gap. The page heads this off ("Whether relying on the estimate was justified is a separate check, covered by uncalibrated reliance").
  - The smaller first-analyst dot (Problems, 1) visually suggests a difference in responsibility that the figure doesn't intend.
- **Does the page overstate the legal case, imply personal exposure, or read as legal advice?** No. It says:
  - "In the figure, 'answer for it' means inside the organization, to the manager and the company. The figure makes no claim about anyone's personal legal liability."
  - "The case concerns a company's own customer-facing chatbot. It says nothing about employees signing an internal recommendation."
  - The record has limits: one decision of a small-claims tribunal, with no evidence about how the chatbot worked.

  The check paragraph says "both answer for it inside the company". The one place a reader could over-read is the in-figure source line (Problems, 3). Nothing reads as advice.
- **Is the source line clear, without overclaiming?** On the page, yes. It is labelled "Related case", not "Source". The full citation "Moffatt v. Air Canada, 2024 BCCRT 149, Civil Resolution Tribunal, February 14, 2024" links to CanLII, and every quote carries a paragraph number. In the figure, the line is clear but has no context (Problems, 3).
- **Any military or classroom wording left?** Inside the figure, none: it says analysts, manager, example task, reviewer's check. In the page chrome: "PME" in the edition switcher (unexplained), "A teaching figure" in the meta description, and the labelled provenance in "Go further" ("an essay about judgment in military education"; "The Irreducible Officer"), which is fine.

## Fact check: *Moffatt v. Air Canada*, 2024 BCCRT 149 (done after writing the read above)

**Access.** The decision itself was out of reach. CanLII (the page's link) and the Civil Resolution Tribunal's own decisions site both returned HTTP 403 to scripted access, and I did not try to get round that. I checked the page against secondary sources that quote the decision verbatim:
- the Obligations Discussion Group archive (stevehedley.com), which quotes para. 27 in full;
- McCarthy Tétrault's TechLex blog;
- Joshua Gans's Substack;
- Law360 Canada;
- BDP Law;
- Global News and Business Today for the amounts;
- the barnoldlaw blog, for paragraph labels.

The essay and `source-spine.md` contain no Moffatt material and no 97%, which I confirmed by grep after writing the read.

| Page claim | Result |
|---|---|
| Customer asked the chatbot about bereavement fares in November 2022, after a death in the family | Confirmed. Moffatt's grandmother died in November 2022 and he used the chatbot that day. |
| Chatbot quote: "If you need to travel immediately or have already travelled and would like to submit your ticket for a reduced bereavement rate, kindly do so within 90 days of the date your ticket was issued…" (para. 15) | Wording confirmed verbatim by Law360 and BDP. The full sentence continues "…by completing our Ticket Refund Application form", which the page's ellipsis fairly marks. Para. 15 matches one blog's labelling. |
| The answer linked to the bereavement page (para. 16) | Confirmed: "bereavement fares" was a hyperlink to a page titled "Bereavement travel". Para. 16 matches one blog's labelling. |
| That page said the policy doesn't apply to requests made after travel is complete (para. 17) | Confirmed as a paraphrase. The decision quotes the page as saying the policy "does not apply to requests for bereavement consideration after travel has been completed". The page doesn't put its version in quotation marks, which is right. |
| "did not permit retroactive applications" (para. 2) | Wording confirmed (Gans: "Mr. Moffatt later learned from Air Canada employees that Air Canada did not permit retroactive applications."). The paragraph number isn't confirmed by any source I could reach. |

**Verdict.** Every quotation I could check is accurate, and so are the amounts, date, forum and characterisations. Nothing overstates the holding. Two caveats:
- Paragraph numbers 2, 14, 28 and 44 are unverified because the primary sources were blocked; 15, 16 and 17 match one blog's labelling, and 27 is confirmed.
- The agents/servants/representatives line is the tribunal's paraphrase of Air Canada's argument. It would be clearer to write "the tribunal summarised the airline's argument as…".
