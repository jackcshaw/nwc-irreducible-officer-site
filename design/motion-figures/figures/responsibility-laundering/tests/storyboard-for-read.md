## 2. Scenario

A war-college student must recommend when to evacuate civilians from a port city. They ask an AI for an analysis, and it drafts a clear recommendation in its own voice: "The analysis indicates the unrest will stay inland." "The model's estimate: the port stays open." "It is recommended that civilians wait for ships." The student reads it closely, agrees, and signs it as written. A second student gets the same draft, reads it just as closely, and names themselves in each call before signing: "I judge the unrest will stay inland." "I rely on the model's estimate: the port stays open." "I recommend that civilians wait for ships." If the recommendation proves wrong, the model can't answer for it, and each student will. Faculty, who have only the two papers, ask each student: "Suppose the port closes on day one. Who made the call to wait?" The first says, "I signed it. But the model expected the port to stay open." The second says, "I did. I chose to rely on the model's estimate."

*Sources.* The task, the draft, both papers and both answers are constructed for this figure, and the task card says "Example task". *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, defines responsibility laundering. Section VII says AI "cannot own what follows", describes conviction-shaped output (Andres, 2026), and asks students to say "why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution". The figure's only number is "day one", part of the constructed example.

Whoever signs the recommendation answers for it, and the model can't. What faculty can hear is whether the student knows that. In the figure, both recommendations would be wrong if the port closed on day one. Asked who made the call to wait, one student points to the model's estimate and the other says they chose to rely on it. Writing "the analysis indicates" is ordinary staff style and shows little by itself; "I recommend" shows little by itself too. Faculty hear the difference when they name one way the call could go wrong and ask who made it. Section VII of the essay asks students to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. The companion's failure-mode lab makes a related point about a signed statement of responsibility: it can add evidence, but it doesn't show that the learner can defend the judgment. Whether relying on the model's estimate was justified is a separate check, covered by uncalibrated reliance.

**From the essay** (*The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, What the Problem Looks Like):

> Responsibility laundering is the final danger. A recommendation becomes easier to defend because the model generated it, or easier to soften because the model's language distributes agency. The analysis lands with the apparent weight of objectivity. But AI does not become accountable for the recommendation. The human remains responsible for the final judgment. When the recommendation proves wrong, shaped by assumptions no one examined and optimizing toward a target no one explicitly chose, the question of who chose the frame does not have a satisfying answer.

And from Section VII, Accountability Is Structural:

> A human recommendation brings context with it. Experience, incentives, reputation, and the way a person answers when pressed all travel with the recommendation. A commander can weigh those signals. A model carries none of them and can still sound equally confident. It can support a judgment, but it cannot provide the human presence that makes accountability clear to subordinates, partners, or commanders who will live with the result.
>
> First-person ownership matters in the classroom. The student should be able to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. Students are practicing the accountability structure their professional roles will require.
