I recognised it at once: my team is having this conversation this year. It's slow, though. The four-word caption and two dots are done by about 5.5 s, and then nothing moves for five seconds. Nothing rushed me, but a busy reader might scroll on.

**3 · "Both analysts read it closely and agree." (7.5 s).** "Written by AI" appears in the key. Both dots move along each line of their copies at the same pace. I read this as: equal effort, nobody skimmed. Good. It closes off "the first one was lazy" before I could think it.

## 2. The point, in my words

When two people sign the same AI-drafted recommendation, the document can't show you which of them understood the call. Asking "why did you rely on it?" can: one points back at the AI, the other names what the estimate rests on. Either way both of them, not the AI, answer for it.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"Before you put your name on this: what is the 97% based on, and name one kind of case where it would be wrong."

## 4. The final frame alone

It mostly works without the animation. Two identical copies inside a "drafted with AI" label, both carrying an "Answerable" tag, show that the documents are the same and so is the accountability. The two answer boxes side by side carry the real contrast: "It was the AI's estimate. It seemed solid." against a three-line answer with a red bracket. The note row ("their own note… Rests on last quarter's questions") tells me where the second answer came from. The definition and the check sit right underneath at a readable size.

What it doesn't carry: why the first analyst's dot is smaller, and that the dashed ring is "their half". Without the animation the ring is just a small symbol explained by the key ("Reason handed to the AI"), which is fine but weaker. Nothing in the frame tells you which answers came from the "What the manager sees" moment, though the numbered questions (1 · / 2 ·) help.

What it says to me: same paper, same signature, same exposure; the difference only shows when someone asks "why".

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Beat 7 is too dense, and its key movement is too small and fast.** Five changes in about 5 s across five places. The dot-halves-into-a-ring move takes about 1 s and is 5–8 px. I missed where the ring came from on first viewing at 1280 and couldn't follow it at 390. Fix: give the ring its own moment (2–3 s, larger), or drop the halving and keep the outline plus ring. Hold beat 7 for 2–3 s more before the weights come in.
2. **Beat 7 compares unlike answers.** The first analyst's "AI reason" is outlined in answer 1 ("The analysis said 97%"); the second analyst's own reason is bracketed in answer 2. The caption is about the *why* (answer 2). Outline "the AI's estimate" in the first analyst's answer 2 so the bracket and the outline sit on the same question.
3. **"Not handed in" is classroom wording** (homework). In a workplace: "not shared", "not sent", "kept to themselves". It shows in the note row at every viewport, and in the description and beat list.
4. **The source line "Based on Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)" overstates the link** (details in the next section). The figure is about two employees signing an AI-drafted internal recommendation; the case is about a company's liability for what its own customer-facing chatbot told a customer. The page's "The record has limits" paragraph says so, but the line under the picture and the intro ("Illustration based on…") don't. "Prompted by" or "Related case:" would be accurate.
5. **"Both analysts will [answer for it]" next to a tribunal decision can read as personal legal liability.** In Moffatt the *company* paid; no employee was held to account. "Answerable" here means answerable to the manager and the company, and the page should say so once.
6. **Phone (390): a stray line under "1 · "I signed it."** in the first answer box looks like an underline, as if "I signed it" were stressed. It seems to be the outline box for "The analysis" breaking across a line wrap. Visible from beat 7 to the end and in the reduced view.
7. **Beat 1 drags** (about 5 s with nothing moving), and the whole run is 1:36. For a business reader that's long. Trimming beat 1 to about 4 s would pay for the extra time beat 7 needs.
8. **"I should have tested exceptions" reads as though the failure has already happened**, while the manager's question is hypothetical. "I'd want to test exceptions before go-live", or a question in past tense, would line it up.
9. **"Agency" in the definition is ambiguous for business readers** ("ad agency"; principal–agent law, which is exactly what Moffatt turns on). "Makes responsibility feel shared" would avoid it, if the essay's wording allows.
10. **The beat list describes the desktop layout** ("the first analyst's cell shows a dash"; the weight "falls down the paper's margin"; source "under the picture, at the right"). At 390 there's no dash, the weight lands as a tag beside the signature, and the source sits below the scrubber. A small mismatch for phone and screen-reader users.
11. **Minor:** before playing, the timer shows "0:10 / 1:36" (a poster at beat 1), but Play starts from 0:00. "Reviewer's check" names a role the figure calls "the manager". "7 of 7" points to a series the reader may not have seen.

Nothing in the figure is unfair to the AI: its estimate is never shown to be wrong, and "Suppose it proves wrong" is said plainly. The first analyst isn't a straw man either. They read as closely, sign, and say "I signed it". The difference between the two is real but double: the second analyst also did something extra before signing (the note). The page's commentary names that as the mechanism ("hard to give without asking the question before signing"), so it's a cause rather than a confound. A quick viewer may still take away "write notes" instead of "own the reasoning".

## Business-reader questions

**Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work here?** Yes, strongly. "Should the support chatbot answer refund questions?" is a live decision for support, CX and operations teams, and nonprofits with member or donor helplines will map it easily. "97% on last quarter's questions, recent ones match, exceptions untested" is exactly how these go-live calls get made. Two less natural details: two analysts independently producing and *signing* identical recommendations (in practice one person drafts and others "sign off" or approve in a doc or ticket), and "signs" itself, which is a bit formal. The "Suppose" framing makes both acceptable.

**Unfair, or a second difference that confounds the contrast?** Not unfair to the AI or either person (see the end of section 5). Three things to tighten: the extra note-taking step is a real second difference, but the page owns it as the cause; the beat 7 highlights compare answer 1 with answer 2 (problem 2); and the dot "halving" puts a visual penalty on the first analyst that the end then partly takes back (both answerable). That's defensible, but it only lands if the viewer saw where the half went.

**Does it overstate the legal case or read as legal advice?** The figure itself makes no legal claim. The page's case section is careful: direct quotes, paragraph numbers, and an explicit "The record has limits" paragraph (one small-claims tribunal decision, a company's own chatbot, a different situation, chatbot workings unknown). It doesn't read as legal advice. The overstatements are in the framing: "Illustration based on" and "Based on" (problem 4), "both analysts will answer" read next to a liability case (problem 5), and the closing link ("The chatbot in the case got that kind of question wrong, a special rule for bereavement fares"), which ties the analysts' untested "exceptions" to the bereavement policy more neatly than the decision supports. The decision says nothing about testing or what would have caught the error.

**Is the source line clear, without overclaiming?** It's clear: "Based on Moffatt v. Air Canada (2024)" under the picture, with the full citation, tribunal, date, "every quote is from the decision", and a list of what's constructed at the foot of the case section. The disclosure of constructed elements appears both in the intro and at the end, which is good. The overclaim is the single word "based"; see problem 4.

**Military or classroom wording still on the page?** "Not handed in" (the note row, the figure description, and the beat 4 list) is classroom wording. "PME (professional military education): the example from The Irreducible Officer, the National War College essay" sits near the top, under the edition switch. It's deliberate, but it's the second thing a business reader sees, and it raises "why is this military?" before the figure starts. Moving it below the figure, next to "Go further", would help. The "Go further" section names military education openly, which is honest and fine. I found no other military terms (no officer, commander, staff or mission) in the figure or the business text.

**Where the page goes further than the decision:**

- **"Illustration based on" / "Based on Moffatt v. Air Canada."** The decision is about a company's liability, through negligent misrepresentation, for what its own customer-facing chatbot told a customer. It says nothing about employees, internal recommendations, sign-off, or relying on AI estimates. The page's own limits paragraph admits this. The "based on" framing doesn't.
- **"The chatbot in the case got that kind of question wrong, a special rule for bereavement fares."** It's true that the chatbot misstated a special-case policy. But tying it to the analysts' untested "exceptions" suggests that testing would have caught it. The decision makes no finding about how the chatbot was built, tested, or why it erred. It notes Air Canada gave no information about the chatbot (the page quotes that line itself).
- **"Both analysts will [answer for it]," juxtaposed with the case.** The decision holds the company liable; no individual employee is held to account. If the page means answerable inside the organisation, it should say so, so the case isn't read as personal legal exposure.
- **Omission worth considering:** the tribunal rejected the defence that the correct information was on another page of the site, saying customers shouldn't have to double-check one part of a website against another (para. 28, per Foster & Co.). For a business reader that's the most practical lesson in the case, and it fits the figure's "the AI can't answer for it" better than the bereavement-exception link does.
