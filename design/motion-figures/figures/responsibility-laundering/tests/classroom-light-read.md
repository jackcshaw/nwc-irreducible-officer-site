## Higher-education instructor (`cast=classroom`)

1. **The failure, in my words.** A student signs an AI-drafted recommendation. Asked why they relied on it, all they can do is repeat the AI's number. They've left the reasoning with the AI, so the decision feels partly the AI's, but they answer for all of it.
2. **What separates them.** Before signing, the second student wrote a private note on what the 97% rests on. Asked why, they can name the basis, why they accepted it, and the test they didn't run. The first can only say "The analysis estimated 97%. It seemed solid." Both say "I did. I signed it."
3. **Cheating, or "don't use AI"?** "Don't use AI": no. The second student used the same AI, signed the same text, and is the good example. Cheating: the setup is explicit ("The seminar allows AI, and both say they used it", and both copies say "drafted with AI"). Still, two word-for-word identical papers would get a similarity or collusion check in any course I've taught. And "sign it as written" (handing in unedited AI text) goes past what many course AI policies allow. A colleague's first reaction may be "that's an integrity case", not "that's a reasoning gap."
4. **Workplace words.** None on stage or in the captions. "Company", "employees" and "liable" turn up only in the *Moffatt* material. One small thing: "Faculty ask each student" uses a collective noun for what is clearly one instructor in one seminar, so it reads like a panel or an oral exam. "The instructor asks" is what an HE reader would say.
5. **As a class assignment.** Yes. A business or information-systems case asking "Should the support chatbot answer refund questions?" followed by a cold call or a short viva ("Why did you rely on it?") is ordinary case-method teaching. One gap: where does "The analysis estimates 97%" come from, the case data or the AI? The note ("Rests on last quarter's questions") implies a data set in the case packet, but the page never says so.
6. **Legal liability, or overstating *Moffatt*?** There's no suggestion that students face legal liability. The page says so outright ("answer for it means to faculty, for the work they submit… no claim about anyone's personal legal liability") and also says the case "says nothing about students signing a recommendation for a class." Two small overreaches remain. (a) "*Moffatt* shows the same move at the scale of an organization" goes further than the evidence. Air Canada disowned its chatbot. The first student owns the call and only hands off the reason, so this is closer to a cousin of the same move. (b) The intro says "its chatbot's answers" and the stage says "its chatbot's answer".
7. **Can each caption be read and each movement followed?** Yes, at both sizes. There are three tight spots. The red bracket on the second student's answer appears about 80 s in, roughly 2–3 s before beat 8 starts (it stays on screen, so this is minor). The first student's dot becoming "whole again" as the weight lands is too subtle to notice at 1280 and invisible at 390. And the opening is about 7 s of nearly empty stage.

**My one-sentence point (HE):** Signing an AI recommendation makes it yours, and the test is whether you can say what it rests on, not just repeat the AI's number.

## High-school teacher (`cast=high-school`)

1. **The failure, in my words.** A student hands in the AI's recommendation with their name on it, and when the teacher asks why, they just repeat what the AI said, as if "the AI said so" covers them. It doesn't.
2. **What separates them.** Same as HE. Only the second student checked what the number was based on, and only they can explain it, including what they didn't check.
3. **Cheating, or "don't use AI"?** Not "don't use AI", for the same reason as above. The integrity risk is higher here, though. In most high schools, two identical papers would be treated as copying, and many policies that allow AI still expect students to write in their own words. "The class allows AI" doesn't settle whether this class allows handing in the AI's text verbatim.
4. **Workplace words.** No role words. "Teacher" and "Teacher's check" are used throughout, and there are no faculty, analysts, managers or teams. The vocabulary still leans toward business or product jargon a teen would need unpacked: "go live", "97% accuracy", "last quarter's questions", "Recent: similar", "who made this call", and above all "I didn't test exceptions". The second student's whole advantage depends on that word, and it's never explained. It is also the link to *Moffatt*, which turned on a policy exception.
5. **As a class assignment.** Plausible in a business, marketing, economics or CTE elective, less so in a general class. "If it misleads a customer, who made this call?" works as role-play. "I signed it" is a little formal for students who "put their name on it", but it's clear.
6. **Legal liability, or overstating *Moffatt*?** No suggestion of liability. The disclaimer is adapted ("to the teacher, for the work they submit"). The same "same move" stretch applies. In addition, the *Moffatt* section (six paragraphs, bereavement fares, damages) is long and adult next to a high-school case. And the stage source line "a company was held responsible for its chatbot's answer" sits in the final frame beside the "Answerable" weights with no hedge, so a quick viewer could map "held responsible" onto the students.
7. **Can each caption be read and each movement followed?** Yes. The timing is identical to HE. At 390 the "The teacher doesn't see this" and "What the teacher sees" labels are squeezed into two cramped lines above the frame, but they can be read.

**My one-sentence point (HS):** Using the AI is fine, but if you can't explain why you trusted its answer, you haven't done the thinking that your name on the work claims.

## Top three problems

1. **Identical copies and "signed as written" invite an academic-integrity reading,** most strongly in high school. Identical papers in a class are a red flag, and the figure depends on them. It needs one line saying the copies are identical by design, so the comparison is about reasoning, not text (or else the policy should explicitly allow submitting AI drafts with disclosure).
2. **High-school vocabulary.** "Go live", "last quarter's questions", "Recent: similar", and especially "test exceptions" carry the argument but aren't explained for teens. A gloss or a plainer phrase would fix it (for example, "I didn't check unusual refund requests").
3. **The *Moffatt* framing goes slightly too far.** "Shows the same move" fits Air Canada (disowning the chatbot) better than the first student (owning the call, outsourcing the reason). The bare stage line beside the "Answerable" weights has no hedge, and "answer" vs "answers" is inconsistent. The prose hedges well. The stage line doesn't.

Lower: "Faculty ask" (plural) for one HE instructor. The "whole again" dot is too subtle. The red bracket arrives late in beat 7. The opening is ~7 s with little on stage. At 1:39 it's long for class time.
