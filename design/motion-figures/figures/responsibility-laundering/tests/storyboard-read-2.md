# Responsibility laundering: storyboard cold read, round 2

October 28, 2026 · read from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` (Intent removed). I hadn't read the essay, the plan, or round 1 before writing this. The "After the read" section at the end was added once the report was written.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · The task (0–7 s).** A card reading "EXAMPLE TASK: Recommend when to evacuate a port city" appears, followed by two red dots labelled First student and Second student. Clear, and the scene is set before anything happens. One small snag: "Two students have to make the same recommendation" sounds like they're required to reach the same *conclusion*, not that they share a task. Since they do end up signing the same thing, it reads like a spoiler. "Two students get the same task" would avoid that.

**Beat 2 · Same prompt, same draft (7–18.7 s).** The dots pulse, a sheet appears, and two grey sentences write in on a beige band. The key names the band "WRITTEN BY AI". The sheet splits into two copies, each tagged "DRAFTED WITH AI". I understood it: one AI output, two copies, AI use disclosed, so the figure isn't about hiding AI use. On screen there's no AI and no prompt, so the caption does that work, and that's fine. I tripped on "ships can **take everyone out**". In a war-college setting "take out" means destroy, and my first parse was the wrong one before the evacuation context fixed it. The Notice says both sentences "name no one". As a viewer I wouldn't have noticed: nothing in the caption or the picture points at it.

**Beat 3 · Both read it closely (18.7–25.9 s).** Both dots move onto their copies and track the lines together. Clear: same care, same pace. Nothing shows that they "agree", but the caption covers it.

**Beat 4 · First signs, second asks first (25.9–35.4 s).** The first dot signs with a red squiggle, and the key adds red "A STUDENT'S DECISION". The second dot pauses while red italic writes under its copy: "Rests on an open port." / "Harbor reports: normal." Then it signs and the note fades. I understood that this is the one real difference. Three problems:
- **Too fast.** The note writes at about 4 words a second and is complete at 32.6. The dot then moves (32.7) and signs (33.1), and the note starts fading at 33.5. It's fully on screen for about 0.9 s, and the moving dot pulls my eye away during that time. This is the only evidence that the second student thought *before* signing, so it's the line I most need to catch.
- **The fade means nothing to me.** Nothing on screen says "not handed in". Because the fade lands exactly as they sign, I read it as the student dropping or resolving the concern, not as private thinking that never reaches faculty.
- **Red italic under a student's paper looks like a teacher's red pen.** As an educator, my first reflex was that faculty were marking the paper. The caption fixes this, but only if I'm reading the caption and not the picture.

Also, the caption says the second student "asks what the estimate rests on", but the note shows them *checking* something too ("Harbor reports: normal"). That's more than asking. It's fine, but the caption undersells it.

**Beat 5 · Who made the call (35.4–49.0 s).** Question 1 appears in the centre: "Suppose the port closes. Who made the call to wait?" First: "I signed it. But it was the AI's analysis." Second: "I did. I chose to rely on the estimate." Clear and well paced. Faculty never appear on screen, but the caption ("Faculty see only the papers") carries that. The first student's line is blunt blame-shifting. It's plausible, but it leans toward a foil.

**Beat 6 · Then they ask why (49.0–62.3 s).** "Why did you rely on the estimate?" First: "It was the AI's estimate. It seemed solid." Second: "It assumes an open port. Harbor reports looked normal, so I accepted it. I didn't check for closures." This is the moment the difference becomes audible, and it worked for me. Then the skeptic in me asked: the scenario is the port closing, and the second student admits they didn't check for closures. So both made the same mistake, and the second one just describes it better? The figure says elsewhere that "whether relying was justified is a separate check", but nothing on screen says so. A teacher could leave thinking the figure rewards fluent self-reporting.

**Beat 7 · The turn (62.3–71.6 s).** The first panel pulses. The first student's dot splits, and half of it moves up beside "The analysis estimates". The second panel pulses, and a red bracket under the second copy writes in "I relied on: the AI's estimate, assuming an open port." I read the split as "the first student put half the call on the AI". Good, that's the strongest image in the figure. But:
- **What the bracket is isn't clear.** It's red, italic, first person, and sits in the same strip under the second paper where the private note was. I read it as something the second student wrote and attached to the paper. That contradicts "identical papers" and "faculty see only the papers".
- Both students' red marks now point at the AI's estimate: a half dot beside it on the left, "I relied on: the AI's estimate" on the right. The difference rests on a dot of radius 5 against two of radius 3.5. Easy to miss.
- The bracket's 10 words write in 1.2 s, with 2.0 s to hold before beat 8 starts pulling the eye. It stays on screen, so I can recover it later, but not in this beat.
- The caption isn't parallel: "leans on the AI" is about agency, "knows what it rested on" is about knowledge, and "it" could mean the call or the estimate. The picture (whole dot, "I relied on") is about ownership, so the caption and picture point at slightly different lessons.

**Beat 8 · Suppose it proves wrong (71.6–80.6 s).** Navy weights labelled "ANSWERS / FOR IT" hang, then fall down both margins. As each passes, a dashed outline draws round "The analysis estimates". On the left, the weight sweeps up the half dot and the halves rejoin under it. Both weights come to rest on the dots, and the key adds "CAN'T ANSWER FOR IT". I got the main idea: the AI can't carry it, both students do. Issues:
- **"ANSWERS FOR IT" reads as a noun phrase.** This figure is full of *answers* (four speech panels), so a block labelled "ANSWERS FOR IT" first read to me as "the answers about it" falling onto the students. "Answerable", "Accountable" or "Owns the outcome" wouldn't collide.
- **A dashed outline round a claim reads as "flagged, questionable".** Without the key I'd take it as "this estimate is the weak point", which is an evidence lesson, not an accountability one. And it only circles "The analysis estimates", not "It is recommended", though the AI wrote both. Why can only the estimate not answer for it?
- **Too much at once.** The fall, the outline on both copies, the pick-up of the half dot and the rejoin all happen in about 2 s across two columns. I can watch one column. I'd probably catch the left one and miss that the right outline drew at all. The pick-up, which is the payoff of beat 7, is easy to miss because the weight is "gathering speed" when it happens.

**Beat 9 · Faculty check (80.6–89.8 s).** "FACULTY CHECK: Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?", then "Name one way it could prove wrong. Ask what the call rested on, and who chose to rely on it." Paced well and actionable. But the headline question is the one the figure showed *doesn't* separate the students. Asked who owns it, the first student already said "I signed it". The question that separated them was "Why did you rely on the estimate?", and the move line swaps that for "what the call rested on", which isn't the question faculty actually asked in beat 6.

## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same AI-drafted recommendation and both stay fully accountable for it, but only the one who worked out what it rested on before signing can explain why they relied on it, and since the papers look identical, faculty have to ask.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Say this goes wrong because the port closes. Why did you rely on the AI's estimate? What did it assume, why did you accept that, and what didn't you check?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Partly. It carries "the AI can't answer for this, both students will": two identical tagged papers, a dashed outline on the AI's phrase, a weight on each student. It does *not* carry the turn. The split dot has rejoined, the private note is gone, and both dots look the same. The only visible difference left between the students is the red bracket under the right-hand paper, plus four speech panels I'd have to read closely. On the still alone, I'd conclude the second student attached a reliance statement to their paper and that this is what made them better. So the lesson becomes "make students attach a reliance note", which the page copy itself says isn't enough (a signed responsibility statement "doesn't show that the learner can defend the judgment"). It's also very text-heavy for a social poster. At card size the speech panels, which carry the difference, won't be legible.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Ambiguous visual: the bracket reads as handed-in work, and the final frame relies on it.** It's red, first person ("I relied on"), and in the same strip under the paper where the student's private note sat. A viewer will take it as part of the second student's submission. That undercuts "faculty see only the papers" and suggests the wrong fix. Once the halves rejoin in beat 8, it's the only visible difference in the still. Consider making it plainly a viewer's annotation tied to the speech panel (not the paper), or keeping some trace of the split in the final frame.
2. **Too fast: the beat 4 note.** It's complete and fully opaque for about 0.9 s while the dot moves and signs. Its fade is unexplained and reads as the concern being dropped. This is the evidence that the second answer wasn't made up afterwards, so it needs a real hold (at least 2.5–3 s on the full note before the dot moves) and a visible reason it vanishes.
3. **The caption doesn't match the picture's evidence: the headline check is the weaker question.** "Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?" gets "I do" from both students. The figure shows the first student claiming the signature. What separated them was "Why did you rely on the estimate?" The move line says "Ask what the call rested on", not "why they relied on it". I'd put the "why" question in the check itself.
4. **Ambiguous label: "ANSWERS FOR IT" on the weight.** In a figure full of spoken answers, it parses as a noun ("answers for it"). The key's "CAN'T ANSWER FOR IT" doesn't fully fix that.
5. **Ambiguous visual: the dashed outline reads as "this claim is questionable".** That's an evidence-quality reading, not "can't be held accountable". It also circles only the estimate, not the AI-written recommendation sentence.
6. **The mechanism isn't shown (fairness to the first student).** The Notice in beat 2 makes the agentless wording ("The analysis estimates", "It is recommended") the mechanism. But the first student's answers don't hide behind that wording. They name the AI outright, twice. That's blame-shifting more than laundering through language, and it makes the first student a slightly easy foil. A first answer that echoes the draft's voice ("The analysis showed ships could take everyone, so waiting was recommended") would show the wording doing the work.
7. **A factual claim I'd want qualified.** Page copy: "A student can't give that answer without having asked the question before signing." Students can rationalise afterwards, or ask an AI for a rationale. This is the crux of whether the oral account is evidence, and it's stated as certain. Relatedly, the second student also didn't check closures. Without the "separate check" line *on screen*, a teacher may feel the figure rewards articulate self-report.
8. **Ambiguous visual: red for student work.** Red italic text under a student paper is how an educator reads teacher marking. It affects the note most of all.
9. **Wording: "take everyone out."** In military usage "take out" means destroy. "Carry everyone out" or "evacuate everyone" would avoid the misparse.
10. **Too much at once in beat 8.** The fall, two outlines, the half dot's pick-up and the rejoin all land in about 2 s across two columns. The pick-up, which pays off the turn, is easy to miss.
11. **Caption 7 isn't parallel** ("leans on the AI" / "knows what it rested on"), and "it" is ambiguous. The picture is about ownership, the caption about knowledge.
12. **Setup copy grammar.** "the model's recommendation makes agency feel distributed or easier to defend": agency isn't what becomes easier to defend, the recommendation is.
13. **Minor.** "Two students have to make the same recommendation" pre-empts the outcome. The kicker says "Failure mode 7 of 7" while the header says figure 7 of 8, so check whether that's intended. The AI draft is unusually bare: two flat sentences with no stated assumptions, where many current AI drafts would at least list one. That's fine for a constructed example, but a teacher might think the AI has been made to look worse than it typically is.

What I did *not* find: no numbers are shown, AI use is disclosed on both papers (so this isn't a cheating story), and both students are presented as honest. That's fair.
