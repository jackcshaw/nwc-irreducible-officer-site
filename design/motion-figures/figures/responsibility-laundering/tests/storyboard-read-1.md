# Responsibility laundering: storyboard cold read, round 1

Read from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed). I read it as an educator who hasn't read the essay. I didn't open the full storyboard, the plan, the author brief, the essay or any other figure before writing this. An after-read fact check is at the end.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · The task (0–6.5 s).** A task card ("Example task: recommend when to evacuate civilians from a port city") and a red dot labelled "Student." Clear: someone has to make a high-stakes call. Nothing confusing. The hold (4.1 s) is generous.

**Beat 2 · The AI drafts (6.5–19.5 s).** A big sheet tagged "AI'S DRAFT" fills one grey sentence at a time, and the key names grey "WRITTEN BY AI." I read three plain sentences and understood "the AI wrote a confident recommendation." I did **not** notice on my own that each sentence opens by naming someone other than the student as the decider ("The analysis indicates…", "The model's estimate…", "It is recommended…"). The Notice expects me to see that, but nothing on screen points at the wording. The caption only says "clear recommendation." One pause: the AI writes "The model's estimate" in the third person. I wasn't sure whether "the model" was the AI that wrote the draft or a separate forecasting model the AI was quoting.

**Beat 3 · The student signs (19.5–27.4 s).** The dot moves along each line, then signs in red, and the key adds red "A STUDENT'S OWN WORK." The tag becomes "STUDENT'S RECOMMENDATION · DRAFTED WITH AI." I got it: the student agreed and signed without changing anything. Two snags:
- The caption says "reads it closely," but the dot covers five lines in 3 seconds, about 10 words a second. On screen that looks like skimming. The caption and the motion disagree on the one thing this beat is meant to establish.
- "A STUDENT'S OWN WORK" lands next to a paper where every word is the AI's and only the squiggle is the student's. For a teacher, "own work" is academic-integrity language ("is this your own work?"). It read to me as ironic or accusatory, not as "what the student decided."

**Beat 4 · A second student signs (27.4–38.5 s).** The first sheet slides left. A second dot appears and gets "the same draft" with the tag "AI'S DRAFT," reads it at the same pace and signs. I understood it as a controlled comparison: same paper, same care. But I didn't see how the second student got the identical draft. Did they ask the AI separately and get word-for-word the same output? Were they handed the first student's draft? The scene doesn't say. Eleven seconds of the same animation again felt a little long, though I can see why it's there.

**Beat 5 · Two identical papers (38.5–44.5 s).** "FACULTY RECEIVE BOTH PAPERS," and navy hairlines run under every line of both. Taken literally, my first reaction as a teacher was "two students handed in the same paper, so that's a copying case." That instinct competes with the figure's point, and nothing on screen defuses it. The hairlines read as "underlined" more than "matching," but the caption says "identical," so I got it.

**Beat 6 · Faculty ask (44.5–64.1 s).** The question appears ("Suppose the port closes on day one. Who made the call to wait?"), then both answers type in under the papers. This is the strongest beat. The question is concrete and the two answers really do differ. The caption's "Faculty have only the papers" was slightly odd: only the papers instead of what? I took it to mean "the papers can't tell them anything." Pacing felt fine. The typing is slower than I read, and both answers stay up. My honest reaction to the answers: the first student says "I signed it," which owns the signature, then gives the basis. That sounds a lot like an officer citing an intelligence estimate. The second says the same facts in the first person and adds "I didn't check the port's status." The difference I can hear is the word "But" and the phrase "I chose to."

**Beat 7 · The turn (64.1–73.4 s).** Caption: "The first answer hands half the call to the model. The second keeps it." On the left, the dot splits and a small half moves up beside "The model's estimate:" with a faint red underline. On the right, the dot stays whole and a **solid** red line runs from it up to and under the same phrase. This is where I got confused:
- Both sheets now show red running from the student to "The model's estimate." The right-hand one is the stronger mark (solid line, full dot), so my eye read the second student as tied *more* firmly to the model. That's the opposite of "keeps it."
- A red underline under a phrase is a teacher's correction mark. My reflex reading was "this phrase is wrong" on both papers.
- Telling a 3.5-radius half dot from a 5-radius whole dot at this scale is a subtle comparison. The beat holds only 1.8 s after the last mark before the caption changes, and this is the beat that carries the idea.

**Beat 8 · Suppose it proves wrong (73.4–82.9 s).** Two navy weights labelled "ANSWERS FOR IT" hang, then fall together. The weight was easy to read: a burden landing on each student. I got "both students end up carrying it." But in 1.8 s of falling, the left sheet has a weight picking up the half dot, the hairline pulling back and a dashed outline drawing. Meanwhile the right sheet's weight falls past the red line, and the key adds a third swatch at the bottom of the stage. That's three places to look at once. I'd have watched the left sheet and missed the key. The "CAN'T ANSWER FOR IT" outline appears **only** on the left paper. The caption says flatly "The model can't answer for it," so I wondered whether the model somehow *can* answer for it on the right paper.

**Beat 9 · Faculty check (82.9–89.9 s).** "Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?" then "Name one way it could prove wrong. Ask who made that call." The second line is the useful one: a move I could make on Monday. The first line has an obvious answer (whoever signed it), so on its own it reads as rhetorical. The figure's real distinction is in the *account* each student gives, and the check question doesn't ask about that.

## 2. The point (my words)

You can't tell from two identical, signed AI-drafted papers whether a student owns the call; you find out by naming how it could go wrong and asking who decided, because the student answers for it either way and the AI never does.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"If this goes wrong, who made the call, and what did you rely on that you didn't check?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It carries "both students answer for it." Two identical papers, a weight resting on each student's dot, and the two answers are side by side. With the speech panels in view, a careful reader can compare the answers and see the difference. The marks that are meant to show the difference work less well. The split has healed, so the left dot is whole again. What's left is a dashed outline on the left and a red line on the right, and I can only decode those through the key. Read through the key, the right sheet says "The model's estimate" is "A STUDENT'S OWN WORK," which muddles the idea. The frame is also dense: a task card, two three-row sheets, tags, signatures, weights, an outline, a line, a centre question, two speech panels, a three-item key and a three-line caption band. Read alone, it says to me: "Both signers carry the weight. The model's phrase is marked as unable to carry it. Ask who made the call." That's close to the point. The "only one gave an account" half rests entirely on reading the speech panels.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Ambiguous visual, caption mismatch (beat 7, final frame).** The second student's solid red line to "The model's estimate:" reads as a *stronger* tie to the model than the first student's faint half-dot. That contradicts "The second keeps it." Red underlines also read as a teacher's correction marks. Find a mark for "I chose to rely on this" that doesn't look like pointing at the model or grading the phrase, or make the left/right contrast much starker than half dot against whole dot.
2. **Fairness to students: the difference is mostly phrasing.** Both students relied on the estimate and neither checked the port. The first opens with "I signed it" and cites the basis, which is normal staff practice with an intelligence estimate. On screen, the second is rewarded mainly for "I chose to" plus a confession. A teacher primed by this figure could start grading wording, and students will quickly learn to say "I chose to rely on…" without anything changing. The page copy hedges this ("whether relying… was justified is a separate check"), but the stage doesn't. It would help to show what the second answer *lets faculty do next* (for example, follow up with "why didn't you check?"), so the account looks like something faculty can question further, not a better form of words.
3. **Two identical papers read as misconduct.** To an educator, two students submitting the same paper triggers "copying" before anything else. How the second student got the same draft isn't shown. The setup needs a line or picture that makes the duplication a deliberate comparison, or a reason it happened, for example "the same AI gave both students the same draft."
4. **Dashed "CAN'T ANSWER FOR IT" only on the first paper.** The caption states it generally, but the mark appears on one side, which implies the model's role differs between papers. Either mark both, or make clear the outline marks *where the first student tried to put the call*.
5. **Key wording "A STUDENT'S OWN WORK."** It conflicts with the colour rule ("what a student decided") and carries academic-integrity weight. Next to a fully AI-written page, and on the right-hand reliance line, it makes the wrong statement. Something like "A STUDENT'S DECISION" would match the grammar.
6. **"Reads it closely" vs. a 3-second skim (beats 3–4).** 0.6 s a line is about 10 words a second. If "both read carefully" is what rules out carelessness, the motion should look careful: slower, or a pause per sentence.
7. **Beat 8 is too busy.** The half-dot pickup, the hairline retracting, the outline drawing, the second weight falling and the key swatch all happen inside about 2 s across three locations. Stagger them, or let the key swatch arrive after the weights settle.
8. **Beat 7 hold is short for the turn.** 1.8 s after the second mark, on a subtle two-sheet comparison, before the caption moves on. This beat deserves the longest hold, not one of the shortest.
9. **The wording mechanism is never pointed at.** The draft's impersonal phrasing is presumably where the "laundering" happens, and the page setup quotes "the model's language distributes agency." But on screen I'm never shown that the sentences name someone other than the student as the decider. I only got there from the Notice text.
10. **"The model" is ambiguous.** An AI drafting "The model's estimate" reads as quoting a separate forecasting model. Then it isn't clear whether "the model" in the answers is the AI that drafted, or that upstream model.
11. **The check's first line is rhetorical.** "Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?" has an obvious answer. The useful diagnostic is the second line. A check about the account ("Can the student say what they relied on and what they didn't check?") would fit the figure better.
12. **Minor.** Beat 4's eleven seconds of repeated reading feels slightly long. The beat-6 caption "Faculty have only the papers" took a second to parse. Underlines mean "matches" in beat 5 and "relies on" in beat 7, two meanings for one mark within 25 seconds.

**Factual claims I'd want sourced:** none on stage. The only number, "day one," is constructed and labelled as an example. On the page: the two block quotes (Section II, Section VII), the Section VII phrases "cannot own what follows" and "apparent weight of objectivity", and the claim that "the companion's failure-mode lab makes a related point about a signed statement of responsibility." See the fact check below.
