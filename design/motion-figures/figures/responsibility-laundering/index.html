Responsibility laundering

Suppose two students give an AI the same prompt, get the same draft, and sign it as written. The Irreducible Officer calls the risk responsibility laundering: a recommendation becomes easier to defend because the model generated it, or easier to soften because the model's language distributes agency. The figure runs about a minute and a half. The task, the draft and both students are a constructed example.

Faculty check

Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?

Name one way it could prove wrong. Ask why they relied on it.

Both students signed the same paper. If it proves wrong, both answer for it, and the AI can't. Asked who owns it, both would say they do. Asked why they relied on the AI's estimate, the first student says it seemed solid. The second says what the estimate assumed, why they accepted that, and the check they should have made. Both answer for the call. Only the second can say what it rested on. That answer is hard to give without having asked the question before signing, and the second student's note shows they did. Section VII of the essay asks students to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. The companion's failure-mode lab makes a related point about a signed statement of responsibility: it can add evidence, but it doesn't show that the learner can defend the judgment. The lab also notes the cost: with many learners, faculty have to sample rather than question everyone. Whether relying on the estimate was justified is a separate check, covered by uncalibrated reliance.

From the essay

Responsibility laundering is the final danger. A recommendation becomes easier to defend because the model generated it, or easier to soften because the model's language distributes agency. The analysis lands with the apparent weight of objectivity. But AI does not become accountable for the recommendation. The human remains responsible for the final judgment. When the recommendation proves wrong, shaped by assumptions no one examined and optimizing toward a target no one explicitly chose, the question of who chose the frame does not have a satisfying answer.

The Irreducible Officer, Section II, What the Problem Looks Like

… A human recommendation brings context with it. Experience, incentives, reputation, and the way a person answers when pressed all travel with the recommendation. A commander can weigh those signals. A model carries none of them and can still sound equally confident. It can support a judgment, but it cannot provide the human presence that makes accountability clear to subordinates, partners, or commanders who will live with the result.

First-person ownership matters in the classroom. The student should be able to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. Students are practicing the accountability structure their professional roles will require.
