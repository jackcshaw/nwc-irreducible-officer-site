# Responsibility laundering: storyboard

Starting point: `concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.7, "Who signs" (8.5 s, not built). This version keeps v0's mechanism: the red dot splits as agency spreads into the AI's wording, and when the recommendation proves wrong a navy weight comes down and the pieces snap back into one dot on the signature line. It applies the lessons of figures 1–5. As a stated supposition, two students give an AI the same prompt and get the same draft. Both read it equally closely and sign it as written, so faculty receive identical papers. The one difference is a habit: before signing, the second student notes what the AI's estimate rests on, in a note they don't hand in. Faculty name one way the call could go wrong, ask who made it, then ask why each student relied on the estimate. Only the second can say. While faculty ask, a frame marks what faculty see and the note row dims. Then the first student's dot halves, and one half travels to the AI's phrase their answers lean on and turns into a hollow dashed grey ring, the reason handed to the AI; a red bracket marks the second student's own reason. A weight labelled "ANSWERABLE" rests on both. The shared runtime and `CONVENTIONS.md` apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** Two students can sign the same AI-drafted recommendation. If it proves wrong, both answer for it; the AI can't. Only one may be able to account for it. Accounting for it means saying why you relied on the AI: what its estimate assumed (an open port), why you accepted that (today's harbor reports looked normal), and the check you should have made, right or wrong. That answer is hard to give without having asked before signing, and the second student's note shows they did. A student who didn't ask can still sincerely say "I signed it", and then lean on the AI's wording: "The analysis said ships could do it." "It was the AI's estimate. It seemed solid." The paper can't show the difference. Faculty find it by naming one way the recommendation could prove wrong and asking why the student relied on it.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "Suppose two students gave an AI the same prompt and got the same draft: the analysis estimates ships can carry everyone out, and it is recommended that civilians wait for ships. Both read it closely and agreed. The first signed. The second first noted what the estimate rested on, an open port, and that the harbor reports looked normal, then signed. That note wasn't handed in, so faculty got two identical papers. They asked: suppose the port closes, who made this call? The first said, 'I signed it. The analysis said ships could do it.' The second said, 'I did. I chose to rely on the estimate.' Then: why did you rely on it? The first said, 'It was the AI's estimate. It seemed solid.' The second said it assumed an open port, today's reports looked normal so they accepted it, and they should have checked for closures. If it proves wrong, the AI can't answer for it and both students will. Only the second can account for the call, gap included; being able to say what it rested on, right or wrong, is different from having been right. So I'd ask who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong: name one way it could go wrong, and ask why they relied on it."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from the site's Core Failure Modes table): *Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?* The final frame adds, directly under it and at the same size, the move the figure makes: *Name one way it could prove wrong. Ask why they relied on it.*

**Essay passage** (Section II, "What the Problem Looks Like"):

> **Responsibility laundering** is the final danger. A recommendation becomes easier to defend because the model generated it, or easier to soften because the model's language distributes agency. The analysis lands with the apparent weight of objectivity. But AI does not become accountable for the recommendation. The human remains responsible for the final judgment. When the recommendation proves wrong, shaped by assumptions no one examined and optimizing toward a target no one explicitly chose, the question of who chose the frame does not have a satisfying answer.

- "In AI-enabled strategic decision games, Andres describes conviction-shaped output. ## 2. Scenario

In every beat the caption changes first (0.3 s fade) and nothing in the picture moves for the first second. A change the caption is about starts only after the caption can be read once, except a lead-in that the caption's first clause describes. Every beat lasts at least (its caption plus the words the viewer must read in it) ÷ 3 + 1.5 s. Two waits are trimmed at the orchestrator's request (noted in the beats): answers type faster than they're read, and the weights hover 2.5 s.

**Faculty check:** Who owns the recommendation if it proves wrong?
**Name one way it could prove wrong. Ask why they relied on it.** (same size)

Both students signed the same paper. If it proves wrong, both answer for it, and the AI can't. Asked who owns it, both would say they do. Asked why they relied on the AI's estimate, the first student says it seemed solid. The second says what the estimate assumed, why they accepted that, and the check they should have made. Both answer for the call. Only the second can say what it rested on. That answer is hard to give without having asked the question before signing, and the second student's note shows they did. Section VII of the essay asks students to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. The companion's failure-mode lab (linked to Practice) makes a related point about a signed statement of responsibility: it can add evidence, but it doesn't show that the learner can defend the judgment. The lab also notes the cost: with many learners, faculty have to sample rather than question everyone. Whether relying on the estimate was justified is a separate check, covered by uncalibrated reliance.

**From the essay** (*The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, What the Problem Looks Like):

> Responsibility laundering is the final danger. A recommendation becomes easier to defend because the model generated it, or easier to soften because the model's language distributes agency. The analysis lands with the apparent weight of objectivity. But AI does not become accountable for the recommendation. The human remains responsible for the final judgment. When the recommendation proves wrong, shaped by assumptions no one examined and optimizing toward a target no one explicitly chose, the question of who chose the frame does not have a satisfying answer.

And from Section VII, Accountability Is Structural:

> … A human recommendation brings context with it. Experience, incentives, reputation, and the way a person answers when pressed all travel with the recommendation. A commander can weigh those signals. A model carries none of them and can still sound equally confident. It can support a judgment, but it cannot provide the human presence that makes accountability clear to subordinates, partners, or commanders who will live with the result.
>
> First-person ownership matters in the classroom. The student should be able to say why they accepted an output, why they rejected one, and why they remain accountable for the recommendation despite the system's contribution. Students are practicing the accountability structure their professional roles will require.
