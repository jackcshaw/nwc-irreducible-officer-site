## 1. Beat by beat

The card isn't easy to read in time, though: caption (11 words) plus card (20) is 31 words in 8.0 s, about 10 s at the figure's own 3 words a second, or 14 s if I also read the four report labels. It stays on screen, so I can catch up, but beat 2's caption takes over at 8.0.

**Beat 2 · 8.0–16.7 · The AI answers both.** Copies of all four reports slide to the centre, two identical answer cards appear, and both get a grey "READY". The caption says "equal confidence". I see "equal", because the cards are identical. I don't really see "confidence". "READY" reads to me like a status flag (the job is done), not like the AI sounding sure. Each answer also opens with "About", which is a hedge. So the confidence lives in the caption, not in the picture. Later the first student says Part 2 "sounded just as sure", and I can't point to anything on the card that sounds sure. Reading load: the answers are fully in at 11.8 s, and the beat ends at 16.7 s. Reading the caption and the two answers (about 31 words) takes me past 18 s, into beat 3's motion. I also noticed the Notice line's "the AI gets every report, including the harbor log". On first viewing I didn't know why that mattered. It pays off in beat 5, and then it makes the AI look careless: it had the closed-dock line and ignored it.

**Beat 3 · 16.7–25.4 · Checks Part 1, uses both.** The dot opens both road reports (I see note bars, not content), a "First student's brief" appears, Part 1 goes in with "✓ CHECKED", and Part 2 goes in with "USED AS IS". The caption matches the picture well. This is the most important moment in the figure, and it's crowded: nine movements in 5.3 s. "USED AS IS" is written at 22.0–22.4, and the reports close and the dot travels home at 22.5. My eye would follow the moving dot away from the tag just as it lands. The hold after it (2.4 s) helps. The tag is also small mono text, the same colour and size as "✓ CHECKED". The difference is in the words only.

During this beat the picture is identical to the first student's, and the caption promises "each", so I spent the hold waiting for the difference. **Beat 5 · 34.1–42.1 · Checks Part 2 and corrects.** The port survey callout says "4 docks, about 5 ships each." The harbor log says "Dock 2: closed for repairs." The answer goes in, and "20" becomes "15" and "4" becomes "3 working", tagged "✓ CHECKED". Very clear, and the arithmetic holds (3 × 5 = 15). It's the most satisfying beat. Two notes. It's 27 words in 8.0 s with a 1.7 s hold, slightly over the 3 words a second rate. And nothing on screen says the harbor log is newer than the survey. "Log" suggests current, but a careful teacher could ask how the student knew which report to believe. The scenario says the survey is "out of date", and the screen doesn't.

**Beat 6 · 42.1–52.8 · Both parts look the same.** Both AI cards pulse identically. Then, after a 3 s gap, a jagged navy line draws between them, with "INSIDE THE FRONTIER" above and "OUTSIDE THE FRONTIER" below. I stumbled on the caption: "From outside, both parts look the same. Researchers call an edge like this a jagged frontier." Within seconds, two different "outsides" are in play: "from outside" (as an onlooker reading the output) and "outside the frontier" (the region where the AI does worse). On first read I parsed "From outside" as "from outside the frontier" and wasn't sure what that meant. Bigger issue: the line is drawn after I've already watched the second student find the error, and it's drawn exactly around the part that turned out wrong. Nothing tells me why ships by sea falls outside and trucks by road inside. My takeaway was "the frontier is wherever the AI happened to be wrong", which is hindsight. A teacher can't use that to decide anything beforehand. The essay's sentence ("tasks that look similar may differ significantly in whether AI helps or hurts") is about kinds of tasks. What I watched was one missed line in one report. I could see a hidden difference if I looked for it: the road reports presumably agreed, while the two port reports disagreed. The figure never names it. The teeth reaching "within 8 units" of each card didn't register for me as meaning "both are near the edge". At that scale the line just looks close to both cards.

**Beat 7 · 52.8–62.1 · The study.** The scene dims, and a panel shows right answers on a task chosen to fall outside the frontier: without AI 84.5%, with AI (two groups) 60% and 70%, with the source line. "Chosen to fall outside" is honest wording, and I liked "This is a real study of consultants, not the port task" in the Notice. The bar heights differ: the without-AI bar is 20 high and the AI bars 12. Thinner bars look weaker even when their length is honest. **Beat 8 · 62.1–68.8 · Rated higher.** A bracket beside the two AI bars reads "Rated higher in quality". The caption names the comparison ("Graders still rated the AI users' recommendations higher"). **Beat 9 · 68.8–82.7 · Faculty ask.** The panel goes, "FACULTY POINT TO PART 2 / AND ASK EACH STUDENT" appears, and Part 2 is outlined in both briefs. The two answers type out at reading pace. Clear and well paced. Two questions came up. Why did faculty pick Part 2? It looks as if faculty already know where the error is, which a real instructor usually doesn't. Beat 10's "Point to one part" implies any part works, but this beat shows a convenient choice. And the first student's line, "This sounded just as sure", hands them a weak, tone-based reason. A real student who spot-checked Part 1 might well say "I spot-checked and it held up", which is a defensible sampling habit. The figure calls the impulse "reasonable" in beat 3, then writes the student's defence to sound naive.

**Beat 10 · 82.7–89.4 · The check.** "FACULTY CHECK / What evidence justified relying on the system here?", then "Point to one part. Was the evidence about that part?" at the same size. The second line is the best thing in the figure. It's concrete, and a teacher could say it tomorrow. "The system" is the one place the figure stops saying "the AI". That's minor, but I noticed it.

Captions never changed before I could read them. The one-second still at the start of each beat works on paper. Where I was rushed, it was by in-picture text (beats 1, 2–3, 5), not by captions.

## 2. The point

An answer that checks out on one part of a task tells you nothing about a look-alike part, so relying on each part needs evidence about that part. Faculty can surface the difference by pointing at a part and asking what justified relying on it.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Put your finger on this part of your work. What did you check that shows the AI got *this* part right, as opposed to some other part?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. Reading left to right, the still tells the story. Both briefs show Part 1 "✓ CHECKED". The left Part 2 says "USED AS IS" and 20, and the right Part 2 is struck through to 15, "3 working", with the harbor log's "Dock 2: closed for repairs" open above. The two speech panels give the contrast in the students' own words. The two lines under the picture tell me what to ask and what to listen for. What the still says to me: same AI answers, same first check, and only one student checked the part they actually relied on, which shows when you ask.

What it doesn't carry. The study (84.5 / 60 / 70, rated higher) is absent, so a reduced-motion reader gets the research only from the beat list. Without the animation, the frontier line reads like a decorative divider with labels. And the still is dense: task card, four reports, two callouts, three columns, a frontier, a header, two speech panels and a three-line caption. On a first look it takes a while to find where to start.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **"From outside" clashes with "outside the frontier" (confusion, caption).** Beat 6's caption uses "outside" in two senses within one beat, and the first sense is never explained. I misread it on first pass. A phrase like "On the surface, both parts look the same" or "By their look, the two answers can't be told apart" would remove the clash.
2. **The frontier looks defined by the error, after the fact (ambiguous visual, concept).** The line is drawn after the reveal, around exactly the part that went wrong, and nothing says what made Part 2 a different kind of task. A teacher may conclude the frontier is "wherever the AI slipped this time", known only by checking, or that one missed line in a report *is* the jagged frontier. The study, by contrast, is about task types. One clause would fix this, in the Notice, the caption or the setup, naming the hidden difference: for example, that Part 2 needed two reports reconciled and Part 1 didn't. That keeps "not visible from the output" true while making the frontier about the task, not the outcome.
3. **The reliance tags and grey AI text sit on the briefs, which undercuts "faculty have to ask" (ambiguous visual).** If the first brief says "USED AS IS" in the student's own red, faculty can read it, and beat 9's question and beat 8's "graders can't tell" both lose force. It also explains why faculty pick Part 2: the tag tells them. If the tags and grey are the viewer's x-ray view, say so once, for example in the setup or the beat 3 Notice ("only we can see these tags"), or move them to the sheet's margin, outside the brief.
4. **Too fast in the picture, and the key moment is crowded (too fast).** By the figure's own 3 words a second rate: beat 1 is 31 words in 8.0 s (10.3 s needed, 14 s with the report labels); beats 2–3 are 63 words in 17.4 s (21 s); beat 5 is 27 words in 8.0 s (9 s). Beat 3 packs nine movements into 5.3 s, and "USED AS IS" (22.0–22.4) is immediately followed by the reports closing and the dot travelling home (22.5–23.0), so the eye follows the dot away from the figure's crux. Start the return 0.5–1 s later, or give "USED AS IS" a beat to itself.
5. **The "equal confidence" caption doesn't match the picture.** The only confidence cue is "READY", which reads as a status, and both answers open with "About", a hedge. The first student's "This sounded just as sure" then refers to a tone I never saw. Either give the cards a visible sure marker, or reword the caption and the speech around "the same form, the same tag".
6. **Mono-12 text vanishes below an 880 px stage (mechanical risk).** Under the 11 px hide rule, 12-unit text disappears once the stage is narrower than 880 px (Serif 15 goes below 704 px). That covers every reliance tag, "READY", the PART labels, the brief labels and the frontier labels. If the desktop page renders the stage narrower than 880 px, for example in a 720–800 px column or on a small laptop, the "✓ CHECKED" vs "USED AS IS" contrast disappears on desktop, not just on phones. Confirm the desktop stage width.
7. **Fairness to the first student and to the AI.** "This sounded just as sure" writes the first student's defence as tone-chasing, when spot-check-and-extrapolate is a reasonable habit the figure itself calls "the reasonable impulse". A line closer to what a real student says ("Part 1 checked out, so I trusted the rest") would be fairer and still fail the check. On the AI side, the figure stresses that the AI had the harbor log and still said 4 docks with "READY". That's plausible, but with no explanation a viewer may generalise to "AI ignores documents", not "AI is uneven across task types" (ties to problem 2).
8. **Faculty pointing to Part 2 looks like foreknowledge.** Beat 9 shows faculty choosing the one part that went wrong. A teacher may infer that the move needs prior knowledge of the error. Beat 10's "Point to one part" helps. A word in beat 9 ("faculty pick a part: Part 2") would make it read as a choice.
9. **Smaller wording points.** "Checks each against its own reports" (beat 4): "its" can be read as the AI's. The first dot is "Student" and the second "Second student". The screen never says the harbor log is newer than the survey. "The system" in the check, after "the AI" everywhere else.
10. **Evidence panel styling.** The without-AI bar is 20 high, the AI bars 12. The lengths are honest, but the thinner bars carry less visual weight. Same height for all three would be neutral. The prose "about 84.5%" pairs "about" with a precise decimal.
11. **Phone width (low, given desktop-first).** At 390 px, the stage shows only shapes: unlabelled cards, unlabelled bars and strikes with no numbers. The beat list carries everything, but the evidence panel without labels is a chart that says nothing on its own.
12. **Factual claims to source.** 84.5%, 60% and 70%. "19 percentage points less likely". AI users' recommendations rated higher in quality on the outside-frontier task "regardless of the correctness of their answer". "BCG consultants". "Field experiment". "HBS working paper 24-013". Checked in section 6.

Not a viewer issue, but noted: the header says "Motion figure 4 of 8" and the kicker "Failure mode 4 of 7". Both may be true. Worth confirming the kicker numbering.
