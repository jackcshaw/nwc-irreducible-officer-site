## 2. The point, in my words

When an AI hands you a plan in several parts, all equally confident, checking one part tells you nothing about the others. Check each part against evidence about that part, especially the parts that depend on information only you have and didn't give it.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"Take each part of this plan in turn. What did you check it against, and did the AI have the information that part depends on?" (For a nonprofit: swap in the grant officer or board member asking about each line of a program plan.)

It carries the idea. Without the animation I can see the two plans side by side, identical except that the second owner crossed out "cut prices 20%" for "same-day service". I can see that the AI's Part 2 came "from their flyer" and sits "outside the frontier". The sales records say "Mostly same-day" and are marked "not given to AI". What I'd still miss without the animation is *why* the first owner trusted Part 2. Their answer covers it ("Part 1 checked out, so I trusted the rest"), so even that survives.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The "right answer" for Part 2 is contestable to a business reader.** The task is to *win back* customers who left for a rival 20% cheaper. "Our sales: mostly same-day" tells me what my *remaining* customers buy, not why the lost ones left. Arguably the lost ones are the price-sensitive, non-urgent jobs. So "They come for speed" → "same-day service" isn't clearly better than a price response, and a sharp owner or lender would say so. The figure needs Part 2's evidence to settle the question. As drawn, it nudges the reader to argue with the example rather than take the lesson. Changing the records line to something about the lost customers (for example "Lost customers: mostly rush jobs", or "Most jobs: same-day, few price complaints") would close the gap. This is my top issue because it undercuts the claim that the second owner was *right*, not just more careful.
2. **The desktop evidence panel implies a second difference between the owners.** The panel arrives straight after the two owners and is headed "Results seem to hinge on which advice owners acted on". Its rows are "Started weaker: nearly 10% worse" and "Started stronger: may have gained…". The natural reading is that the first owner is the weaker businessperson and the second the stronger one. The page text below says "the two owners in the figure don't stand for its groups", and the phone panel is headed "A real study, not these owners", but the desktop panel says neither. The page text also says weaker owners were "especially likely to implement 'generic' advice focused on lowering prices", which is exactly what the first owner did, so the mapping is reinforced rather than broken. Fix: put "A real study, not these owners" (or similar) on the desktop panel too.
3. **Beat 7's caption runs ahead of its picture, then the picture is cut short.** "A jagged frontier marks where…" is on screen from 0:51.6, the line draws only at 0:57.5–0:58.5 with labels at 0:59–0:60, and the panel dims everything at 0:62. That leaves about 1.5 s with the finished frontier. Either split the caption (underlines first, frontier sentence when the line draws) or hold 2–3 s after the labels land. "Jagged" also isn't shown: two boxes and a squiggle don't convey "uneven across look-alike tasks".
4. **The evidence panel doesn't say what was measured.** "Nearly 10% worse" and "over 15%" have no unit, and a business reader will ask "of what?" (it should say revenue and profits, if that's what the study measured; see the fact check). "Started weaker / stronger" isn't defined in the panel (the page text says below or above median performance before the study). The panel also leaves out the headline result, no detectable effect on average. That isn't wrong, but it lets a reader take the split as the whole story.
5. **What was given to the AI is ambiguous on desktop.** The "Given to the AI" stack is three unlabelled blank cards, and the sales records aren't marked as kept back until beat 6. The phone's "Given the quotes and flyer" label is better and should be used on desktop. On the phone, though, "Not given to the AI." sits on its own line under both the flyer line and the sales-records line, so it can read as covering the flyer too, which contradicts "given the quotes and flyer".
6. **"The reviewer sees only the plans" while the flyer and sales-records contents stay legible at the top.** Fade those boxes the way the battery quotes fade.
7. **First owner as a mild straw man.** They're holding the sales records and accept a 20% price cut without a glance. To an owner, cutting prices by a fifth is a big, margin-eating move that nobody makes casually. Their stated reason ("Part 1 checked out, so I trusted the rest") is realistic and fairly voiced. Still, the size of the move makes them look careless rather than reasonably misled. A smaller or more ordinary recommendation (a 10% cut, a discount card, a promotion) would make the reliance error more sympathetic.
8. **The AI is fair, with one caveat.** Its Part 2 is visibly "From their flyer", and it never saw the sales records, so the wrong advice is something it couldn't have known. That's handled well. A business reader may still say a good adviser would have asked "why do your customers come to you?" before recommending a price war, so the AI reads as overconfident rather than careless. The figure owns this ("equal confidence"), so I'd leave it.
9. **Military wording on the page.** The edition switcher at the top reads "See this with: Workplace · **PME**". PME is unexplained military jargon to a business reader. The essay excerpts and links name "The Irreducible Officer", and one section title is "…as a Teachable Competency". These are labelled as the original essay "written for military education", which is acceptable provenance. Nothing in the animation itself uses military or classroom words.
10. **Source line.** "Illustration based on Otis et al. (2026) and its working paper" is clear enough. On the final frame alone, "based on" could suggest the shop, battery and price cut come from the study. The intro text says they're constructed, but someone who only sees the frame (or a screenshot) won't get that. The phone version shortens it to "Based on Otis et al. (2026)…", which is more ambiguous still. Something like "Constructed example; study: Otis et al. (2026)" would avoid that.
11. **Minor.** The "Owner" label vanishes from 0:20 to 0:34. The dot covers "Battery quote" during beat 3. The phone panel's fade-out at about 1:12 briefly overlaps panel text on the stage text for about half a second. The stage is mostly empty space during beats 1–8 at desktop sizes. Before play, the timer shows 0:11.

**Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work?** Yes. A small shop owner asking an AI about suppliers and a competitor, checking the easy factual part and running with the strategy part, is very recognisable. So is a lender or adviser asking "where did this come from?" Managers will map it to staff handing in AI-drafted plans. Nonprofit readers get it at one remove: a grant officer or board reviewing a program plan. The Nairobi setting feels specific rather than distant, because the page ties it to the study.

**Does the AI look careless?** No. Its Part 2 is labelled "From their flyer", and the deciding information ("not given to AI") was never shared. It reads as confident beyond its information, which is the point.

**Straw man or second difference?** Neither owner is a straw man in what they say. The first owner's 20% cut is a little too big to be sympathetic (problem 7). The one real second difference is the one the desktop evidence panel implies: weaker owner versus stronger owner (problem 2).

**Evidence panel and source line:** they read correctly and are hedged ("seem to", "may have", "less certain"). They don't claim the study measured checking, and the caption says plainly that it didn't. Still missing: the outcome measure, a definition of weaker and stronger, and a "not these owners" note on desktop.

**Claims that come only from the working paper:** the median and above/below-median definition (as the page says), 640 and the business-guides control, the owner profile (26, one year, college), the cybershop and printing detail, "noisier", the generic-versus-tailored implementation finding with blackouts and car wash, and the Feb 2024 "more challenging tasks" explanation. The page labels each one as working-paper material. I found nothing in the working-paper material presented as a published result.

**Mislabelled or overclaimed:** nothing outright wrong. Three things to tighten:
1. The evidence panel's percentages have no outcome named. They should say revenue and profit, and they are "equivalent" percent changes converted from SD effects.
2. Desktop doesn't carry the phone panel's "not these owners" heading (see problem 2 above).
3. The figure's two-part task closely echoes the working paper's exploratory finding (price cuts versus blackout power). Combined with the panel, that makes it easy to read the two owners as the study's two groups, which the page itself warns against.
