# Uncalibrated reliance: storyboard cold read, round 1

October 27, 2026 · read from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` (Intent removed). I read only that file before writing sections 1–5. The number check at the end was done afterwards against the source spine.

I'm reading as a teacher who hasn't read the essay. A storyboard is not the motion itself, so my timing judgments are estimates from the times and word counts given.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · The task (0–8 s).** A card says "Example task: Brief relief planners on a port," with two parts: summarize the road reports, and estimate relief ships a day. A red dot labelled "Student" appears, then four closed reports. Clear and calm. I'd read the task card twice, and 8 s is enough for that.
- I'd already wonder which reports each part needs. Part 1 obviously needs the two road reports. Part 2 could need the port survey, the harbor log, or both. The port survey never opens at any point in the figure, so I never learn what it is for. I'd guess it is where the AI got "4 docks", but I'm guessing.

**Beat 2 · The AI answers both (8–16.7 s).** The dot pulses, two grey answer cards rise together under "AI'S ANSWERS", then a "READY" tag lands on each. The caption says "with the same confidence", and the identical cards show it well. Of everything in the figure, this is the clearest visual idea.
- "READY" reads to me as a status ("done", "ready to paste"), not as confidence. The caption has to do the translating. That works, but the tag carries less than the storyboard expects.
- **I'm not told what the AI was given.** Did the student hand it all four reports, or only ask a question? This matters a lot later (see Problems).

**Beat 3 · Checks Part 1, uses both (16.7–25.4 s).** The dot visits both road reports, which open to show grey note bars. A brief appears, Part 1 slides in, the dot writes "✓ CHECKED FIRST", Part 2 slides in, and the dot writes "USED AS IS". It's a lot of travel in 5 s: ten movements one after another. I think I'd follow it because the caption names the action, but my eyes would be on the dot, not the caption.
- "✓ CHECKED FIRST" is ambiguous. On Part 1, I'd read "first" as an order: "this is the part I checked first." "USED AS IS" is perfectly clear. "CHECKED FIRST" isn't.
- The note bars carry no text, so "it's right" comes only from the ✓ and the caption. That's acceptable.

**Beat 4 · Look with us (25.4–32.7 s).** The harbor log opens on its own ("Dock 2: closed for repairs."), the student's dot stays put, "4 docks" gets a navy underline in the first brief, and a navy tag reads "ONLY 3 DOCKS WORK: ABOUT 15 A DAY". "Look with us" tells me this is the narrator showing me, not the student. Good.
- This beat felt **tight**. In about 7 s I read the caption (11 words), the callout (5), and the tag (7), and I do the arithmetic (4 − 1 = 3, 3 × 5 = 15) while an underline draws. The tag lands at 29.5 and the next caption arrives at 32.7. That's 3 s to read it, at the very moment it matters most. It stays on screen until beat 7, but by then my attention is on the frontier and the bar chart.
- I wouldn't know navy means "us" and red means "a student". In this beat it doesn't matter, because the caption says "with us." But I never learn the colour rule.

**Beat 5 · The jagged frontier (32.7–44.7 s).** A zigzag line draws straight across the whole stage between the Part 1 row and the Part 2 row, labelled "INSIDE THE FRONTIER" above and "OUTSIDE THE FRONTIER" below. Then a panel rises: "FIELD EXPERIMENT · BCG CONSULTANTS", "Right answers on a task chosen to fall outside the frontier", and three bars: No AI about 84.5%, AI group 70%, AI group 60%.
- What I'd take away: **AI is good at summaries (above the line) and bad at estimates (below the line).** The line is a regular zigzag that runs dead level across every column, so it looks like a category boundary between kinds of task. I don't think "jagged" comes through as "irregular and hard to see from outside." It comes through as a decorative edge on a straight divider. If that's the intended point, it's being taught backwards.
- "This edge" in the caption confuses the constructed port task with the study. Researchers didn't call *this* edge anything. A quick viewer might think the port task is from the study, even though the card says "Example task."
- I'd also ask: **which AI group is which?** Two bars both say "AI group" with different values, and nothing tells me how they differ. A teacher will ask.
- "About 84.5%" is odd: "about" plus a decimal. Use either "85%" or "84.5%".
- This is the densest stretch. In motion I'd probably still be reading the bar labels when the caption changes to beat 6, but the panel stays, so I'd catch up.

**Beat 6 · Rated higher (44.7–51.4 s).** A bracket beside the two AI bars reads "Rated higher in quality." With the caption, I understand it: the less-correct groups got better grades for quality. This is the most striking fact in the figure, and the bracket on the two *shorter* bars makes it land. "Higher" than whom isn't stated on the bracket, but the caption covers it.

**Beat 7 · Another student (51.4–60.4 s).** The panel and our note clear, and the harbor log closes. **The frontier and its "OUTSIDE THE FRONTIER" label stay.** A second red dot and an empty brief appear at right. The dot pulses and both READY tags blink, then the dot underlines "4 docks" on the AI card, goes straight to the harbor log, and opens it.
- I'd feel **this student had the answer key**. The line saying Part 2 is outside the frontier is still on screen. The harbor log was revealed to me 25 s ago as the culprit. The second student walks straight to it and skips the port survey. In the picture they look prescient, not skilled. Nothing shows *why* they suspected Part 2 or *why* they picked the harbor log over the port survey.
- Blinking READY tags as "the AI gives this student the same answers" only works because the caption says so.

**Beat 8 · Corrects Part 2 (60.4–67.4 s).** Part 2 goes into the second brief with "20" and "4" struck through in red and "15" and "3 working" written in. The dot tags it "✓ CHECKED FIRST", then drops Part 1 in tagged "USED AS IS". The caption says "That student corrects Part 2, then uses Part 1 as is."
- This is where I'd stop and push back. **The second student used Part 1 unchecked, with no reason shown.** The two briefs now carry the same tags on opposite parts, so each student checked one part and trusted the other. Unless I already believe "summaries are safe, estimates aren't", the second student just chose better, or got lucky.
- "CHECKED FIRST" plus "then uses Part 1" makes the ordinal reading stronger: each student's "checked first" part is simply the one they handled first.
- The red strikes and red numbers look like a **teacher's red pen**. In motion I'd see the dot write them. In a still, the second brief is the one that looks "marked wrong".

**Beat 9 · Faculty ask (67.4–83.3 s).** "FACULTY POINT TO PART 2 / AND ASK EACH STUDENT", navy outlines around Part 2 in both briefs, then two speech panels typed at reading pace: "I checked Part 1. It was right, and this sounded just as sure." / "It assumed four working docks. I checked the harbor log: one is closed." The pacing is good, and the two answers are well written and easy to tell apart.
- The question is about "relying on" Part 2, but the second student didn't rely on Part 2 as given. They changed it. Their answer explains why they *didn't* rely on the number. Close enough, but slightly off.
- Faculty point only to Part 2. I'd immediately think: **what if they pointed to Part 1 on the second brief?** That student would have nothing to say.
- Small point: the second student also kept "5 each" unchecked. Their evidence covers one of the two numbers Part 2 rests on.

**Beat 10 · The faculty check (83.3–90 s).** "FACULTY CHECK" and "What evidence justified relying on the system here?", then "Point to one answer. Was the evidence about that part?" The figure rests here.
- "Point to one answer … about that *part*" switches nouns. "Answer" could mean an AI answer card (the screen literally says "AI'S ANSWERS") or a student's spoken answer (the most recent thing called an answer). "Part" is what faculty actually outlined. I'd want one noun.
- "The system" appears for the first time here. Everywhere else it's "the AI."
- **Applied to the figure's own model student, the check fails:** point to the second student's Part 1, and the evidence wasn't about that part, because there wasn't any.

## 2. The point (my words)

Checking an AI's answer on one part of a task tells you nothing about its answer on another part, so the evidence for relying on any answer has to be about that answer, because confident, polished output looks the same either way.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards (my words)

"Pick any piece of your work that came from the AI. What did you check that was about *that* piece, and if you checked nothing, what made it safe to use?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Partly. A careful viewer can reconstruct it: same AI answers, both "READY"; one student's brief has Part 1 ticked and Part 2 "used as is"; the other's has the reverse with Part 2 corrected; the harbor log is open showing the closed dock; the two spoken reasons sit underneath. The first student's quote, "this sounded just as sure," does most of the work. With the check below, it says to me: *your reason for trusting one answer has to be about that answer.*

What weakens it:
- **Nothing on the first brief says it's wrong.** Our "ONLY 3 DOCKS WORK" tag faded in beat 7, so in the still the error is only inferable by comparing the two briefs and reading the callout.
- **Red ticks and red strikes read as grading.** The corrected brief looks like the marked-down one.
- **The frontier band reads as a rule:** summaries inside, estimates outside.
- **The second student's unchecked Part 1 sits in plain view** next to a check that asks "Point to one answer."
- It's dense: roughly 120 words of picture text plus the two-line check. That works on a desktop poster. A glance won't get it.
- Both dots are unlabelled by the end. The brief labels carry it, which is fine.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The model student would fail the figure's own check (confusion / unfair).** The second student uses Part 1 "as is" with no visible reason. The check says "Point to one answer. Was the evidence about that part?", so pointing to the second student's Part 1 gives the same failure as the first student's Part 2. Faculty point only to Part 2, which looks convenient. A teacher will catch this and conclude the second student just guessed better. The figure needs either a visible reason why Part 1 was safe to accept unchecked (something on screen, not "summaries are easy"), or to stop presenting the mirrored tags as the good pattern.
2. **The frontier reads as "AI is good at summaries, bad at estimates" (ambiguous visual).** A regular zigzag running level across the stage between task types looks like a category boundary. The passage the setup quotes says the opposite: performance is uneven "in ways that are not always visible from the outside." As drawn, the frontier is perfectly visible and perfectly predictable by task type. Teachers would carry away a rule of thumb the essay seems to warn against.
3. **The second student looks like they had the answer key (unfair to the first student).** The "OUTSIDE THE FRONTIER" label is still on screen when the second student arrives, and they go straight to the one report we were just shown, skipping the port survey. Their check looks like hindsight. Show what made Part 2 suspicious *from the student's side*, before the frontier, or remove the frontier before they act.
4. **What the AI was given is never stated (unfair to AI / factual ambiguity).** If the student didn't give the AI the harbor log ("which nobody opened"), the Part 2 error is missing input, not the AI being outside its frontier. Calling it a frontier error would then be unfair to the AI and would muddle the concept. One line ("The student gives the AI all four reports") would fix this. If the AI *was* given the log and missed the line, that's a clean frontier example.
5. **"✓ CHECKED FIRST" reads as an order.** Both students' "checked first" part is the one they handled first, so the tag looks like a sequence marker. "✓ CHECKED" or "CHECKED BEFORE USE" against "USED AS IS" would be unambiguous.
6. **Beat 4 is tight.** About 23 words to read plus a subtraction and a multiplication in 7.3 s, and the key tag gets about 3 s before the next caption. It's the beat where the viewer learns Part 2 is wrong, and that shouldn't be the rushed one. Suggest 1.5–2 s more hold.
7. **The two bars labelled "AI group" are unexplained.** A teacher will ask how they differ. Either name them in a few words or show one combined AI bar.
8. **"Point to one answer … that part"** mixes nouns, and "answer" collides with both the AI's answers and the students' spoken answers. "The system" is new vocabulary in the last line.
9. **Red reads as a teacher's correction to an audience of teachers.** In the still, the second student's red strikes and ticks look like grading. Consider whether the student's marks should look like a student's pen.
10. **The caption "Researchers call this edge the jagged frontier"** can be read as researchers having studied this port task. "Researchers call an edge like this…" would be safer.
11. **Small consistency points:** "about 84.5%" (approximate, yet precise to a decimal). The page's Evidence paragraph orders the AI groups "60% and 70%", but the panel and summary order them 70%, 60%. The first dot's label is "Student" but the sheet says "FIRST STUDENT'S BRIEF". The second student checked "4 docks" but kept "5 each" unchecked, so their Part 2 evidence is also partial.
12. **At 390 px every word in the picture is hidden,** so the phone viewer watches unlabelled cards, a zigzag, and red strikes, and gets the meaning only from the beat list. I note it, but I'd rank it low for a desktop-first page.
13. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:** the 84.5% / 70% / 60% figures, and which AI condition each is; "BCG consultants"; that the quality-rating finding applies to the *outside-frontier* task specifically (the bracket implies it does); the two working-paper quotes ("19 percentage points less likely to produce correct solutions" and "regardless of the correctness of their answer"); the working paper number 24-013.
