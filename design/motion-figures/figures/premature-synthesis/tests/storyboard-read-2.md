**Beat 10 · Faculty check (83.3–90.0 s).** The caption band shows "FACULTY CHECK", "Can the student explain why these connections matter?" and then, at the same size, "Point to one connection. What would make it fail?" Clear, and readable in the time given. The second line is the usable move.

## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same AI-assisted paper and redraw the same map from memory, and the difference only shows when faculty ask what would break a key connection: the student who tried connecting the readings before asking the AI, and hit one that didn't hold, can answer, and the one who started from the AI's map can't.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Take the connection your argument leans on hardest. What case or condition would make it stop holding, and how do you know?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. The three column headers read left to right as a procedure: what faculty receive, then a sketch with the papers aside, then a question. The two rows show identical papers, identical sketches and different answers. Without the animation it tells me: the paper and the redrawn map don't separate these students; asking what would break a connection does. The caption band gives me the move to make. That is the useful half of the idea, and it lands.

What it doesn't carry is the cause, the "premature" part. Nothing in the still shows that the second student tried connections before asking the AI. Both papers say AI helped, but not when. The only trace is the red ✕ and "In 1, the country found another supplier." Out of context, the ✕ is unexplained and "In 1," is cryptic. Someone seeing only the still could easily conclude "the second student knew one more fact" or "had a note", rather than "built the map before taking the AI's". The page title and setup copy carry the order. The still doesn't.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The first student read and cited Reading 1, so not knowing "another supplier" looks rigged (fairness, confusion).** Beat 2 insists the first student read all six cards closely, with notes on each, and paper A's third paragraph cites "1 · 4 · 6". The fact the second student learned, "in 1, the country found another supplier", is presumably stated in Reading 1. A sharp teacher will ask how a student who read Reading 1 closely and wrote a paragraph on it can't name another supplier as the thing that breaks the claim. Then the figure reads as "the first student forgot a fact" or "read carelessly", which undercuts both "reads just as closely" and the claim that order is the only difference. There may be a defence: the AI's map placed Reading 1 next to "Allies split" (1–4), so the first student read it through that link and never set it against Reading 2. But nothing on screen shows that. Either the fact has to be something that only appears when 1 and 2 are compared, or the figure has to show that the first student knows Reading 1 but never held it against 2.

2. **The final question is ambiguous, and the first student's answer is a defensible literal reply (ambiguous caption, unfair to the student).** "What would make this connection fail?", asked of a link between two historical cases that both worked, invites "nothing, they both worked", which is roughly what the first student says. "Fail" also shifts meaning from beat 4 (a link that didn't hold) to beat 8 (a condition that would break a generalization). This is the line teachers will carry into class, so it has to be unmistakable. Wording that asks when the claim the connection supports would stop holding, or what case would break it, would remove the escape hatch and make the first student's non-answer clearly a non-answer.

3. **The second student's paper leaves out what they learned (plausibility, could look staged).** The second student found the limiting condition, another supplier, yet hands in the same title, "Blockades work once supplies run out", which says nothing about it. A teacher would expect that qualification in the paper, and would say the papers don't differ only because the figure stopped the better student from writing down what they knew. The near-tautological thesis also makes "a good paper" and "a sound map" harder to believe for a war-college reader. On top of that, the identical verbatim titles risk reading as copying (beat 6), which the figure explicitly wants to avoid.

4. **The still doesn't show order, and the returning note can look like a crib (final frame, fairness).** See section 4. The "premature" part of premature synthesis isn't legible in the poster or in the reduced-motion view. And in beat 9, the red note appearing in the second student's row during a "papers aside" check can look as if that student was allowed notes.

5. **"Main connection" depends on a half-second cue (too fast, ambiguous visual).** The only signal that the main claim rests on 2–5 is a 0.5 s thickening in beat 3, and the second thickening with the "2 · 5" tag muddies it. No caption says it until beat 8's "the main connection". The ring in beat 8 tells the viewer which line, but not why it's the main one until the second student says so.

6. **"Papers aside" isn't shown (caption doesn't match the picture).** Papers dimmed to 80% look present and readable. The caption and header say they're set aside.

7. **Grey vs red can feel like a verdict rather than a description (fairness to the first student).** The first student "studies every line and takes each one up", and their lines stay grey. The second student rereads two cards for 1.2 s, and the AI's 3–4 line turns red. The colour rule explains it (red is what a student decided), but the visible difference between "studied" and "checked" is a brief note flash. A teacher could fairly ask why the first student's careful study doesn't count as checking. If the answer is "going back to the readings", the first student needs to visibly not do that in beat 3.

8. **The AI is shown as bare lines (fairness to AI).** Real AI connections usually come with reasons, and one might well mention that Reading 1's country found another supplier. A teacher could object that a decent AI output would have given the first student the answer. The figure doesn't need to solve this, but the setup copy's "sound map" invites the objection. The absence at the ✕ in beat 5 ("none goes near") is the one thing that defends the AI, and it's hard to see.

9. **Length and density (too fast in places, long overall).** 90 s in all, with 47 s before the faculty side starts. Beat 4 has ten sub-steps. In beat 9 the returning note and the typing answer overlap. Nothing is unreadable, given the holds, but a teacher's attention may fade before beat 7, where the payoff starts.

10. **"In 1," is cryptic (ambiguous label).** It reads as "in one". "In Reading 1," costs one word and matches the answer.

11. **Narrow screens lose the payoff (low, desktop-first).** At 390 px all picture text is hidden, including both answers and the note, so on a phone the decisive contrast lives only in the beat list, not the animation.

12. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:** the Section II and Section VI quotations; Section I's "the papers will not tell you by themselves"; Section VIII's "what would change it?"; that Section IX's pilot uses the order "a map formed before AI, then brought to an AI". The blockade content is labelled constructed ("Example task"), which is good, and the only on-screen numbers are reading numbers.
