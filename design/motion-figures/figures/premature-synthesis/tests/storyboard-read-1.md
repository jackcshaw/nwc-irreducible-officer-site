# Premature synthesis: storyboard cold read, round 1

Read on October 27, 2026 by a cold reader: an educator who hasn't read the essay. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only, with the Intent section removed. There's no build yet, so I pictured the animation from the beat timings and the stage table.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · Six readings (0–7 s).** A task card appears ("Example task: Using the six readings, explain when a blockade works"), then a red dot labelled "Student", then six reading cards, fading in in number order. This is clear: one student, six unconnected readings, a synthesis task. The cards fade in as 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 but sit at scattered positions (top left, top right, middle, left, bottom right, bottom left), so my eye would ping-pong across the stage for 2.4 s. That isn't a real problem, because the titles stay up for six beats. Later I noticed that the layout already has the shape of the AI's map: a left column 1–4–6, a right column 2–5 and 3 in the middle. Nobody would spot that on a first view.

**Beat 2 · The AI connects them (7–16.5 s).** The dot touches each card briefly and pulses. Then five dashed grey lines appear at once, tagged "AI'S MAP". I'd read it as: the student skimmed, asked the AI, and the AI joined everything. The pulse only means "asks" because the caption says so. There's no AI on stage, just its lines, and that's fine. The impression I'd carry out of this beat is that **this student only read the abstracts.** That impression sticks, and it matters later (problem 1).

**Beat 3 · The map makes sense (16.5–26.5 s).** The dot traces each grey line, and each turns from dashed to solid. Then it writes a paper. The title is "Blockades work once supplies run out", the three paragraphs are tagged "2 · 5", "3 · 4 · 5" and "1 · 4 · 6", and a grey note at the foot says "AI HELPED CONNECT THE READINGS". I'd take this as: the student wrote a decent paper from the AI's map and disclosed the AI's help. The lines turn solid while I'm still reading the caption, so I'd probably miss it and never learn that solid means "taken up". I also wouldn't register that paragraph 1 is the main claim or that it rests on 2–5, because each tag and line-thicken pair lasts 0.8 s. That's acceptable if it's meant as a later reveal. The cost is that in beat 8, faculty point at 2–5 and I don't know why that line was chosen.

**Beat 4 · A second student builds a map first (26.5–37 s).** The grey lines and the first dot disappear and a second red dot appears. As the dot reads each card, the card opens to show notes. Four red lines go down one at a time. A fifth red line, from 1 to 2, is tried, the notes flash, a red ✕ appears, and the line retracts. The outline is clear: this student read properly and built their own connections. I hesitated twice:
- A lot changes in the first two seconds, while I'm reading a two-sentence caption. The lines go, the first student goes, a new student arrives and the cards start opening.
- The caption sets up the dropped connection ("One doesn't hold"). It arrives at 34.9 s and is gone, ✕ and all, by 35.4 s. The ✕ is on screen for well under a second and leaves no trace. I wouldn't decode "the notes flash" as "rereads and checks" without the caption.

**Beat 5 · That student checks the AI's map (37–46.5 s).** The second student asks the AI and the same five grey lines appear. Four merge into the red lines. The dot visits cards 3 and 4, and then the fifth line, 3–4, turns red. I'd read it as: the AI agreed with the student's four lines and added one they'd missed, which they checked and kept. This is the clearest beat, and it's fair to the AI. The storyboard says "no line appears where 1–2 was". But the dropped line left no mark, so I can't see that absence. I'd have to remember where the ✕ was.

**Beat 6 · The same paper (46.5–54.1 s).** The second student writes a paper identical to the first, down to the tags and the grey note. The cards and lines leave, and "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE" appears over the two papers. The point lands: the papers can't tell these students apart. But two papers identical line for line trigger a teacher's academic-integrity reflex. They suggest collusion, or two students pasting the same AI output. The figure means "indistinguishable", but literal identity says "copied" (problem 4).

**Beat 7 · Papers aside: sketch it (54.1–62.1 s).** The papers dim and faculty ask, papers aside: "Sketch how the readings connect." Both students draw the same sketch: six nodes, five lines. I'd understand this and appreciate it. The first student isn't a fraud; they know the map. That's a good move for fairness. The sketches show only numbers, though, so from here on "2" and "5" are just numbers unless I remember the card titles.

**Beat 8 · What would break it? (62.1–72.9 s).** Faculty then ask, "What would break this connection?" Line 2–5 thickens and is ringed on both sketches. The first student answers: "It's solid. In both readings, the blockade worked once supplies ran out." That's clear, with two snags:
- The caption, "The first student is sure it holds", frames the problem as certainty. My first reading would be "the confident student is the suspect one", when the real problem is that the student can't name anything that would break the connection.
- "Break this connection" took me a second. A connection between two readings isn't obviously something that breaks. I worked out that it means: what fact would stop these two readings from supporting the same mechanism? A high-school teacher might not get there on a first view.

**Beat 9 · The second student answers (72.9–83.1 s).** "Another supplier. In Reading 5, the country nearly found one. My main claim rests on this link." I'd get it: the student names a condition that would break the connection, and the claim that depends on it. Then I'd immediately think: **of course they know that, they read Reading 5. The other student only read its abstract.** That's where the point wobbles for me (problem 1). "The country" isn't tied to anything (which country?), but I can infer that it's the blockaded one.

**Beat 10 · The faculty check (83.1–89.8 s).** The band shows "FACULTY CHECK", then "Can the student explain why these connections matter?" and "Point to one connection. What would break it?" It's clear and usable. The first line asks why the connections matter, but what the figure tested is what would break one. The second student's "my main claim rests on this link" bridges the two, so it holds together, only just.

By the storyboard's own reading rate, no caption changes before it can be read. The tight spots are inside beats: the ✕ in beat 4, the change from dashed to solid in beat 3, and the checking flashes in beats 4 and 5.

## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same AI-assisted paper and redraw the same map from memory, but only the one who built the connections before asking the AI can say what would break a key link and what in the argument rests on it, so that's the question to ask.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Take the link your main claim leans on hardest. What would you have to find in the readings for it to fail, and what in your paper falls if it does?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It carries half the idea. The still shows two identical papers, both marked "AI helped", two identical sketches with the same ringed line, two very different answers, and the check underneath. On its own it says that neither the paper nor the redrawn map separates these students, but asking what would break one link does. It's a strong picture that could be reused in a faculty meeting.

What it doesn't carry is the cause. Nothing in the still shows that the first student let the AI build the map and the second built theirs first. There are no cards, no grey and red lines and no dropped connection. Someone seeing only the poster could put the difference down to ability or preparation, or most likely to the second student having actually read Reading 5. The title "Premature synthesis" and the page's setup have to supply the mechanism.

The reading titles are gone too, so "2–5" is just two numbers. The answers partly rescue this ("In Reading 5"). One fix would be to leave a small trace of the process in the still, such as keeping each row's 2–5 line in its earlier colour: grey for the first student, red for the second.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Confound: reading depth versus map-first.** The second student differs in two ways. They read each reading closely, where the first student read only abstracts, and they built connections before asking the AI. The decisive answer turns on a fact from inside Reading 5 ("the country nearly found one"). Close reading gives you exactly that, and skimming abstracts doesn't. A teacher could fairly conclude the lesson is "make them do the reading", not "make them build the map before the AI does", and the figure gives no way to separate the two. Possible fixes:
   - Have the first student read the full readings too: drop "After reading each abstract" and lengthen the card touches. Then the only difference is order.
   - Make the second student's answer come visibly out of their map-building (see problem 2).

2. **The struggle doesn't pay off.** The dropped 1–2 connection is the climax of beat 4, the essay's "developmental event". It never returns, and neither does the 3–4 line the student checked and kept. The test falls on 2–5, a line the second student drew without incident and the AI drew too. So the viewer watches the struggle and then sees the win come from something else. The ✕ is on screen for under a second and leaves no mark, which also makes beat 5's missing 1–2 line invisible. Two possible fixes:
   - Let the condition that breaks 2–5 come out of the dropped connection. For example, Reading 1's blockade failed because the country found another supplier. That is why 1–2 didn't hold, and why the student knows another supplier would break 2–5. This would fix problems 1 and 2 together.
   - At least leave a faint trace of the dropped line, such as a ghost ✕, through beat 5.

3. **The beat 8 caption points at confidence, not reconstruction.** "The first student is sure it holds" suggests that confident answers are the tell. That's unfair to confident students, and it isn't the test. The test is whether the student can name what would break the connection and what rests on it. Something like "The first student can only say that it holds" would keep the focus there.

4. **Literally identical papers read as copying.** To a teacher, two papers that match line for line and tag for tag are an integrity flag, or a sign the AI wrote both. They don't read as two pieces of equally good work, and that undercuts "the student writes the paper". One option is to call the papers indistinguishable and show it with the same title, structure and tags but different bar lengths. Another is to say once, in a caption, that each student wrote their own.

5. **"Speaking pace" contradicts the timings.** The answers are 13 words in 1.2 s and 17 words in 1.5 s. That's about 10 words a second, roughly four times the pace of speech. Either the description is wrong or, if the answers are built at a real speaking pace (5–7 s each), beats 8 and 9 run over. The holds as timed do give reading time, so the description just needs to match.

6. **The first student comes across as a skimmer, which undercuts the fairness.** "After reading each abstract" and the brief card touches say "lazy" more than "a careful student who believes the map". Beat 7 then has to work hard to restore them. If the figure wants this student to be careful, it should show them reading.

7. **The checking gestures are brief and abstract.** The notes flash for 0.6 s, the dot runs back, the ✕ lasts 0.5 s and the check of 3–4 takes 0.9 s. Beats 4 and 5 depend on these moments, and a flash doesn't obviously mean "rereads". A slightly longer pause on each card being checked would help.

8. **"What would break this connection?" is abstract wording,** especially for teachers in higher education and high school. The second student's answer really targets the claim ("blockades work once supplies run out") more than the link between two readings. Wordings like "What would prove this link wrong?" or "What would make this link fail?" could be tested on a reader from outside PME.

9. **A first-time viewer won't see the colour and dash coding.** The first student's adopted lines stay grey, while the second student's adopted line turns red. That asymmetry quietly says the synthesis belongs to the model, but nothing on screen points it out, and dashed versus solid grey at a 2.2 stroke is subtle. For an audience of educators, red lines and a red ✕ on student work might also briefly read as a teacher's correction pen. Having the dot draw them mostly prevents that.

10. **The page copy promises a bit more than the figure shows.** It says, "Both students in the figure can sketch the map and say what it argues." The figure shows them sketching, but neither student says what the map argues. And the first line of the check (why these connections matter) isn't quite what faculty asked (what would break it).

11. **Narrow screens (low priority, since the site is desktop-first).** At 390 px all picture text is hidden. The speech panels become empty boxes and the sketches lose their numbers, so the ring on "2–5" can't be identified. Empty speech panels might look broken. They could be hidden, or given a mark.

12. **Factual claims to source.** There are no statistics, and the only numbers on screen are reading numbers. These should be checked against the essay: the two block quotes on the page (Sections II and VI), the storyboard's references to Section I ("the papers will not tell you by themselves"), Section VI ("the first failed attempt to connect") and Section VIII ("what would change it?"), and Section IX's pilot order. "Failure mode 3 of 7" should match the essay's list. I checked these after writing; see the note at the end.
