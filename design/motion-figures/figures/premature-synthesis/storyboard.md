# Premature synthesis: storyboard

Motion figure 3 of 8 · slug `premature-synthesis` · storyboard round 7, with the nameplate standard (all eight figures) · October 27, 2026

Starting point: `concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.3, "Pull the lines" (8.5 s, not built). This version keeps its mechanism: take the lines away and ask for them back. It applies the lessons of figures 1 and 2. Both students read the same readings just as closely, both use an AI, both hand in a paper with the same argument, and both can redraw the same lines without it. The only difference is order: one asks the AI to connect the readings first; the other tries the connections first, and one doesn't hold. Both students know the fact behind that connection. Only the one who tried it can apply it when faculty point to the main connection and ask what evidence would change it. Frame capture's shared runtime and conventions apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** Two students read the same material equally closely and know the same facts. One asks an AI to connect the readings and studies its sound map; the other works out the connections first, learns from one that doesn't hold, and then checks the map with an AI. They hand in the same argument and can redraw the same map. Asked what evidence would change the main connection, both can recall the fact that matters. Only the student who built the connections can apply it, because trying them, including the one that didn't hold, is how they learned what each rests on.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "Both students read all six readings closely. The first then asked an AI to connect them. The map was good; the student studied it, believed it, and wrote their own paper from it. The second tried the connections first. One, between readings 1 and 2, didn't hold: in Reading 1 the navy held the sea, but trade went overland through a neighbour. Then they asked an AI, checked its map against their own and kept a line it found. Both papers made the same argument and said AI helped. Without the papers, both sketched the same map. Then faculty asked what evidence would change the main connection. The first student remembered that Reading 1's trade went overland, but couldn't see how it bore on the connection. The second, who had seen why 1 and 2 don't fit, named an overland route as the evidence and said their main claim would then no longer hold. Knowing the readings and having built the map are different things. So I'd ask whether the student can explain why these connections matter, and to find out I'd point to one and ask what evidence would change it."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from the site's Core Failure Modes table): *Can the student explain why these connections matter?* The final frame adds, directly under it and at the same size, the move the figure makes: *Point to one connection. What evidence would change it?*

**Essay passage** (Section II, "What the Problem Looks Like"):

> **Premature synthesis** appears when a student asks AI to connect material before doing enough work to know what should be connected. The output links sources, themes, and concepts in ways that feel coherent. But if the student cannot reconstruct why those connections matter, the synthesis belongs to the model. The student has bypassed the developmental struggle of forming a mental map and inherited one instead.

Related:
- Section VI, Friction as Developmental Design: "These are developmental events. If AI removes them too early, supplying the first frame before the student has struggled to form one or synthesizing sources before the student has built the mental map to evaluate those connections, it produces a more polished artifact and a weaker thinker." (beats 4–6)
- Section IX, pilot steps one and two: an unaided frame first, "the work of forming a mental map before receiving one", then students "bring their initial frame to an AI system". The second student follows that order. Step five's oral defense asks "what evidence would change the recommendation"; beat 8 asks it of one connection.
- Section VIII, frame evidence: "The questions are: why this problem, why this frame, what would change it?" (beats 8–10)
- Section I: "Now ask which officer understands the problem. The papers will not tell you by themselves." And: "Disclosure tells faculty that a student used AI. It does not tell them whether the judgment in the work is theirs." (beat 6)

**Misreadings to design against.**

1. *The lesson is "do the reading".* Both students read all six with the same motion at the same pace (beats 2 and 4), and in beat 8 the first student recalls the very fact the second uses. The difference is applying it.
2. *The AI's map was wrong.* The second student checks it, ends with the same map, and keeps a connection the AI found that they had missed. The ✕ brightens when the AI's map arrives and nothing appears on it.
3. *Using AI to connect sources is the failure.* The second student also asks an AI and keeps its line. Both papers say AI helped connect the readings, and the final frame says so.
4. *The first student was lazy or forgot.* They read closely, study every line of the map, write their own paper, sketch the map from memory and recall Reading 1 under questioning.
5. *The papers were copied.* Each student writes their own paper: the line lengths differ, the caption says "their own paper", and a label says "Same argument, own words". Nothing else on the page differs, so the papers can't separate the students.
6. *The first student doesn't know the map.* In beat 7 they sketch it perfectly.
7. *Asking a student to explain or redraw the map is enough.* Both redraw it.
8. *The test is about wording or confidence.* Both answers are the students' own words, in the same ink. The caption for the first describes what faculty hear: the student recalls Reading 1 but can't apply it.
9. *The second student brought notes to the oral check.* The papers are moved aside, and the red note returns in the second student's row labelled "What they learned", as something remembered, with no card around it.
10. *A good student finds the AI's errors.* The second student keeps every line the AI drew.
11. *The question is a trick.* "What evidence would change this connection?" is Section IX's oral-defense question asked of one connection, and the second student answers it plainly.
12. *This is fluency substitution again.* The first student isn't repeating the paper's sentences. What they lack is what the connection rests on.

**Why this scenario.** The lab's premature-synthesis case asks an educator to connect two of the essay's own ideas, which a viewer can't judge without reading the essay. The figure keeps the PME setting of figures 1 and 2 with a new task, so the three don't read as the same student. Connecting seminar readings is the ordinary form of synthesis in any classroom. The connection that matters, two blockades that worked while the navy held the sea, can be followed without specialist knowledge, and so can the reason a third blockade didn't fit: trade went overland. Seeing that an overland route is what would change the 2–5 connection takes one step of inference, which is the step the first student never had to make. The task, the readings, the map, the papers, the note and both answers are constructed, and the task card says "Example task".

**Considered and left out.**
- *Pull one reading as a changed fact.* A student who memorised the map could delete that reading's lines and keep the rest, and the viewer would have to track which claims depend on which reading.
- *An AI map with one weak connection.* The papers would differ, and the figure would teach "check the AI for errors" rather than ownership.
- *v0's cluster labels on the AI's map.* More words, and words the first student could repeat.
- *v0's ending, where only two or three lines come back.* A student who studied the map can redraw all of it.
- *Revealing what each map rests on,* as frame capture did. The order sequences and the note carry it.
- *The HE edition's remote-work studies* (Bloom et al., 2015; Bloom, Han & Liang, 2024; Emanuel, Harrington & Pallais, 2023). Real and sourced, but they bring numbers, a workplace setting and a frame problem. A good HE variant.

## 2. Scenario

A war-college student must use six assigned readings to explain when a blockade works. The student reads all six closely, then asks an AI to connect them, studies the sound map it draws, and writes the paper from it. A second student reads them just as closely but tries the connections first. One doesn't hold: in Reading 1 the navy held the sea, but trade went overland through a neighbour. That student then asks an AI, checks its map against their own and keeps a line it adds. Each writes their own paper making the same argument, and without the papers both sketch the same map. Asked what evidence would change the main connection, the first student recalls Reading 1 but can't apply it; the second applies it.

*Sources.* The task, the six readings, the map, the papers, the note and both answers are constructed for this figure, and the task card says "Example task". *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, defines premature synthesis. Section VI warns against "synthesizing sources before the student has built the mental map to evaluate those connections". The second student follows the order of Section IX's pilot: a map formed before AI, then brought to an AI. The question in beat 8 is Section IX's oral-defense question, "what evidence would change the recommendation", asked of one connection. The only numbers on screen are reading numbers.
