## 2. The point

Two students can hand in the same paper, one having inherited the AI's reasonable frame and polished inside it, the other having weighed every option and chosen that frame on purpose. Only asking each of them "why this frame?" shows the difference.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before you settled on 'deter the adversary', what else could this problem have been, what did you set aside, and what would make you frame it differently?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly, yes. Without the animation it shows two identical papers side by side, each titled "Deter the adversary." Under the first sits only the AI's frame with two considerations; under the second sits a red "Chosen" frame with the same two and four set aside. "Why this frame?" sits to the right, and the Faculty check questions sit below. It tells me the product is the same, the reasoning underneath differs, and you have to ask to find out.

What the still loses:
- **The narrowing.** The task card ("Recommend a response…") and the paper titles ("Deter the adversary") are both visible, but nothing links them. You'd have to compare them yourself to see the task was narrowed.
- **The small red ticks** on the second student's chips go unexplained.
- **The "this student" label.** "What faculty receive from this student" is vague once two papers sit side by side.
- **Empty space.** The bottom quarter of the stage is empty.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The headline Faculty check doesn't tell apart the two students it was just shown.** Both papers answer "What problem did the student choose to solve?" the same way: deter the adversary. Beat 10 has just shown that "Why this frame?" is the question that separates them. Then beat 11 leads with a question both students answer identically and demotes the separating question to "Then ask." A teacher could ask the first question, get two identical answers, and stop. The word "choose" carries the intent, but a captured student will still say "I chose deterrence."
2. **It can read as "AI bad, no AI good" (unfair to AI).** Caption 8 never says whether the second student used an AI. The only student shown using one is the one who gets captured, and the only student who weighs is shown working alone. A teacher could take away "don't let students use AI for framing" rather than "make students own the frame." If the second student also asked an AI and treated its frame as one option to weigh, the contrast would be about ownership, not tool use.
3. **The picture for beat 7 is too subtle for its caption.** The AI's marks fading out is a low-contrast tint wipe on a small paper in the corner of a mostly empty stage. No legend ever says that tint means "AI". The red revision bars also disappear when the paper lifts, and no caption covers that. The caption carries the beat; the picture alone reads as "paper tidied up."
4. **Beat 3: the asking isn't shown, and the key rewrite is fast.** There's no AI figure or exchange; the frame just materializes. The task-to-"Deter the adversary" rewrite runs in about 1.5 s (17.8–19.4), and for about 0.5 s the flying text ghosts, unreadable, over the task card and "Room to negotiate." It can be recovered from the end state, but a viewer still on the caption may miss that the task was rewritten.
5. **"AI's frame" under the first paper (beat 10 and the final frame) has an unclear voice.** Is it the student's answer or the narrator's label? If it's the student's, saying "the AI framed it" is honest and shows something, which blurs "one can show what they weighed."
6. **Labels come loose from the dots.** The "Student" label disappears when the dot enters the frame (24.5 s), and the "Another student" label stays behind as its dot moves around (57–62 s). Small moments of "which dot is that?"
7. **Beat 8 is crowded.** Six visits at about 0.5 s each, then a fast reshuffle with overlapping boxes at 60.5 s. You see "touched all six" but can't follow each visit. The 2 s hold on Chosen/Set aside rescues it.
8. **At 390 px the stage drops all text.** Considerations, task card, "Deter the adversary", "AI's frame", "Chosen", "Set aside" and "Why this frame?" all become blank boxes. The beat list adds "On screen" descriptions on phones to compensate, but the viewer has to read them separately from the moving picture, and the rewrite is invisible on a phone. Ranked low because the figure looks desktop-first.
9. **The poster is blank, and autoplay competes with Play.** At t = 0 the stage is empty. On first load at 1280×900 the figure wasn't far enough in view to autoplay, so a visitor sees an empty box above the controls. When keyboard focus reaches Play, the page scrolls the figure into view and autoplay starts, so the first Enter pauses it. The label updates correctly, so it isn't broken, just surprising.
10. **Fairness to students.** No concern. The first student is shown working hard and revising honestly, not cutting corners, and the AI's frame is called reasonable and turns out to match the careful student's choice. The only assumption worth noting is that the captured student can't reconstruct any reasons when asked; a real student might rationalize after the fact.
11. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:** "Failure mode 1 of 7"; "Section VIII of the essay asks: why this problem, why this frame, what would change it?"; and the quotation attributed to Section II. The page labels the task and the six considerations as a constructed example, which is right. See section 6.
