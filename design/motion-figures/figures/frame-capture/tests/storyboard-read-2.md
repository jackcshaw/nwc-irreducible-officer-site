# Frame capture: storyboard cold read, round 2

Read from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (no Intent section, no round-1 report, no essay) until section 5 was written. Section 7 is a fact check done afterwards.

Reader stance: an educator who hasn't read the essay, picturing the figure at desktop width from the storyboard's positions and timings.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1, 0–6 s. The task.** A card at top right says "Example task: Recommend a response to an adversary's military buildup." A red dot labelled Student appears in the open space on the left. I read this as: a student, a strategy assignment. "Open" in the caption tells me the task could go several ways. I didn't register that "a response" is the important phrase until beat 9 told me. That's fine, since the card stays up all the way through, but nothing here makes me look at those two words.

**Beat 2, 6–15.5 s. Many things could matter.** Six dashed chips arrive one by one, three above the dot and three below. The caption defines framing as picking which considerations define the problem. Clear. It's the cleanest beat in the figure. I can't tell yet that dashed means "nobody has taken this up". No legend says so, and I only work it out later when two chips turn solid.

**Beat 3, 15.5–25 s. The AI proposes a narrower frame.** The dot pulses, a grey copy of the task sentence floats to just above the dot and shrinks to "Deter the adversary", grey brackets snap around the title and the dot, two chips (Adversary's intent, Past buildups) slide inside and go solid grey, and "AI'S FRAME" appears. This is the beat where I'd most likely miss something:
- Seven movements run in 4.7 s, and the key one, the sentence narrowing, happens 2.6 to 3.8 s in. A 13-word caption takes about 4.3 s to read, so I'd still be reading the caption band at the bottom when the words change at the top left of the stage. I'd probably catch the result ("Deter the adversary") and miss the change itself.
- Nothing on screen *is* the AI. The dot pulses, then a sentence comes off the task card and moves toward the dot. If I'm watching the picture and not the caption, that reads as *the student* reinterpreting the task. Grey isn't marked as the AI's colour until the tag lands at 20.9 s, the last thing in the beat. The caption covers this if I read it first.
- The brackets "spring tight" around the dot. That's a strong image: the student is inside the frame before writing a word. It also looks like a trap closing, and there's no moment where the student takes the frame up. I come away feeling the AI did this *to* the student. That cuts against the caption's "reasonable".
- Small build note: the source word is "adversary's", so the possessive has to drop when it becomes "adversary". Someone watching closely will see the word change.

**Beat 4, 25–32.4 s. Up close.** The camera pushes in until the frame fills the view and the dot writes a rough draft as ink bars under the grey headings. The caption ("the student sees only the frame") tells me how to read the camera: what I see now is what the student sees. That works, and it sets a rule that beat 6 then breaks (see below). Ink bars as lines of text is a familiar convention, so no problem there.

**Beat 5, 32.4–39.4 s. Revisions.** Rows straighten, a chart grows, a thick conclusion bar goes across. Red bars appear in the margins. The caption matches the picture. I believe the student worked hard. One snag for a teacher: red marks in a paper's margins mean *the teacher's pen* to me before they mean "the student's revisions". The dot drawing them is what corrects that, so in motion it's fine. In the still (section 4) it isn't.

**Beat 6, 39.4–46.1 s. From a distance.** The camera pulls back, the four dashed chips and the task card return, and the dot stays where it is. Caption: "From a distance, every revision stayed inside the AI's frame." Here I wasn't sure what I was looking at. Beat 4 taught me that the camera is the student's view. So by the same rule, pulling back means *the student* now sees the whole problem, and the dot not moving means the student sees it and does nothing. That makes the student look complacent, which is the reading the figure is trying to avoid. If the pull-back is meant as *our* distance, the distance the student never had, the caption has to say so. As written it has no subject. I also didn't feel much of a turn. I watched the frame get imposed in beat 3, so the pull-back reminds me of something I already know and doesn't reveal anything.

**Beat 7, 46.1–54.1 s. What faculty receive.** A small copy of the paper appears under the task card, labelled "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE", still with its grey title, grey headings and its own grey brackets. About 2.7 s later a wipe turns the grey to ink, then the brackets fade. Caption: "The paper faculty receive doesn't show where the frame came from." For roughly the first three seconds the picture contradicts the caption: the label says this is what faculty receive, and the paper plainly shows the AI's grey. I understood after the wipe, but I had to resolve the contradiction myself. I'm also unsure I'd *see* the wipe. The paper is at 52%, so its title is about 9 viewBox units tall, roughly 7–9 px on screen. Grey `#5b564f` and navy `#0a2242` are both dark, so at that size the visible change is mostly a pale band disappearing, and that band is already low-contrast on the bright paper. Separately, the student's red change bars are simply absent from paper A. That's a second loss of evidence (revision history) that the figure doesn't point out. Fine to leave it implicit, but it's there.

**Beat 8, 54.1–63.1 s. The same paper.** An identical paper appears below, labelled "FROM A STUDENT WHO CHOSE THIS FRAME", with a small red bracket mark. Caption: "Another student weighs all six, chooses this frame, and writes the same paper." I got this, and it clearly heads off "the AI's frame was bad" and "should have covered all six". Two things snagged:
- The labels aren't parallel. Paper A is labelled by who *receives* it, paper B by who *wrote* it. Do faculty receive paper B too? Presumably yes, but the labels make A look like "the faculty copy" and B like something else. I'd expect "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE" over both, with "from this student" and "from a student who chose this frame" under it.
- The small red bracket sits right above paper B. The point is that the two papers can't be told apart, but one of them has a red mark beside it. Only the storyboard's notes say the mark sits outside what faculty get. On screen it looks like part of the paper.

**Beat 9, 63.1–69.8 s. Faculty check.** "FACULTY CHECK: What problem did the student choose to solve?" in large italic. As a closing line for *me*, the viewer, it lands: I look at the picture and my answer is "the student didn't choose; the AI did." As a question a teacher would *ask a student*, it doesn't work. Both students would answer "Deter the adversary", and the picture at that moment shows exactly that on both papers. The question that separates them is the follow-up the page copy puts in the essay's mouth: why this frame, what else, what would change it. Also, "FACULTY CHECK" is set in red, the colour the figure has spent a minute reserving for the student.

## 2. The point

An AI can hand a student a reasonable frame that quietly becomes the whole problem, and because the finished paper looks the same as one from a student who weighed the options and chose that frame, faculty can only find the difference by asking how the frame was chosen.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"What else could this problem have been about, and why did you settle on deterrence instead?" (Follow-up: "What would make you frame it differently?")

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. What it tells me: on the left, a grey box marked as the AI's, with the student's red-marked work entirely inside and four unused considerations outside; on the right, the original open task above two papers that look the same, one from "a student who chose this frame"; underneath, a question about choice. I'd get "the paper doesn't show whether the frame was chosen" from the still.

What the still loses or muddies:
- **The erasure.** Without the wipe, nothing tells me paper A *used* to show the AI's grey. Grey title on the left against an ink title on the right says it in principle, but at 52% and 7–9 px I doubt I'd notice.
- **The gaps.** The two missing chips (top middle, bottom left) can read as uneven layout, not "these two were taken into the frame".
- **The narrowing.** The task card's "a response" and the frame's "Deter the adversary" are both on screen but far apart, and nothing links them.
- **The colour of red.** Static red margin bars beside a paper, plus a red "FACULTY CHECK", read as a teacher's markup. Someone seeing only the poster could take red to mean faculty.
- **Label size.** The labels that carry the argument ("AI'S FRAME", "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE", "FROM A STUDENT WHO CHOSE THIS FRAME", "STUDENT'S DRAFT 3") are mono 9.5–10.5 units, which is 7–10 px at common desktop widths (see problem 4). The still depends on them more than the animation does.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Beat 6's camera reverses its own meaning (confusion, unfair to students).** Beat 4 says the camera is the student's view ("Up close, the student sees only the frame"). Beat 6 pulls back with a caption that has no subject ("From a distance..."). By the figure's own rule the student now has distance, and the still dot then reads as a student who sees the whole problem and stays put anyway. That's the "lazy student" reading beat 5 works to prevent. The caption needs to make the distance ours, e.g. "Seen from outside, every revision stayed inside the AI's frame", or say outright that the student never gets this view.
2. **The faculty check doesn't separate the two students.** "What problem did the student choose to solve?" gets "Deter the adversary" from both, and the final frame shows that answer on both papers. It works as a rhetorical prompt for the viewer. It fails as the question to ask the student, which is how beat 9 presents it. The separating question ("why this frame over the others; what would change it?") is pushed into the page copy. Either make the check that question, or reword it so it can't be answered in three words.
3. **No cost is shown, so "so what?" is a fair reaction.** Beat 8 shows the captured student and the choosing student producing the same paper. A sceptical teacher can conclude "same output, no harm". The figure shows that capture is invisible but never shows why it matters (e.g. the captured student can't defend the frame or adapt when the situation changes). That may be out of scope for one figure, but the setup copy or the follow-up line should give the stake in a sentence.
4. **Key labels too small to read in motion (legibility).** The narrow-screen rule says text drops under 11 px below 720 px of figure width. That's true of the 15-unit chip labels. The mono labels are 9.5–10.5 units, so they render at 7–8 px at 720 px and only 9.5–10.5 px at a full 960 px. They stay under 11 px until the figure is about 1,110 px wide. Beats 7 and 8 rely on exactly those labels to tell paper A from paper B. If the figure sits in an article column under 720 px on desktop, they disappear entirely.
5. **Beat 7's picture contradicts its caption for about 3 s.** The paper is labelled "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE" while it still shows the AI's grey and brackets. Either bring in the label after the wipe (or after the brackets fade), or label the sheet "the paper" first and "what faculty receive" once it's in one ink.
6. **The grey-to-ink wipe may be invisible at 52%.** The two colours are both dark, and the pale band is low-contrast already. The main visual change of beat 7 may come down to a faint tint disappearing from a 7–9 px title. Consider a larger contrast step (a lighter grey for AI text, or a more visible band), or briefly enlarging paper A for the wipe.
7. **Paper labels aren't parallel, and the red mark sits on paper B.** "WHAT FACULTY RECEIVE" (A) against "FROM A STUDENT WHO CHOSE THIS FRAME" (B) leaves it unclear whether faculty also receive B. The red bracket beside B puts a visible difference next to the paper in the beat that says there is none.
8. **Red is overloaded, and educators read red margin marks as grading.** Red is the student, the student's revisions, the student's labels, the "FACULTY CHECK" heading and the current-beat marker in the beat list. "FACULTY CHECK" in red breaks the colour rule outright. Red margin bars are the teacher's pen to this audience, a reading the dot corrects in motion and the still doesn't.
9. **Beat 3 is dense, and the AI has no on-screen presence.** Seven movements in 4.7 s, with the narrowing landing while I'm still reading the caption. And the narrowing starts from the dot's pulse and the task card, with no AI source, so in the picture alone it reads as the student narrowing the task until the tag arrives last. Consider holding the narrowing until the caption has been read once (as beat 7 does), and showing the tag or some mark of the AI's source before the sentence moves.
10. **The frame closes on the student with no acceptance moment (fairness to AI and to students).** The brackets "spring tight" around the dot, so the AI looks like it traps the student. The essay's idea, as the setup copy puts it, is that the first plausible frame *becomes* the student's. Nothing shows the student taking it up. Combined with grey AI frame against red human-chosen frame, it's easy to come away with "don't let AI frame the problem", not "own whatever frame you use". Paper B's student might also have got the frame from an AI and then chosen it. The figure doesn't say, and the colour binary suggests they didn't.
11. **The example may look like a straw man to PME faculty.** A deterrence frame that ignores allies' interests and economic pressure will strike security-studies faculty as thin (extended deterrence runs through allies, and economic pressure is a standard deterrent tool). The figure calls the frame "reasonable", and expert viewers may not agree. Swapping one heading (e.g. Allies' interests in, Past buildups out) or changing the title might keep the point and make the frame more credible. For K–12 and HE viewers, the military scenario is followable but remote, and the setup could say the pattern isn't specific to military tasks.
12. **Small text and build points.** The "adversary's" to "adversary" morph has to drop the possessive. Beat 2's caption defines framing as picking considerations, while the figure's frame is also a goal statement ("Deter..."). That's acceptable shorthand but slightly narrower than the picture. "Dashed means untaken" is never explained on screen, and beat 6's notice depends on it.
13. **Sources to confirm.** The four quoted phrases attributed to Sections I and II, the Section VIII follow-up questions, "Failure mode 1 of 7", the Section II heading in the essay link, and the book chapter title "Frame the problem". Checked in section 7.
