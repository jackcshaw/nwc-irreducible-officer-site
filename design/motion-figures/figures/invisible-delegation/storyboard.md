# Invisible delegation: storyboard

Motion figure 5 of 8 · slug `invisible-delegation` · storyboard round 6: round 5 plus the shared nameplate · October 28, 2026

Starting point: `concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.5, "What crossed over" (8.5 s, not built). This version keeps v0's mechanism: a ledger of decisions, and ordinary requests that carry cards across it. It uses three of the essay's four request pairs, the three the site names (criteria, structure, meaning). The ledger has three lanes: what the student decides, what the student asked, and what the AI decides. Each of the first student's requests visibly carries a decision card across into the AI's lane. The turn shows what each request left unsaid: whose standard to use. The AI used its own. A second student, with the same task and rubric, starts from the rubric and writes each decision down before its request, with words that point back to it. That habit is the one difference. It shows in their working: they turn down one AI note that doesn't serve the rubric. Faculty can't see the ledger or the notes. They ask a question each student can answer honestly, and the answers show whose standard the help followed. The shared runtime and `CONVENTIONS.md` apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** Ordinary requests for help (feedback, a better structure, a more strategic version) can each hand the AI a decision: the criteria, the argument, what "strategic" means. The request names the kind of help and leaves out whose standard to use, so the AI uses its own. A student with the same task and rubric gets the same help and keeps the decisions. They decide first, starting from the rubric, and point to that decision when asking. Their standard shapes what they accept. The papers don't show the difference. Faculty find it by asking each student whose standard the help followed.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "Both students had the same task and the same rubric: evidence must back the call. The first asked an AI for feedback, then a better structure, then a more strategic version, and revised after each. Each request carried a decision across to the AI: what counts as good, how the argument runs, what 'strategic' means. The requests never said whose standard to use, so the AI used its own. The student worked through every note. The second student started from the rubric. Before each request, they wrote the decision down, and the request pointed to it: 'against my criteria', 'for my argument', 'as I define it'. Both got the same AI notes. The second turned one down, 'Be more decisive', because the evidence has to back the call. Both papers came back revised with AI and look alike. Asked 'Better by whose standard?', the first said 'The AI's notes, I suppose.' The second said 'Mine, set first from the rubric.' So I'd ask what the AI decided for the student, and to find out I'd ask each student whose standard the help followed."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from the site's Core Failure Modes table): *What did the AI decide for the student?* The final frame adds, directly under it and at the same size, the move the figure makes: *To find out, ask each student: better by whose standard?*

**Essay passage** (Section II, "What the Problem Looks Like"):

> **Invisible delegation** occurs when the student does not notice which parts of the work they have handed over. Asking for "feedback" may delegate criteria. Asking for "a better structure" may delegate the argument. Asking for "counterarguments" may delegate the range of imaginable objections. Asking for "a more strategic version" may delegate the meaning of strategic. The language of assistance hides the transfer of judgment. The student believes they are working; the model has already done the framing.

Related:
- Section IV: "When the human fails to provide a purpose, provides one too vaguely, or accepts the system's inferred purpose without noticing, a default can govern the work. That is still not the same as authorizing the purpose." (beat 5)
- Section V: "Minimal-context prompting inherits the model's frame almost entirely. Structured prompting with explicit purpose and evaluation criteria moves the human contribution upstream." (beats 6–8)
- Section VIII, frame evidence: before submitting, "the student names the problem frame, the key assumptions, the criteria for success, the evidence standard, and the role AI played in the work." (beat 9)
- Section I: "Disclosure tells faculty that a student used AI. It does not tell them whether the judgment in the work is theirs." (both papers carry the same "Revised with AI" tag)

**Misreadings to design against.**

1. *Asking an AI for help is the failure.* The second student asks for the same three kinds of help and gets the same notes, headings and paragraph. Both papers say "Revised with AI".
2. *The AI's decisions were bad.* The cards carry only the name of each decision. The note the second student turns down ("Be more decisive") isn't wrong in general; it doesn't serve their rubric.
3. *The first student was lazy or careless.* They tick every note and revise after every request, through four drafts, and answer honestly.
4. *The first student lacked knowledge.* Both have the same task card and rubric from beat 1. The first student's answer is about a habit.
5. *"Mine" puts a personal standard above the course's.* Beat 6 is the second student starting from the rubric, enlarged on screen, and their answer says "set first from the rubric".
6. *Deciding first changed nothing.* The second student's cards say what they decided, and beat 7 shows them judging the same notes against those decisions: they strike "Be more decisive" with the reason "evidence too thin". The first ticked it.
7. *The fix is a prompt phrase.* The second student writes each decision on its card in words ("Evidence must back the call"), then asks. Beat 6's caption names that order, and a red pointer runs from each clause back to the card that says what was decided.
8. *The unsaid words were typed.* They sit on their own line under each request, outside the quotation marks, after an "UNSAID" tag, in a dashed ghost box, with a "LEFT UNSAID" key.
9. *An answer comes from the AI.* Each speech panel carries the student's name in red.
10. *Faculty can see delegation in the paper, or knew the answer.* The ledgers and notes dim when faculty ask, and both students get the same open question.
11. *Never accept an AI's criteria.* The page copy carries the lab's fair objection: an assistant can legitimately propose criteria; the trouble starts when a student accepts them without seeing how they relate to the task.

## 2. Scenario

A war-college student wants to improve a draft paper recommending whether to keep a forward base. The task card carries the course rubric: evidence must back the call. The student asks an AI for feedback, then a better structure, then a more strategic version. They work through every note and revise after each request, through four drafts. Each request names only the kind of help. Each carries a decision to the AI: the criteria, the argument, what "strategic" means.

A second student, with the same task, starts from the rubric. Before each request they write the decision on its card:
- "Evidence must back the call";
- "Lead with the call, then its evidence";
- "Serves the ally's long-term interest".

Then they ask for the same help, with words that point to the card: "against my criteria", "for my argument", "as I define it". Both get the same AI notes. The second student judges them against those decisions and turns one down: "Be more decisive", struck, with "evidence too thin" written under it.

Both papers come back revised with AI and look alike. Faculty, who have only the papers, ask each student "Better by whose standard?" The first says "The AI's notes, I suppose. I worked through what it flagged." The second says "Mine, set first from the rubric: does the evidence back my call?"

*Sources.* The task, the rubric, the notes, the decisions, the requests, the papers and both answers are constructed for this figure, and the task card says "Example task". *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, defines invisible delegation and supplies the request pairs. Section V describes structured prompting with explicit purpose and evaluation criteria. The figure shows no numbers.
