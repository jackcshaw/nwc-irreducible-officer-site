# Invisible delegation: storyboard cold read, round 1

Reader: cold-read tester playing a first-time educator who hasn't read the essay. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed). Written October 27, 2026, before I opened any source.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · The draft (0–7 s).** A task card ("Example task: recommend whether to keep a forward base open"), a red dot labelled "First student", and a paper. I read it as: this is a student and their own draft. Clear, and the pace is easy. The dot is abstract, but the name label does the work.

**Beat 2 · Three decisions (7–15.7 s).** A divider with "THE STUDENT DECIDES" (red) and "THE AI DECIDES" (grey), and three dashed cards on the student's side. I read it as the three judgment calls behind "improving" a paper, none made yet. Two small snags:
- No caption has mentioned an AI yet, but "THE AI DECIDES" is already on screen. It works as foreshadowing: I immediately expect cards to move right. It also gives away the shape of the story.
- I took "these three" on trust. A teacher might ask why these three and not, say, evidence or audience. The caption's "deciding three things" asserts it rather than showing it.

**Beat 3 · Feedback (15.7–25.5 s).** "Give me feedback." grows above the criteria card, settles over it, then slides right across the divider and parks. A fainter dashed slot stays behind. The AI's notes appear on the paper, the dot rewrites three rows, and "REVISED WITH AI" appears. The caption says "Watch the criteria", so I'm watching the card. **This is the moment I was least sure what I was looking at.** The card is almost completely hidden under the bubble (a 3-unit edge, about 3 px on a laptop). What stays behind is also a dashed outline, only fainter (30% against 45%) and without the label. So my likely read is "the bubble moved, and the card's label disappeared", not "the bubble carried the card away". If I do catch the carry, I get the metaphor (the request's words cover the decision) and it's good. A lot happens after the crossing (chip, ticks, rewriting, tag) with only 1.6 s of hold, and the tag barely registers.

**Beat 4 · Structure and strategy (25.5–37 s).** The same cycle twice more, with grey headings and then a grey paragraph appearing in the paper. I understood "same thing again", and the caption is fine. It's busy: 13 sub-steps in 11.5 s. The dot "moving two blocks of rows" to show reordering happens inside a paper 100 units wide, so I'd probably see shimmer, not reordering. The student's effort, which the storyboard cares about, may not read at this scale.

**Beat 5 · Behind the requests (37–45.5 s).** The parked bubbles pulse, then shrink into quote lines, and solid grey cards show under them: decided by the AI. The empty slots pulse. The caption, "each one handed the AI a decision", lands clearly. But because I already watched the cards cross in beats 3–4, the reveal is mostly a colour change (dashed to solid grey). The difference between "carried across" (beat 3) and "decided" (beat 5) is subtle. A viewer who missed the carry in beat 3 gets the whole idea here. A viewer who caught it may feel this beat repeats itself.

**Beat 6 · A second student (45.5–56.5 s).** The first block slides up, and a second block fades in below with all its parts at once. The dot writes in the criteria card, which turns solid red, and then asks "Give me feedback against my criteria." with the clause in red. The bubble passes *above* the card, and the card stays put. Same notes, same rewriting, same tag. The contrast with beat 3 is clear and well chosen: same request plus a clause, and the card doesn't move. **New confusion here:** the request settles as a quote line on the AI's side, under the grey "THE AI DECIDES" heading, and it carries red words. Red means "the student decided", but the text sits in the column labelled "the AI decides". For a moment I read the bottom-right column as "the AI decided 'against my criteria'".

**Beat 7 · Each request says the decision (56.5–68.4 s).** Two more cycles. All three cards stay red, and the same grey headings and paragraph appear. "The AI helps just as much" is fair to the AI, and I liked that. But the identical grey help in both papers also made me think: *so the papers came out the same?* That's presumably the setup for beat 8 (faculty can't tell the papers apart), but at this point it reads as "the decisions changed nothing".

**Beat 8 · Faculty ask (68.4–81.8 s).** The ledgers dim and the papers stay. "FACULTY ASK EACH STUDENT / Better by whose standard?" appears, and the two answers type out. I read the dim as "this is what faculty can't see", and the caption confirms it. Good. The answers are the emotional centre: "The AI's notes, I suppose. I worked through every one." is sympathetic and honest, which is fair to the student. "Mine, set first: does the evidence back my call?" is clipped and reads as written, not spoken. I had to parse "set first" ("which I set first"). The caption, "They ask each student about the AI's help", doesn't quite match the question on screen, which is about *the standard*, not the help. This beat runs at the reading-budget ceiling, but the answers stay on screen, so it doesn't feel rushed.

**Beat 9 · Faculty check (81.8–89.3 s).** "FACULTY CHECK / What did the AI decide for the student?" rises into the caption band, the ledgers return, and then "Ask: better by whose standard?" appears at the same size. **I wasn't sure which question I, as a teacher, am meant to ask.** Two questions at equal weight with no connective read as two separate checks. The first sounds like something to ask the student, yet beat 8 just told me faculty can't see the ledger. Only the page copy explains that a student can honestly answer "nothing" to the first, which is why you ask the second. The figure alone doesn't say that. "For the student" is also singular when there are two students on screen.

## 2. The point

Ordinary requests for AI help ("give me feedback", "make it more strategic") can quietly hand the AI the decisions that define what "better" means, and the finished paper won't show it, so faculty have to ask the student whose standard the improvement followed.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before you asked the AI for help, what had you already decided good would look like, and how did you judge whether its suggestions met that?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. The two blocks stacked one above the other make the contrast at a glance: three grey cards under plain requests and an empty student side on top, three red cards and requests with red clauses on the bottom, and two answers at the right. To me it says: *same help, same-looking papers; one student let the AI set the standard and the other set it first and can say so.* The quote-over-grey-card pairing reads as "this request → the AI decided this" even without the animation, which is the strongest part of the still.

Where the still is weaker:
- It's dense. I count roughly 130 words, many at 12–15 units, with three question-like lines (the right-column header plus two lines in the caption band).
- The red student requests under "THE AI DECIDES" have the same column-meaning problem as in beat 6.
- The bottom student's AI side shows no grey cards, but their paper has grey AI-written text. That's actually a nice distinction (the AI can *supply* without *deciding*), but nothing in the still says so, and a sharp viewer may call it a contradiction.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The ending's two questions compete (confusion).** "What did the AI decide for the student?" and "Ask: better by whose standard?" appear at equal size with no stated relationship. A teacher may take the first as the question to ask, which the page copy says fails ("nothing"). The figure needs the bridge on screen, e.g. "To find out, ask: …", or a clear hierarchy (the check as faculty's private question, the "Ask" line as the move). Also fix "the student" (singular) with two students shown.
2. **The key crossing in beat 3 is an ambiguous visual.** Under "Watch the criteria", the card vanishes under the bubble with about 3 px showing, and a near-identical, fainter dashed outline stays behind. Likely misread: "the label disappeared" or "the bubble replaced the card", not "the request carried the decision away". Show more of the card riding under the bubble (a visible strip, part of the label), or make the slot plainly different from a card. This matters because beat 5's payoff depends on it.
3. **The "THE AI DECIDES" column holds student-written text (spatial rule against colour rule).** Every request lands as a quote on the AI's side, including the second student's red words. Position says "the AI decided this" while colour says "the student decided this". Consider a header that covers both ("ASKED OF THE AI / THE AI DECIDES"), or keep the quote lines visually separate from the decision row.
4. **The "so what" isn't shown, and the first student may be treated unfairly.** Both papers get identical grey help, the first student worked through every note, and no cost to their paper or recommendation is shown. A sceptical teacher may say: if the papers match and the notes were good, why does it matter? Also, accepting proposed criteria after working through them is arguably a decision, yet the ledger scores the first student at zero. The "an assistant can legitimately propose criteria" objection lives only in page copy. A hint of consequence would help, e.g. the AI's idea of "strategic" drifting from the keep-or-close call.
5. **Possible misread: the fix is a prompt phrase.** The visible difference in the final frame is a red clause appended to the same request. Some teachers will take away "add 'against my criteria' to your prompt" rather than "decide first". Red also reads as a teacher's red-pen correction, which strengthens that misread. The card turning red *before* the request helps, but only in motion.
6. **The caption hardens the essay's hedge.** The essay excerpt says a request "may delegate", but beat 5's caption says "each one handed the AI a decision". That's fine for a constructed case, but teachers may generalise to "asking for feedback is delegation". Consider "here, each one …".
7. **The beat 8 caption doesn't match the picture.** "They ask each student about the AI's help" against an on-screen "Better by whose standard?". Align them.
8. **The second answer is hard to parse aloud.** "Mine, set first: does the evidence back my call?" Something like "Mine. I set it first: does the evidence back my call?" reads as speech.
9. **Pace and density.** Beat 4 (13 sub-steps in 11.5 s) and beat 6 (a whole new block plus a full cycle) are the busiest. Beat 3's 1.6 s hold leaves "REVISED WITH AI" barely noticed, yet beat 8 leans on it. The paper-scale "rewrite" and "reorder" motions are probably too small to read as student effort.
10. **Narrow screens look optimistic.** Two two-column mini ledgers side by side at 390 px means four columns of about 85 px. Chips like "What 'strategic' means" and quotes like "Make it more strategic, as I define it." will wrap to three or four lines, so the ~700 px final-frame estimate looks low. Desktop-first, but worth a build check.
11. **Numbering.** The header says "Motion figure 5 of 8"; the kicker says "Failure mode 5 of 7". Probably two different counts, but check that the kicker matches the companion's list.
12. **Factual claims to source.** The figure shows no numbers, which is good. Claims to verify: the Section II and Section V quotations, and that the companion's failure-mode lab contains the "assistant can legitimately propose criteria" objection. (See the addendum below.)
