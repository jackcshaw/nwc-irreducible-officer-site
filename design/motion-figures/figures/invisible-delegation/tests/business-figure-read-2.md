# Cold read: invisible-delegation, business edition, figure, round 2

Reader stance: a manager or analyst at a company or nonprofit who has never read the essay.

Material: `figures/invisible-delegation/index.html?edition=business` only, served locally on port 5241. I watched it at 1280×900, 1280×720 and 390×844, scrolled fully into view so it autoplayed, with a frame captured every 0.5 s for the whole run (209, 195 and 194 frames), looked at in order. I also held moments with `?t=` and checked `?reduced`, `prefers-reduced-motion`, keyboard use and overflow. The machine was under load during the 1280×900 run, so its clock skipped by up to about 3 s a few times between 0:38 and 0:50. I re-checked those beats in the 390 run, which had no skips, and with `?t=` holds. Timings below come from the clean run.

Layouts differ. At 1280×900 the figure is a three-lane ledger: "The analyst decides", "Requests", "The AI decides", with small email thumbnails. At 1280×720 and 390 it switches to a compact HTML layout with two columns (720) or a single stack (390): no email thumbnails, no middle "Requests" lane, and a colour key instead.

## 1. Beat by beat

**0:00–0:03, name card.** "Failure mode 5 of 7 / Invisible delegation", large, then it shrinks into the corner. Clear.

**Beat 1, 0:03–0:13 (10 s). "An analyst improves a client email to the firm's standard. We'll track three decisions."** A task card, "Tell the client the project slips three weeks", then the standard, "Bad news first, with the new date." A red dot labelled First analyst appears with a draft. The ledger draws in, with three dashed cards in the analyst's lane: "The criteria", "How the email runs", "'Professional' means". The dot visits the standard, which gets a faint grey highlight. Plenty of time. I knew at once what the three cards were: the things the analyst hasn't decided yet.

**Beat 2, 0:13–0:24 (11 s). "The analyst asks an AI for feedback. The request carries the criteria across."** The bubble "Give me feedback" clips onto "The criteria" at about 0:14. The two travel slowly into "The AI decides" (0:15–0:18), and the card turns grey. The request settles in the middle lane. The AI's notes appear, "Cut the jargon / Formal phrasing / Add a recap", and the analyst ticks all three, one per second. The draft becomes "Draft 2, revised with AI." The caption arrives about 1.5 s before the bubble, enough to read first. This is the clearest beat in the figure. At 390 there are no lanes, so nothing goes "across": the card nudges right and turns grey in place, and only the key ("AI decides" swatch) tells you what grey means.

**Beat 3, 0:24–0:36 (12 s). "Then for a better structure, and a more professional version."** "Suggest a better structure" carries "How the email runs" across (0:26–0:29). Draft 3. "Make it more professional" carries "'Professional' means" across (0:30.5–0:32). Draft 4, with grey AI lines in the thumbnail. The second handover follows the first quickly, but I could follow both.

**Beat 4, 0:36–0:45 (9 s). "Each request left out whose standard to use. The AI used its own."** A "Left unsaid" key appears. Under each request, in turn: "UNSAID whose criteria?", "whose outline?", "whose meaning?". The grey cards pulse. Readable, and the moment the idea lands for me: the requests were reasonable, and what they left out was the standard.

**Beat 5, 0:45–0:52 (7 s). "A second analyst starts from the firm's standard."** The first ledger moves up and a second analyst appears below with the same three dashed cards. Their dot visits the task card, and this time the standard grows larger and is underlined. In beat 1 it got only a faint highlight. Together with the caption, this reads as though the first analyst *didn't* start from the standard. See problem 2.

**Beat 6, 0:52–1:03 (11.5 s). "Before each request, the second analyst writes the decision down."** The dot writes in red on each card, "New date, and why", "Date, reason, plan", "Clear and courteous", and after each it asks the same three requests word for word. The AI lane stays empty. Each write-then-ask takes about 2.5 s. I could follow it, but it is brisk, and the red writing is small italic.

**Beat 7, 1:03–1:14 (10.5 s). "Both get the same AI notes. The second analyst judges them against their decisions."** The same three notes. Ticks on the first two (1:05, 1:06). At 1:08 "Add a recap" is struck in red and "not in my outline" is written under it. In the wide layout the outline card ("Date, reason, plan") pulses for about half a second at that moment. The draft goes from 2 to 4. The strike is the strongest single image in the figure. The pulse linking it to the outline is easy to miss, and in the 720 and 390 layouts the wrong card pulses (problem 3).

**Beat 8, 1:14–1:27 (13 s). "The manager has only the emails, and asks each analyst about the standard."** The ledgers fade and the two emails stay: "Draft 4, revised with AI". "The manager asks: Better by whose standard?" appears. First analyst: "The AI's notes, I suppose. I worked through what it flagged." Second: "Ours, set first from our standard: bad news first." In the wide layout the first answer types out over about 2 s. Readable.

**Beat 9, 1:27–1:33 (6.5 s), the end hold.** The caption is replaced by "INVISIBLE DELEGATION: A request for help hands over criteria, structure, or meaning. REVIEWER'S CHECK: What did the AI decide for them?" The source line appears, the ledgers return at full strength, and at about 1:30 the second line arrives: "To find out, ask each analyst: better by whose standard?" A lot arrives in four seconds, but the figure stops and holds, so nothing is lost.

At no point did a caption change before I could read it. The longest caption (beat 7, 13 words) gets 10.5 s.

## 2. The point, in my words

Two people can hand in the same AI-polished email, but only one decided what "good" meant before asking for help, and the emails won't tell you which; asking whose standard they used might.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"Before you asked the AI, what had you already decided this email had to do, and which of its suggestions did you turn down, and why?"

The figure's own question, "Better by whose standard?", is the first half of that. I'd want the second half; see problem 1.

## 4. The final frame alone

At 1280×900 it carries the idea without the animation. Two rows, the same three requests in each. The first row's three cards sit grey in "The AI decides", with "UNSAID whose…?" tags under the requests. The second row's three cards sit red in the analyst's lane, in their own words. The AI's notes sit side by side, one set fully ticked, the other with "Add a recap" struck. The two answers sit in speech bubbles, and the takeaway and the question sit below. It says: same task, same AI, same words, same output, different person in charge, and here's the question that finds out who. It is dense, roughly 40 pieces of text, but it is laid out as a ledger, so I read it in order. At 390 the same content stacks into two narrow columns with a colour key. It's crowded but legible, and the lane headings are replaced by the key.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The question separates them only because the first analyst is candid.** Their email meets the standard. They read the standard (beat 1 shows it). Asked "Better by whose standard?", a real analyst would most likely say "the firm's: bad news first", the same words the second analyst uses, and believe it. The figure has them say "The AI's notes, I suppose", which is the answer the figure needs. The page's closing text says, "The second line is a question they can answer, and the answer shows whose standard the help followed." A business reader will push back on that sentence. What actually separates them in the picture is the follow-up the figure doesn't show: what did you decide before asking, and what did you turn down? The struck recap is the visible evidence that the second analyst judged; the first analyst has nothing they turned down. Consider making that the reviewer's check, or adding it as the second question.
2. **Caption 5 suggests a second difference.** "A second analyst starts from the firm's standard," together with a much bigger highlight on the standard than in beat 1, implies the first analyst didn't. The page's own text says both read the same standard. The real difference is writing the decisions down before asking. Something like "A second analyst reads the same standard, then decides before asking" would keep the comparison clean. The standard should get the same highlight in beats 1 and 5.
3. **Wrong card pulses in the 1280×720 and 390 layouts.** In beat 7, when "Add a recap" is struck as "not in my outline", the narrow layouts pulse "New date, and why" (the criteria card), not "Date, reason, plan" (the outline card). I confirmed this with `?t=68.2` at 1280×720: the `is-pulse` class is on the criteria chip. The wide layout pulses the right card. In every layout the pulse lasts about half a second, which is short for the one visual link between a decision written in beat 6 and a rejection made in beat 7.
4. **At 390, nothing goes "across".** Beat 2's caption says "The request carries the criteria across", and beat 3 shows the same move, but the phone layout has no lanes: the card nudges right and greys in place. The key makes it decodable, but the caption doesn't match the picture. At 1280×720 the card does cross between the two columns.
5. **The one difference between the emails can't be seen.** The page says the first email keeps the AI's recap and the second "has no recap". In the wide thumbnails that is one line's colour in a roughly 50 px icon: the first has a grey line mid-email where the second has a blue one. Both thumbnails end in the same two grey lines, which look like a recap. The narrow layouts have no thumbnails at all. Near-identical emails suit the argument, but the description promises a difference the picture doesn't show. Also, the recap is drawn in the middle of the email, not at the end.
6. **The second analyst ticks "Formal phrasing" though their meaning of professional is "Clear and courteous".** A sharp reader will ask why formal phrasing passes their own test, especially beside "Cut the jargon". It is the one place where the careful analyst's judging looks unexamined. A note that fits "clear and courteous", or a second strike, would be cleaner.
7. **The second analyst's line is clunky.** "Ours, set first from our standard: bad news first" says "our" twice and blurs "my decisions" with "the firm's standard". Something like "Mine, written down before I asked, from the firm's standard: bad news first."
8. **Beat 6 is brisk.** Three write-then-ask cycles in about 8 s, in small red italic (13 px). I could follow it, but it is the fastest stretch.
9. **Wording that leans on agency.** "The AI used its own [standard]" and the lane name "The AI decides" credit the AI with intent; it filled in generic defaults, and the first analyst still ticked every note. This doesn't mislead, because the figure shows the ticks, but "the AI's defaults filled the gap" would be fairer to both.
10. **Source line.** "Illustration based on a 2025 study of knowledge workers' AI use" could be read as saying the study observed this scenario. It's a survey; it didn't compare two analysts. "Informed by" would be safer. The evidence section further down is careful and names its limits.
11. **Factual claims to source.** 319 workers, 936 examples, and the quoted passages with page numbers. Lee et al. isn't in `sources/source-spine.md` or the essay; see the mechanical checks.
12. **Trivial.** Before autoplay starts, the clock reads 0:12 while the figure shows the start. Holding exactly `?t=12.5` shows an empty caption bar.

## Business-edition questions

**Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work?** Yes. Telling a client that a project slips three weeks is everyday work in consulting, agencies, IT services, construction and nonprofit programme management, where the client is a funder or partner. A manager reviewing an analyst's client email before it goes out is realistic. "Cut the jargon / Formal phrasing / Add a recap" is exactly what an assistant returns. "Analyst / firm / client" leans towards professional services, and nonprofit readers will translate it without effort. One realism note: a manager seldom sees two versions of the same email. In practice they see one, which makes the point stronger.

**Straw man, second differences, access.** Neither analyst is a cartoon. The first reads the standard, makes sensible requests, ticks reasonable notes and produces an email that meets the standard. Both have the same task card and standard, the same AI, the same requests word for word, the same notes and the same number of drafts, so access is equal. Two second differences creep in: (a) caption 5 and the bigger highlight on the standard imply the first analyst didn't start from it (problem 2); (b) the first analyst answers the manager with convenient candour (problem 1). The first ticking every note is a consequence of the difference the figure is about, not a separate one.

**Can the deliverable separate them?** Mostly no, as the figure intends. Both end at "Draft 4, revised with AI", both meet the standard, and the thumbnails are near-identical. The one difference the page describes, a recap in one and not the other, doesn't reveal who decided: an analyst could choose a recap deliberately. But the question alone doesn't reliably separate them either, unless the first analyst is candid (problem 1). What separates them is a follow-up about what each decided before asking and what each turned down.

**Does the figure contradict its research, and is the source line clear?** The figure as I read it doesn't contradict what the evidence section reports. A low-stakes "make it professional" task done without critical thought matches the quoted P275, and checking whether the AI changed the main point matches P144. The difference shown, deciding first and then judging the help, matches the quoted "stewardship" passage. The headline finding quoted is about *confidence*, and the figure shows nothing about confidence, so the strongest quote is not the mechanism on screen. That isn't a contradiction, but it's a loose fit. The source line is short and clear; "based on" slightly overstates (problem 10). The evidence section names its limits plainly: self-report, correlations, one participant per quote, and the authors' affiliations. Fact check against the paper below.

**Military or classroom wording.** None inside the figure. On the page:
- the edition switcher shows "See this with: Workplace · PME", an unexplained acronym for a business reader (its hidden note reads "PME (professional military education)… the National War College essay");
- "Go further" lists "The original essay, written for military education: The Irreducible Officer, Section II", which is labelled honestly and is fine as provenance;
- the meta description begins "A teaching figure".

The figure's own labels ("Reviewer's check", "The manager asks", "Example task", "Standard") are business-neutral.

## After the read: the page's claims about Lee et al. (2025), checked against the paper

Source used: the Microsoft Research copy of the CHI '25 paper (`microsoft.com/en-us/research/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/lee_2025_ai_critical_thinking_survey.pdf`). It carries the ACM CHI '25 header, the CC-BY licence and DOI 10.1145/3706598.3713778. The ACM Digital Library PDF, which the page links via the DOI, returned 403 to a scripted download. I didn't try to get around that, so I couldn't confirm that the ACM copy's page numbering matches. The page says "Page numbers are the PDF's", and they match the Microsoft-hosted PDF.

| Page claim | Paper | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| "surveyed 319 knowledge workers" | Abstract, p. 1: "We survey 319 knowledge workers" | Correct |
| "who shared 936 first-hand examples" | p. 1: "Participants shared 936 first-hand examples of using GenAI in work tasks"; p. 6: 957 submitted, 936 retained after cleaning | Correct |
| "higher confidence in GenAI is associated with less critical thinking, while higher self-confidence is associated with more critical thinking" (p. 1) | Abstract, p. 1, word for word | Correct |
| P275, "It's a simple task [make a passage professional] … as critical thinking didn't feel relevant" (p. 11) | p. 11, word for word; the bracket is the paper's own | Correct |
| P144, "whether the AI added irrelevant content and if it changed up my main point of the letter" (p. 8) | p. 8, word for word | Correct |
| Stewardship: "translating intentions into queries, steering AI responses, and assessing if the AI response meets their quality standards for work, while retaining accountability for the work" (p. 13) | p. 13, word for word ("AI stewardship") | Correct |
| Authors, title, venue | Lee, Sarkar, Tankelevitch, Drosos, Rintel, Banks, Wilson; title as given; CHI '25 | Correct |
| "The authors are at Microsoft Research and Carnegie Mellon University" | Lee is at CMU, the other six at Microsoft Research | Correct |
| Limits: self-reported, perceived critical thinking, correlations | The title says "Self-Reported"; the limitations section (p. 15) flags possibly inaccurate self-reports; the results are correlations and regression, with no causal claim | Fair |

**Does the figure contradict the paper?** No.
- The difference it shows, deciding the criteria before asking and judging the AI's notes against them, fits the paper's qualitative account: goal and query formation, ensuring quality through objective criteria or subjective standards, and stewardship.
- The page applies "stewardship" to the second analyst's behaviour, a fair stretch.
- The paper's closest code to writing decisions down first is "Form goal (6/319)" on p. 8: "Before engaging with a tool, knowledge workers reflect on their goals, needs and intents." That would be a tighter citation than stewardship. It is a free-text mention count, not a measure of how often people do it.

**Two nuances the page leaves out:**
1. The headline finding concerns confidence (in the AI and in oneself), which the figure doesn't depict. The quote is accurate, but it isn't the mechanism on screen.
2. The authors write that reliance "may be tolerable for low-stakes tasks, like grammar checking". They add that applying critical thinking only when the stakes are high is risky (p. 11). A client-facing delay email isn't low-stakes, so the figure stands, but a reader who knows the paper might raise the point.

**Overclaiming?** The evidence section doesn't overclaim. The one soft spot is the figure's source line, "Illustration based on…" (problem 10). The paper is a survey of self-reported examples and contains no two-analyst comparison.
