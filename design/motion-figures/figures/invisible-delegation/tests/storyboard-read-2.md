# Invisible delegation: storyboard cold read 2

Tester: cold reader acting as a first-time educator who hasn't read the essay. Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed). October 27, 2026. Before writing sections 1–6 I didn't open `storyboard.md`, `storyboard-read-1.md`, any other figure's folder, the plan, the brief or the essay. The fact check at the end was done afterwards.

## 1. Beat by beat

### Beat 1 · 0–7 s · "A student wants to improve a draft strategy paper."

What I see: a task card ("Example task: recommend whether to keep a forward base open"), a red dot labelled "First student", and a sketch of a paper.

What I think it means: here's a student, their draft and the assignment.

It's clear, and 7 s is generous. "First student" tells me a second one is coming, which is fine. Small point: the paper carries no "Draft 1", and the counter starts at "DRAFT 2" in beat 3. I'd infer draft 1 without trouble.

### Beat 2 · 7–15 s · "Improving it takes many decisions. We'll track three."

What I see: three lanes ("THE STUDENT DECIDES", "REQUESTS", "THE AI DECIDES"), three dashed cards in the student's lane, and three empty slots in the AI's lane.

What I think it means: this is a ledger. These three decisions belong to the student and haven't been made yet. "We'll track three" frames them as a sample, which is good.

Unsure moment: a card sitting under a heading that says "THE STUDENT DECIDES" first reads as "the student has decided this". Only the dashed outline says otherwise, and the caption never says "not decided yet". I had it sorted by the end of the beat, and beat 6 confirmed it when the second student's cards turned solid red. For the first second or two, though, I read them as decided.

### Beat 3 · 15–26.1 s · "The student asks an AI for feedback. The request carries the criteria across."

What I see: the bubble "Give me feedback" clips onto the criteria card. The two cross slowly into "THE AI DECIDES", the card turns grey, and an empty slot is left behind. The bubble returns to the middle lane as a quote. The AI's notes appear on the paper, the dot revises, and "DRAFT 2" and "REVISED WITH AI" appear.

What I think it means: asking for feedback handed the AI the job of choosing the criteria. This is strong. It's slow, the card stays visible all the way, and the caption lands before the move.

Unsure moments:
- The bubble goes all the way to the AI's lane and then comes back to the middle. For a moment I read that as the AI handing the request back. After that I took it as the request being filed.
- The caption states the transfer as fact. My teacher reflex was: does it always? The task card already sets part of the standard (make a recommendation), and the essay excerpt at the bottom of the page says "may delegate". This is the first place I felt the figure overstating.

### Beat 4 · 26.1–37.1 s · "Then for a better structure, and a more strategic version."

What I see: two more requests, two faster crossings, grey headings and then a grey paragraph appearing in the paper, then draft 3 and draft 4.

What I think it means: the same thing happens for structure and for meaning, and the student keeps working.

The faster crossings are fine because the pattern is set. The elliptical caption ("Then for…") reads fine.

Unsure moment: I would probably miss the paper changing. The paper is a thumbnail at the far left, and my eyes are on the cards crossing in the centre and right. The grey headings and paragraph matter later ("the same in both papers"), so missing them here costs me in beat 7.

### Beat 5 · 37.1–45.7 s · "Each request left out whose standard to use. The AI used its own."

What I see: the three quote lines pulse. Grey words appear after each one: "against your criteria", "for your argument", ", as you define it". The grey cards pulse.

What I think it means: each request was incomplete, and the AI filled the gap with its own defaults. This is the clearest beat in the figure.

Unsure moments:
- "Your." On first read I took "against your criteria" as addressed to the student, like a teacher's margin note, which is the opposite meaning. It took the caption's "its own" and the grey colour to work out that "you" is the AI.
- The voice is mixed. The words are phrased as the student speaking to the AI, but they're coloured grey, which the colour rule says means "AI supplied". So who is saying them? I settled on "the unspoken end of the student's sentence", but it took a moment.
- "Standard" stretches to cover all three. An argument and a definition aren't really standards. That's acceptable in a caption.

### Beat 6 · 45.7–56.6 s · "A second student writes down each decision first, then asks for the same help."

What I see: the first block slides up and a second student appears. The dot writes in the criteria card, which turns red. It asks "Give me feedback against my criteria", and the bubble settles in the middle lane with a red pointer back to the card. Notes come back, the dot revises, and "DRAFT 2" appears.

What I think it means: this student decides first, then asks, and the decision stays home.

Unsure moments:
- The caption says "writes down each decision first, then asks", so I expected all three cards written first and then the requests. Instead I get one decision, one request and one revision, while two cards stay dashed. On first viewing the caption and the picture disagree. Beat 7 shows it's one decision per request.
- This bubble never goes to the AI's lane. With the first student, the crossing read as "sending the request to the AI", but here the request stops in the middle. Does the AI get it? The notes come back, so it must, but the picture changed its own rule for what sending looks like.
- The biggest one: the AI is asked to use "my criteria", and nothing in the picture gives the AI those criteria. The red card stays in the student's lane, and the pointer points back toward the student, away from the AI. As a teacher I'd ask whether the student pasted the criteria into the prompt. If they didn't, "against my criteria" means nothing to the AI. As drawn, the picture suggests the added clause is the fix.

### Beat 7 · 56.6–68.1 s · "Each request points to a decision already made. The AI helps just as much."

What I see: two more cycles of deciding and then asking. All three cards are red, the AI's lane is empty, the same grey headings and paragraph appear in this paper, and it reaches draft 4.

What I think it means: the help is the same, and the decisions stayed with the student.

Unsure moments:
- "Just as much" rests on small grey marks in two thumbnail papers far apart on screen. I'd take it on the caption's word.
- The AI wrote the headings, and choosing headings is a structural decision, yet the AI's lane stays empty. A sceptical colleague would say the AI decided less, or decided inside the student's frame, rather than not deciding at all. Beat 2's "We'll track three" covers this partly, but by beat 7 that framing is a minute old.

This beat is busy: two full cycles in 9 s, with 17 new picture words. I kept up only because it repeats beat 6.

### Beat 8 · 68.1–82.4 s · "Faculty have only the papers. They ask each student about the standard."

What I see: both ledgers dim. "FACULTY ASK EACH STUDENT / Better by whose standard?" appears top right. Two answers type out beside the blocks: "The AI's, I suppose. I'm not sure what good looks like here." and "Mine. I set it first: does the evidence back my call?"

What I think it means: faculty see two papers, both at draft 4 and both "revised with AI". The only difference shows up in what the students say. Dimming the ledgers is a good way to say "faculty can't see this". This is a strong beat.

Too fast, possibly: the text is in three places, the caption band at the bottom, the question top right, then a speech panel beside each block. The question appears at 70.2 s while I'm still reading the 12-word caption (about 4 s, so until roughly 72.1 s), and the first answer starts typing at 72.5 s. My eye goes from the bottom to the top right, then middle right, then lower right, in about five seconds. That's doable at the stated budget of 3.0 words a second, which is the ceiling, but this is the payoff beat and it's the most crowded.

Fairness:
- The first student's answer is a confession: "The AI's, I suppose." If the delegation was invisible to them, I'd expect "Mine, it's clearer now" or "It just reads better". A student who says "Mine" without having set anything would pass this check. The figure shows one question sorting the two students cleanly. What would actually make the check work is the follow-ups in the page copy ("feedback against which criteria…"), and the animation doesn't show them.
- The second student's standard, "does the evidence back my call?", is generic. An AI would propose the same criterion for any recommendation paper. So the contrast is about who set the standard, not whether it's any good. That's fine if it's intended, but a teacher may notice that the "good" student's standard is thin and covers only the criteria, not the argument or what "strategic" means.

### Beat 9 · 82.4–90 s · "Faculty check: What did the AI decide for the student?" / "To find out, ask each student: better by whose standard?"

What I see: the check rises into the caption band, the ledgers return to full strength, and then the second line appears at the same size.

What I think it means: here is the check, and here is how to run it. The ledgers coming back are the answer. The second line repeats beat 8's question, which works as reinforcement.

Unsure moments:
- The order is slightly backwards. The answer (the ledgers) comes back before the line telling me how to find it out. It does no harm.
- "The student" is singular when two are on screen. This is minor.
- The second line has 2.9 s before 90 s, but the figure rests on this frame, so that's fine.

## 2. The point

When students ask an AI for help without first settling their own criteria, argument and meaning, the AI's defaults quietly make those decisions. Because both papers come back "revised with AI", faculty can only find out by asking each student whose standard the work meets.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before you asked the AI for help, what had you decided would make this paper good, and is that what you used to judge the revision?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly, yes. The mirror image does the work. In the top block the student's lane is empty and three grey cards sit on the AI's side. In the bottom block three red cards sit on the student's side and the AI's lane is empty. The papers and tags are the same, and the two answers and the check are right there. Reading it slowly, I get: same help, a different owner of the decisions, and a question that tells the two apart.

What it says to me: "Two students got the same AI help and handed in the same 'revised with AI' paper. One let the AI make three decisions and the other made them first. Ask them whose standard they used."

Weaknesses in the still:
- **The grey unsaid words read as said.** Without beat 5, "Give me feedback against your criteria" looks like what the first student typed. That turns the first student into someone who deliberately asked for the AI's criteria, which is the opposite of invisible delegation. Nothing in the still labels those words as unsaid. The grey band is the only cue, and the colour key isn't on screen. The reduced-motion view is this frame, so reduced-motion viewers get this misreading unless they read beat-list item 5.
- **It's dense.** It holds two ledgers with three lanes and three rows each, two papers, a right column with five pieces of text, and a two-line check. A teacher would need a minute with it. That's acceptable for a figure people pause on, but not as a thumbnail.
- **It doesn't show the transfer.** The first student's cards simply sit in the AI's lane. The empty "—" slots in their own lane hint at it. That's enough.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The second student's decisions never reach the AI (confusion, and a misleading takeaway).** "Against my criteria", "for my argument" and "as I define it" only work if the criteria, argument and definition go into the prompt. In the picture the red card stays put and the red pointer points back to the student, away from the AI. A teacher or student could take away "add 'against my criteria' to your prompt" as the fix. Section V, quoted on the page, says structured prompting includes explicit purpose and evaluation criteria, which means the content goes in. Idea: have a red copy or excerpt of the card travel with the request while the original stays red in the student's lane. Or put the criterion text in the request itself, for example "against my criteria: does the evidence back my call?".
2. **In the final frame and the reduced-motion view, the grey unsaid words read as words the student said (ambiguous visual).** Without beat 5, "against your criteria" looks typed, and "your" can be read as the student's own criteria. Both readings flip the meaning. Idea: mark them as unsaid in the picture itself, with a small grey mono "UNSAID" tag, brackets, or a different treatment from quoted text, and test whether "your" survives a cold glance.
3. **The check looks cleaner than it is (unfair to students, and it overpromises).** The first student confesses on cue. On screen the student never fails to notice anything: the transfer is large and slow, and the student admits it as soon as they're asked. The invisibility the figure dramatizes is invisibility to faculty (the dim), not to the student, which is what the essay's definition is about ("the student does not notice"). A student who bluffs "Mine" would pass the single question shown. The follow-ups in the page copy are what make it a real check, and they aren't in the animation.
4. **The captions state as fact what the essay says "may" happen, and "its own" gives the AI preferences (unfair to plain requests and to the AI).** "The request carries the criteria across." "The AI used its own." The essay excerpt on the same page says "may delegate" four times. The fair objection (an assistant can legitimately propose criteria) appears only in the copy below the figure. A teacher who has used AI feedback well will push back here. Idea: "can carry", or "The AI filled the gap with its defaults."
5. **Beat 6's caption doesn't match its picture.** "Writes down each decision first, then asks" sounds like all three decisions and then all three requests. The picture shows one decision and one request, while two cards stay dashed.
6. **"The AI helps just as much" isn't visible, and the empty AI lane overstates.** The grey changes happen in thumbnail papers while attention is on the ledger, in both beat 4 and beat 7. And picking headings is deciding structure, so "supplies text without deciding" is a claim a colleague could dispute.
7. **Beat 8 is the payoff and the busiest beat (risk of being too fast).** There are three reading locations, the budget is at the 3.0 ceiling, and the question appears while the caption is still being read. Consider bringing in the question after the caption can be read, or adding a beat of rest before the first answer.
8. **The sending motion changes between students (ambiguous visual).** The first student's requests cross into the AI's lane and the second student's stop in the middle. That can read as the second student's request never reaching the AI. It compounds problem 1.
9. **The second student's standard is generic and covers only the criteria.** "Does the evidence back my call?" is what an AI would propose too. It works as a statement of ownership, but it's weak as a model of a good standard, and the answer says nothing about the argument or about "strategic".
10. **Smaller things:**
    - Beat 2: dashed cards under "THE STUDENT DECIDES" briefly read as already decided.
    - Beat 3: the bubble's return trip briefly reads as the AI handing the request back.
    - Layout to check at build: a bubble that grows "just above" row k's card will overlap row k−1, and for row 0 the lane headings. The second student's requests probably wrap to two lines, so their bubbles in rows 1 and 2 may briefly cover the red card above them.
    - The narrow-view threshold (a stage under about 880 px) may hand laptop viewers the text stage if the page's content column is narrower than that. Worth confirming what a typical laptop gets.
    - At 90 s with the payoff around 70 s, the figure is fine for someone who pauses but long for someone scrolling past.
11. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:**
    - the Section II excerpt (definition and request pairs);
    - the Section V excerpt;
    - the failure-mode lab's objection that an assistant can legitimately propose criteria;
    - the kicker "Failure mode 5 of 7" (the header says "Motion figure 5 of 8", so the essay's count and order are worth confirming);
    - the essay section anchors in the links.

    The picture shows no numbers.
