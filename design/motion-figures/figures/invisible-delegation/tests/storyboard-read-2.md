# Invisible delegation: storyboard cold read, round 2

Read October 28, 2026, from `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent section removed). I read it as an educator who hasn't read the essay. I didn't open `storyboard.md`, `storyboard-read-1.md`, the essay, or any other figure's folder before writing sections 1–5.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · The draft (0–7 s).** A task card ("Example task: Recommend whether to keep a forward base open"), a red dot labelled "First student", and a small paper. I take it to mean one student with their own draft and a real assignment. Clear. The 4.3 s hold is generous.

**Beat 2 · Three decisions (7–15 s).** A three-lane ledger appears: "THE STUDENT DECIDES" in red, "REQUESTS", "THE AI DECIDES" in grey. Three dashed cards sit in the student's lane, with empty "—" slots opposite. I read this as "these decisions belong to the student but aren't made yet." "We'll track three" tells me it's a sample. Clear. One small question: if nobody has decided yet, why do the cards already sit under "The student decides"? I settled on "that's where they belong by default," which I think is the intended reading, but nothing says so.

**Beat 3 · Feedback (15–26.1 s).** The student asks "Give me feedback", and the bubble clips onto the criteria card. After a pause long enough to read the caption, the two travel slowly into the AI's lane and the card turns grey. This is the strongest moment in the storyboard. The slow, unmissable move makes "the request carries the criteria across" literal. After that comes a cluster of small events at the far left of the stage: the AI's notes chip, ticks, the dot working, the chip fading, "DRAFT 2", "REVISED WITH AI", all in about 2.3 s. My eyes would still be on the grey card at the far right, so I'd probably catch "DRAFT 2" late and miss the notes chip. That's not fatal, because the counter and tag stay. One doubt at this beat: I accept that the criteria went across, but I don't yet know why a plain request for feedback should do that. The answer comes two beats later.

**Beat 4 · Structure and strategy (26.1–37.1 s).** Two more crossings, faster, each followed by grey AI text in the paper and a new draft number. I got the pattern. But the second request bubble appears 1.3 s into the beat and its card crosses at 1.8–3.2 s. That's while I'm still reading a 10-word caption *and* the 4-word bubble. Thirteen movements in 11 s makes this the busiest beat. Because the pattern is already set, I'd follow the gist, but I'd want to pause here.

**Beat 5 · The turn (37.1–45.7 s).** The three plain requests pulse, then grey words appear after each: "against your criteria", "for your argument", ", as you define it". The grey cards pulse. Caption: "Each request left out whose standard to use. The AI used its own." This is where it clicked for me: the unsaid half of each request was "by your standard", and the AI filled it in. I hesitated for a moment over "your". The words are coloured grey, which the colour rule says means AI-supplied, so for a second I read them as the AI speaking ("your" = the student's). The caption's "its own" resolved it. This is the key beat, yet it's one of the shortest, with a 2.1 s hold after the cards pulse. The grey phrases are small (italic 13) on a tinted band. I'd want another second or two here.

**Beat 6 · A second student writes first (45.7–56.6 s).** The first block slides up and an identical second block appears. The dot "writes in" the criteria card, which turns solid red. Only then does it ask "Give me feedback against my criteria", with red words and a red pointer back to the card. Same notes, same revision. The order (decide, then ask) comes through clearly, and the caption states it before it happens. One thing struck me: the second student's bubble never enters the AI's lane. It stops in the middle. In beat 3 the first student's request went all the way into the AI's lane and came back. So for a moment I wondered whether this request had reached the AI at all. The notes arriving answer that, but the two requests travel differently, and that difference could carry meaning nobody intended.

**Beat 7 · Each request points to a decision (56.6–68.1 s).** Two more decide-then-ask cycles, with red pointers, and the same grey headings and paragraph land in this paper. "The AI helps just as much." Clear and fair to the AI. By the end of the beat the two blocks read as mirror images: one student's lane is empty and the AI's lane is full, the other is the reverse. That diagonal is the figure's best visual idea.

**Beat 8 · Faculty ask (68.1–82.4 s).** Ledgers dim; the papers, counters and "Revised with AI" tags stay bright. "Faculty ask each student: Better by whose standard?" Then the two answers type out at reading pace. The dim is a good way to say "faculty can't see this part". The question fades in at 2.1 s, before I'd finish a 12-word caption that is about the question. That's minor, but it breaks the storyboard's own rule that a change the caption names waits until the caption can be read. Thirty-one picture words plus 12 caption words in 14.3 s is right at the stated budget. Because the answers appear at reading pace, it should be fine. But the first answer made me stop and think (see problem 2).

**Beat 9 · Faculty check (82.4–90 s).** "FACULTY CHECK: What did the AI decide for the student?" The ledgers return, then "To find out, ask each student: better by whose standard?" I understood the relationship: the check is what you want to know, and the second line is how you find it out. There are two questions in large italic, though, and one of them already appeared in the picture, so a teacher may not be sure which one is "the" takeaway. The figure rests here, so nothing is rushed.

## 2. The point

Generic requests like "give me feedback", "make it better structured" or "make it more strategic" quietly hand the AI the decisions about criteria, argument and meaning. Papers revised that way look identical to papers from a student who made those decisions first, so the only way to tell them apart is to ask the student whose standard the changes serve.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"Before the AI touched this, what did you decide 'better' would mean for this paper, and where did that standard come from?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. The mirror-image ledgers carry the core contrast without motion. On top, the student's lane is empty and the AI's lane holds three grey cards. Below, three red cards sit in the student's lane and the AI's lane is empty. The two answers beside the blocks give the human consequence, and the check under the picture says what to do. On its own the still tells me: two students got the same AI help; one kept the decisions and one didn't; you find out which by asking.

What the still loses:
- **The transfer itself.** Without the crossing, the top block can read as "the AI was assigned these decisions" rather than "the student's requests gave them away". The "—" slots hint that something left, but only faintly.
- **That the grey words were never said.** In the still, the top requests read as complete sentences: "Give me feedback against your criteria", "Make it more strategic, as you define it". Only the grey band marks those words as unsaid, and nothing on the frame explains the band. A teacher scanning the still could easily conclude the first student *asked* the AI to use its own criteria. That would be a deliberate delegation, the opposite of "invisible". This is the biggest risk in the still (problem 1).
- **Density.** About 150 words on one frame. It works as a reference diagram to study, not as a glance.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **Ambiguous visual / misread (high): the unsaid words read as said.** In the final frame (also the reduced-motion and print view) and in the narrow layout, the grey phrases join the quotes, so the first student appears to have literally written "against your criteria". The grey band is the only marker, and grey elsewhere means "AI-supplied", which fits awkwardly with words in the student's voice. A small "unsaid" tag, brackets, or a strikethrough-style treatment would stop the still from saying the opposite of the point. The pronoun adds a little friction too: in the student's voice "your" means the AI's, but in grey (AI-supplied) it can read as the student's.

2. **Unfair to students / logic gap (high): the first student's problem looks like missing knowledge, and the fix shown assumes it.** "I'm not sure what good looks like here" describes a learner who doesn't yet know the criteria. That is a legitimate reason to ask for feedback. The figure's remedy, "write down each decision first", only works for a student who can already state the criteria, so the two students may read as differing in ability rather than habit. Teachers will also bring in a third party the figure leaves out: the course and its rubric. Asked "better by whose standard?", "the assignment's" may be the honest right answer. The second student's "Mine" can read as putting a personal standard above the course's. Without saying where the instructor's criteria fit, a war-college or high-school teacher may push back on the core contrast.

3. **Ambiguous visual / logic (medium-high): the same AI text in both papers.** Both papers get identical grey headings and paragraph, and "The AI helps just as much". I see why: faculty must not be able to tell the papers apart. But it also says that setting the criteria first changed nothing in the work, which weakens "the AI decided the argument" for the first student. A sharp viewer will ask: if a structure suggested "for my argument" produced the same headings as one suggested for the AI's argument, what did the decision change? Consider making the papers look equally polished but not literally identical, or have the caption carry "faculty can't tell them apart".

4. **Too fast (medium): beat 4.** The second crossing runs while I'm reading both the caption and the bubble, and the beat packs 13 movements into 11 s. Beat 3's revision cluster (about six small events in 2.3 s, at the opposite side of the stage from where the eye is) has the same problem on a smaller scale.

5. **Too fast (medium): beat 5, the turn, is under-held.** The figure's key idea gets 8.6 s, with a 2.1 s hold after the final pulse, and its new words are the smallest, lowest-contrast text on the stage.

6. **Mismatch between figure and page copy (medium).** The page copy concedes a fair objection: an assistant can legitimately propose criteria, and the trouble is accepting them without checking them against the task. The animation shows only "write it first" versus "don't". The legitimate middle path (ask the AI for criteria, then judge them) never appears, so the figure's prescription is stricter than the page's own standard. Teachers who read both will notice.

7. **Ambiguous visual (low-medium): the requests travel differently.** The first student's bubble goes into the AI's lane and comes back; the second student's stops in the middle. That can read as "the second request never reached the AI".

8. **Scope of the faculty question (low-medium).** "Better by whose standard?" probes the criteria. It doesn't obviously surface who decided the argument or what "strategic" means. A coached student can also just say "Mine". The page copy's follow-ups handle this; the figure doesn't hint at them.

9. **Caption timing (low): beat 8.** The question appears 2.1 s in, before the caption that names it can be read once. That breaks the storyboard's own rule, and the pacing checks don't list it as a lead-in.

10. **Colour coding (low).** Red usually signals error. Here red means "the student decided", and the good student's lane ends fully red. The captions carry it, but a first glance at the still may read the bottom block as flagged.

11. **Two takeaway questions (low).** Beat 9 shows two large italic questions, and one repeats the faculty question from beat 8. That's fine once you see that one is the check and the other is the method, but a teacher could be unsure which to remember.

12. **Spec and layout risks (low; for the builder):**
    - "REVISED WITH AI" in mono 12 at x 24 runs to about x 132, 3 units from the row-2 card at x 135.
    - The "AI'S NOTES" chip is wider than the 80-unit paper.
    - The second student's longer bubbles (Serif 14) may need two lines, but the gap between card rows is 30 units, so a two-line bubble would overlap the card or heading above.
    - "What “strategic” means" may wrap to two lines inside a 30-unit card that also has a bar under the label.
    - In the narrow layout, "At the turn the grey unsaid words join the quote" could mean beat 3–4 or beat 5.
    - "In beat 8 the panels dim" would also dim the counter and tag, which stand in for the papers. That's the opposite of the desktop logic, where what faculty have stays bright.

13. **Numbering (low).** The header says "Motion figure 5 of 8" and the kicker says "Failure mode 5 of 7". That may be correct, but a reader who sees both will wonder.

14. **Factual claims I'd want sourced (low).** The figure itself shows no numbers. On the page, I'd want to confirm:
    - the two essay quotations and their section titles;
    - that the companion's failure-mode lab really raises the "assistant can legitimately propose criteria" objection.
