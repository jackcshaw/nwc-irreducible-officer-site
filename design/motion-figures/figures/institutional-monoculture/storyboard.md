# Institutional monoculture: storyboard

Motion figure 6 of 8 · slug `institutional-monoculture` · storyboard round 5, revised after figure cold read 2 · October 28, 2026

Starting point: `concepts-v0/proposal.md` §2.6, "Turn the seminar" (9.4 s, sketched in CSS 3D in `concepts-v0/monoculture.html`). This version keeps the sketch's idea: the class's papers really do differ on the surface, and the sameness sits in how each one defines the problem, which only shows when you read them a different way. It drops the 3D turn for a flat move faculty can make themselves: lining up the one-line problem statement from every paper. The papers never move or merge; only copies of their problem lines do. The unit is the seminar, so the contrast is seminar to seminar: a thought experiment that imagines the same ten students working in the order the essay's pilot proposes, defining the problem first and then asking the AI to challenge it. Faculty then ask what other frames the task could take, in a beat of its own before the imagined seminar, and the question stays open to the end. The shared runtime and `CONVENTIONS.md` apply throughout.

## 1. Intent

*The cold-read tester does not see this section.*

**The point.** When every student in a seminar asks similar AI tools for help in a similar way, their papers can differ in evidence, structure and layout while all setting out to solve the same problem, the one the AI's outlines opened with. Each paper holds up and nobody copied, so no single paper shows it; the seminar's range of frames has narrowed. Faculty see which frames the seminar has by lining up the problem each paper defines and judging the goal each sets (the wording differs; the goal doesn't), and look for the missing ones by asking what other frames the task could take. The essay proposes a change to test: students define the problem first and then ask the AI to challenge it. Imagined that way, the same seminar might spread across several frames.

**What a first-time educator should be able to say afterwards.** "Ten students got the same open task, with context about trade, ports, an election, minerals and a rival's loans. Each asked an AI for an outline, and every outline opened the same way: counter the rival's influence. Each student wrote their own paper, with different evidence and a different shape, and each one held up when I read it. When I lined up the problem each paper defined, they were all worded differently: outbid rival lenders, win back trade, deny them mines. Reading them, every one set out to counter the rival. Different words, one frame, and it was the frame every AI outline opened with. I asked what other frames the task could take, and that question stayed open. Nobody copied anybody; each kept a reasonable default. Then the figure imagined the seminar the way the essay proposes: each student defines the problem first, then asks the AI to challenge it ('What did you assume? What else could it be?'). Those papers held up too, and in that one possible spread their problems fell under three goals: counter the rival, back the partner's goals, secure what we need. My real seminar can't be rerun, and nobody has shown that framing first spreads a seminar's frames. So I'd ask what frames are missing across the seminar: line up each paper's problem to see which frames are there, then ask what other frames the task could take, from my own knowledge or the class's."

**Faculty check** (verbatim from the site's Core Failure Modes table): *What frames are missing across the seminar?* The final frame adds, directly under it and at the same size, the move the figure makes and a faculty member can run: *Line up each paper's problem, then ask what other frames the task could take.*

**Essay passage** (Section II, "What the Problem Looks Like"):

> **Institutional monoculture** is the class-level version of the same problem. When many students use similar systems, similar prompts, and similar defaults, the range of strategic frames available to a seminar narrows. AI can create a surface appearance of diversity (different arguments, different structures, different evidence) while reproducing common assumptions at the level of problem definition. Research into the diversity of AI-generated ideas finds that language models aggregate knowledge into a unified distribution in ways that human cognition does not: people exhibit knowledge partitioning, each occupying a distinct semantic region, in ways that independent AI samples do not replicate ([Deng, Brucks & Toubia, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20408)). Post-training alignment compounds this further, compressing the distribution of outputs toward the statistical center ([Murthy, Ullman & Hu, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04427)). Pedagogical research on LLM integration in higher education frames this compression as epistemic narrowing: the constraining of students' exposure to diverse, ambiguous, or contested knowledge by tools optimized for convergence and fluency ([Vendrell & Johnston, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2026.100572)). In a war college seminar, that compression raises a serious possibility: the same systems that help students produce stronger work may also narrow the range of strategic imagination the seminar is meant to develop.

**3D or flat: flat.** Jack asked whether the 3D turn earns its place or a flat version teaches as well. For this figure the flat version teaches better, for five reasons. Both cold reads bore this out: the testers called the line-up the strongest beat and read every line.
1. *Faculty can't turn a seminar.* They can read across it. The turn is a view only the viewer can take; lining up each paper's problem statement is a move faculty make with work they already receive (Section VIII's frame evidence). Figures 1–5 kept learning that the difference has to be observable by faculty. In the flat version the mechanism and the faculty move are one action.
2. *Depth was a mapping the viewer had to accept on the caption's word.* In v0, depth stood for "how each essay defines the problem". The proposal flagged it as risk 3 and as the point its author was least confident about. In the lineup the problems are on screen in words, and faculty read each and bracket the goal they share, so the caption's claim is something the viewer can check by reading.
3. *The 3D frames had nothing to read.* v0's final frame is an edge-on plane of tiny cards beside four unlabelled dashed planes, "Frames no one chose". The flat board names every frame as a goal, so the final frame works as poster, print and reduced-motion view, and the stacked text stage can carry it at 390 px.
4. *v0 had no people, no AI and no faculty in the picture.* The flat version puts the AI's outlines and challenges in grey, the students' own problem lines in red and faculty's reading in navy, so the cause, the proposed change and the check are all in the still.
5. *Shared runtime.* The figures are SVG with the legibility rule, the stacked narrow stage and the poster. CSS 3D would need its own path; v0 notes it flattens under opacity and filter ancestors and was never checked in Safari.

What flat keeps from the 3D idea: the papers never collapse. They stay where they are and stay different; only copies of their problem lines move, so the sameness appears because faculty read differently, while the work itself doesn't change. v0's empty planes survive as one dashed bracket with a question mark, set in open space: the frames faculty still have to ask about.

**Misreadings to design against.**

1. *AI is the problem; keep it out of seminar.* In the imagined seminar the AI challenges each student's frame and suggests others, both seminars' papers say "All drafted with AI", and the AI panel stays on screen to the end.

3. *The rival frame is wrong.* Nothing marks it wrong. In the imagined seminar three students choose it themselves. The other frames are absent from the first seminar, and the figure never says they are better.
4. *A shared frame is a shared word.* The ten lines are worded differently, only three contain "rival", and "Win back trade" names no one. Faculty read each line (a reading mark appears beside it) and then bracket the goal. There is no underline under a repeated word. The page copy says that telling real frames apart takes reading.
5. *Someone copied.* Ten different layouts and ten differently worded goals.
6. *The students were lazy.* Each writes their own paper, and faculty read each one and find it holds up. The ticks stay on every paper to the final frame. No student is singled out; all ten do the same reasonable thing.
7. *Faculty could spot it in any one paper.* Beat 3: each paper holds up on its own. Beat 10: the imagined seminar's papers hold up too and look just as varied, so the papers can't tell the two apart.
8. *Framing first is proven to diversify a seminar.* Beat 8's caption says "Imagine the essay's proposal", the block is labelled "imagined", its board is headed "IMAGINED: ONE POSSIBLE SPREAD", and the page copy says the essay proposes framing first and whether it spreads frames across a seminar is untested.
9. *The imagined goals are the answer to the check.* The faculty's question comes first, in its own beat, and stays empty to the end; it sits in the first seminar's open space, over the gap between imagined columns. The imagined board is headed as one possible spread.
10. *Agreement is the failure.* The check asks what is missing. The page copy carries the failure-mode lab's objection: convergence can reflect a justified shared purpose, and a seminar can compare frames and still agree.
11. *The fix is for faculty to assign frames.* Section IV warns that when faculty hand students a precisely bounded question, faculty have already made the hard strategic choices. The move line asks faculty to ask; in the imagined seminar the students write their own problems.
12. *The fix is a different AI tool.* The page copy cites Kim et al. and Jiang et al.: different vendors give strikingly similar open-ended answers.
13. *Faculty knew the answer.* Faculty line up all ten papers in both seminars. Their question stays a question.
16. *The AI's challenge is another outline.* The imagined seminar's AI reply is a speech bubble of questions ("What did you assume? What else could it be?"), shaped differently from beat 2's outline sheets, and it lands under the student's own red line.
14. *Lining up tells you which frames are missing.* The move line splits the job: lining up shows which frames are there; asking what other frames the task could take looks for the rest.
15. *This is frame capture again, ten times.* Each student's frame is reasonable and each paper holds up. The failure appears only across the seminar, as one bracket around ten lines, and the check asks about the class.

**Why this scenario.** The lab's monoculture case has three authored AI-tutoring options that share a success standard; a viewer can't follow it without the essay, and it has no class in it. The essay's PME passage gives no concrete example; the HE and high-school editions do ("the evidence is mixed; hybrid is the balance" and "mixed but largely successful"). The figure keeps the PME setting of figures 1–5 with a new task. A partnership with a country a rival is lending to gives goals a non-specialist can tell apart: counter the rival, back the partner's own goals, secure what we need. The task names only the partnership, and the context gives every item an owner, so opening with the rival is the AI's choice. It is also a reasonable one, so nobody is wrong to keep it. The task, the context, the seminar, the outlines, the papers and every problem line are constructed, and the task card says "Example task".

**Why all ten.** The essay says "many" students. The figure shows all ten in one frame so that no student stands out from the others: if eight kept the frame and two didn't, the eight would read as weaker students, and the lesson would become individual. The page copy uses the essay's "many".

**Why a thought experiment.** A different seminar would carry different students and different knowledge, a second difference. Imagining the same ten students isolates the change. It is labelled as imagined because no real seminar can be rerun and no study has measured the effect, and the ending gives faculty a move that works without either.

## 2. Scenario

A war-college seminar of ten students gets one open task: recommend a strategy for our partnership with Country A. The context lists our trade, its ports, its election, its minerals and a rival's loans. Each student asks an AI for an outline, and the outlines come back alike. Each student writes their own paper, with different evidence, structure and layout, and faculty find that each one holds up. Lined up, the papers' one-line problems are worded differently ("Outbid rival lenders", "Win back trade", "Deny them mines"), and faculty judge that every one sets the same goal: counter the rival. Every AI outline had opened with that frame. Faculty then ask what other frames the task could take, and the question stays open. Last, the figure imagines the seminar working the way the essay proposes: each student defines the problem first, then asks the AI to challenge it. Those papers hold up too, and in this one possible spread their problems fall under three goals: counter the rival, back the partner's goals, and secure what we need.

*Sources.* The task, the context, the seminar, the outlines, the papers and every problem line are constructed for this figure, and the task card says "Example task". *The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, defines institutional monoculture: similar systems, prompts and defaults narrow the range of frames available to a seminar, behind a surface of different arguments, structures and evidence. Section VIII asks each student to name the problem frame before submitting; in the figure each paper's first line stands for it, and lining the lines up across a class is the figure's own application. Section IX's pilot has students write a problem frame without AI, then ask an AI to find assumptions they missed and generate alternative frames; the imagined seminar follows those two steps. The essay proposes that order; whether it spreads a seminar's frames is untested. The figure shows no numbers and plots no study.

The figure's imagined seminar follows the essay's proposal for a framing assignment: each student writes a problem frame without AI, then asks an AI to find assumptions they missed and suggest other frames (Section IX, steps one and two). Lining up the problems across a seminar is this figure's way of applying the essay's per-student frame evidence (Section VIII) to a class. **From the essay** (*The Irreducible Officer*, Section II, What the Problem Looks Like):

> **Institutional monoculture** is the class-level version of the same problem. When many students use similar systems, similar prompts, and similar defaults, the range of strategic frames available to a seminar narrows. AI can create a surface appearance of diversity (different arguments, different structures, different evidence) while reproducing common assumptions at the level of problem definition. Research into the diversity of AI-generated ideas finds that language models aggregate knowledge into a unified distribution in ways that human cognition does not: people exhibit knowledge partitioning, each occupying a distinct semantic region, in ways that independent AI samples do not replicate ([Deng, Brucks & Toubia, 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.20408)). Post-training alignment compounds this further, compressing the distribution of outputs toward the statistical center ([Murthy, Ullman & Hu, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.04427)). Pedagogical research on LLM integration in higher education frames this compression as epistemic narrowing: the constraining of students' exposure to diverse, ambiguous, or contested knowledge by tools optimized for convergence and fluency ([Vendrell & Johnston, 2026](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.caeai.2026.100572)). In a war college seminar, that compression raises a serious possibility: the same systems that help students produce stronger work may also narrow the range of strategic imagination the seminar is meant to develop.

**The evidence.** The essay cites three sources for this failure mode: Deng, Brucks & Toubia (2026) on language models aggregating knowledge where people partition it; Murthy, Ullman & Hu (2024) on alignment compressing outputs toward the statistical center; and Vendrell & Johnston (2026). "Epistemic narrowing" is the essay's term, describing Vendrell & Johnston; the companion's source notes cite that paper for its advice on sequencing AI-free and AI-assisted work. Studies the essay doesn't cite bear on this too, within limits:
- Romasanta, Thomas & Levina (HBR, 2026) report that across thousands of simulations, leading models gave the same trendy strategy advice regardless of context, defaulting to options such as differentiation, augmentation and collaboration. It is the closest of these to strategy work. It is a practitioner article, and its methods are visible only through the authors' summary.
- Doshi & Hauser (Science Advances, 2024) found that AI story ideas made individual stories better rated and made the stories more similar to one another. It measured stories, not strategies.
- Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg (ICML, 2025) and Jiang et al. (NeurIPS, 2025) found that when two models both get a question wrong they usually give the same wrong answer, and that different vendors give strikingly similar open-ended answers. Asking a second model is not an independent check.
- Counter-evidence: Terwiesch et al. (Production and Operations Management, 2026) found that better prompting, assigned personas and pooling several vendors brought the diversity of AI-generated ideas close to human levels. The companion's source notes read this as sameness being the default, with deliberate design needed to escape it. The study is about prompting AI for ideas; it doesn't test a seminar or the essay's pilot.
