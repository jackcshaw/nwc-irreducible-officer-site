# Institutional monoculture: storyboard cold read, round 1

October 28, 2026 · reader: cold-read tester, reading as an educator new to the essay · material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed)

Sections 1–6 were written before I opened the essay or the source spine. The fact check at the end came afterwards.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 (0–8 s).** A task card ("Example task: Recommend a strategy as a rival courts a partner country"), then ten blank papers with red dots, labelled "The seminar". I read: ten students, one assignment. It's clear, and the 5 s hold is generous. I noticed one thing already: the task's grammatical subject is the rival. "As a rival courts a partner country" puts the rival first and makes it the one acting. I half expected students to start from the rival with or without AI (problem 2).

**Beat 2 (8–16 s).** A grey "AI" box appears, a line runs from every dot up to it, and ten grey outlines drop back, all in one pattern. I read: everyone asked the same tool and got near-identical scaffolds. Clear. One AI box for ten students reads as "one shared tool". The caption's "an AI" could mean each student picks their own, but the picture says one. That's fine for the point, but a skeptical teacher's first reaction will be "so tell them to use different tools", and nothing in the picture answers it (problem 8).

**Beat 3 (16–25.5 s).** The papers turn from grey to ink in ten different layouts. One grey row stays in each, and the first line stays an ink bar. "ALL DRAFTED WITH AI" appears. A navy ring steps across and ticks each paper. I read: real, varied, disclosed work that passes faculty review one paper at a time. At 0.2 s per paper the ring is quick, but it reads as a sweep, which is fine. I wasn't sure what the kept grey row was. It looks like "some AI text survives in every paper", which is honest, but it becomes a puzzle in beat 6.

**Beat 4 (25.5–39.5 s).** The ticks fade, the header "EACH PAPER'S PROBLEM" appears, and each paper's first line lifts out and becomes words in a list, from "The rival's loans… ports… media…" through "cables". The papers stay put. I read: faculty stop reading through each paper and start reading across them. This is the strongest beat, and the pacing (0.6 s per three-word line, then a 5 s hold) let me read every line. I hesitated twice. (a) When the ticks faded, it looked like the approval was being withdrawn, as if the papers now failed. (b) I wondered where a real paper's one-line problem statement comes from. The figure treats it as a given first line, and nothing on screen says it's an assignment requirement.

**Beat 5 (39.5–48 s).** The last word of each line brightens, an underline runs under "The rival's", and a bracket closes around all ten: "ONE FRAME: THE RIVAL". Caption: "Different details, one frame…". It's clear and satisfying, and the underline is the right gesture.

**Beat 6 (48–56.5 s).** The AI panel shows "EACH OUTLINE OPENED WITH / The rival's influence". The bracket turns grey and becomes "AI'S FRAME: THE RIVAL", and each paper's first line gets a grey band. Caption: "Each AI outline opened with that frame, and each student kept it." I got the reveal, but the colours tripped me. In beat 3 the first line was ink (the student's writing), and a *different* row was the grey one they kept. Now the first line goes grey too. Did they keep two things? The band also covers the whole line, which says the whole line was the AI's. But the words ("loans", "ports") were the student's, and only the frame "The rival's…" came from the outline. My eye also had to jump from the panel at top left to the bracket at right, though the 3 s gap helps.

**Beat 7 (56.5–69 s).** The first run shrinks into the top half, its list becoming a column under "THE RIVAL" with "AI'S FRAME" beneath. "The same seminar, replayed" arrives below. Red lines come first, then requests, then outlines under the red lines, then writing, and the sequence label builds "DEFINE PROBLEM → AI OUTLINE → WRITE". I read: same everything, with one step added up front. The caption makes the idea clear. The picture, though, is the busiest in the figure: a layout change, a label split, six movements in 10 s and a 1.5 s hold, with the papers now 48 × 62. I'd lose track of the first run's board during the shrink.

Two more things. The replay's request lines and outlines travel between the bottom grid and the AI panel at top left, so they cross the first run's papers, which makes it look as if the first run is involved again. And the AI panel still says each outline opened with "The rival's influence", so I couldn't tell whether the replay students gave the AI their problem line or just wrote it above whatever came back.

**Beat 8 (69–82.5 s).** Both grids brighten. The replay's red lines lift out, turn ink and sort into four columns: THE RIVAL (3), THE PARTNER (3), OUR INTERESTS (2) and THE COST (2). Brackets close. Caption: "The papers look just as varied. Faculty line up the problems again." I read: the same surface variety, but four frames instead of one. Two notes. (a) The caption never states the result. Beat 5's caption said "one frame", but this one leaves "four frames" to the picture. (b) The lines are small (12.4 units), arrive every 0.5 s and jump between columns in student order: student 7 goes to column 3, students 8–9 to column 4, and student 10 back to column 3. On a first viewing I'd read the column labels and miss most of the lines. The final frame rescues it.

**Beat 9 (82.5–90 s).** "FACULTY CHECK: What frames are missing across the seminar?" appears. Then dashed, empty brackets appear on the first run's board under THE PARTNER, OUR INTERESTS and THE COST, labelled "MISSING". Then "To find out, line up the problem each paper defines." I read: the question, then the answer drawn for me, then the method. This is where I got stuck.

The "missing" frames are knowable only because the figure ran a replay, and a real seminar has no replay. If I line up my seminar's problems and see ten "The rival's…" lines, I learn there's one frame. I don't learn which ones are missing. The second line promises that lining up will find out what's missing, but it can only find out what's there. "The seminar" also points at the figure's seminar, which the picture has just answered, so the check reads more like a recap than a question for my class.

## 2. The point

When every student starts from the same AI outline, a seminar can produce ten different-looking papers that each hold up yet all define the problem the same way, and faculty see it only by lining up the problem statements side by side. Having students define the problem before they open the AI brings back a range of frames.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"If I lined up the one-line problem statement from every paper in my last assignment, how many different framings would I find? And did my students settle on theirs before the AI handed them one, or after?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes. The two blocks and their sequence labels ("AI OUTLINE → WRITE" against "DEFINE PROBLEM → AI OUTLINE → WRITE") do the heavy lifting. One grey bracket with three dashed MISSING frames, sitting above four filled ones, is a clear before and after. The still says that the order changed and the seminar went from one frame, the AI's, to four.

What the still loses:
- **"Each paper holds up."** The final frame has no ticks. Half the argument is that grading one paper at a time passes every paper, and the still doesn't show it. It reads only as "a one-frame seminar against a four-frame seminar", not "a seminar that passed review and still had one frame".
- **Accuracy in the grey bands.** The top block's first lines are grey-banded and the bottom block's are red. That reads as "the AI wrote the first run's problem statements", which isn't what happened.
- **Legibility.** It has twenty papers, five brackets, twenty lines at 12.4 units, two sequence labels, the AI panel, the task card and three lines of caption. It's orderly, but on a laptop the papers will be too small to compare their layouts. I'd have to take the caption's word that they're "just as varied".

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The check's second line overclaims, and the page copy says so.** The check reads "What frames are missing across the seminar? To find out, line up the problem each paper defines." Lining up shows which frames are present, not which are missing. The figure gets "MISSING" only from a replay that no real seminar has. The standalone copy gets this right: "Lining up the problems shows how many frames a seminar has. Naming the missing ones takes faculty judgment, or students who define the problem before they open the AI". So the caption and the page disagree. A teacher who follows the caption will line up their papers, see one frame, and still not know what's missing.
   - Fix: make the second line the honest version (lining up shows how many frames there are, and naming the missing ones is the faculty move).
   - Or ask a first line that lining up can answer, such as "How many frames does your seminar have?"
   - *Type:* confusion; the caption doesn't match the method.
2. **The task wording could be the cause, not the AI.** "Recommend a strategy as a rival courts a partner country" makes the rival the subject and the one acting. A thoughtful teacher will say that ten students without AI might also have opened with the rival. In the replay the rival is still the largest group, which fits that reading. This weakens beat 6's "AI'S FRAME" and is unfair to the AI.
   - Fix: reword the task so it doesn't lead with the rival, e.g. "Recommend a strategy toward a partner country that a rival is courting", or name the partner first.
   - Or have the page copy acknowledge that a task's wording frames too.
   - *Type:* unfair to AI; confound.
3. **The ticks fade and never return.** When the ticks fade at the start of beat 4, it reads as approval withdrawn, which is unfair to students and contradicts "each holds up". The final still also has no sign that every paper passed.
   - Fix: keep the ticks, dimmed if needed, through the final frame in both runs (the replay's papers get ticks too). The still then says "passed one by one; one frame together".
   - *Type:* ambiguous visual; the final frame loses half the point.
4. **The grey row and the grey first line (beats 3 and 6).** Each paper has two different grey things, and the first line turns from ink to grey after the fact. Is the kept row the frame? Is the first line the student's or the AI's?
   - Fix: make the first line the kept grey row from beat 3, so beat 6 only labels what was already grey.
   - Or band only the frame "The rival's…" on the board lines, the way the underline already does, so the student's own word stays ink.
   - *Type:* ambiguous visual; conflicts with the colour rule.
5. **Beat 7 is too busy, and its request lines cross the first run.** It packs a layout change, a label split and six movements into 10 s, with a 1.5 s hold. The replay's requests and outlines travel through the top block on their way to and from the AI panel.
   - Fix: lengthen the hold, or split the beat (move and arrive; then define, ask and write).
   - Route the replay's lines around the top block, or dim the top block while they travel.
   - *Type:* too fast; ambiguous visual.
6. **Beat 8's lines are small and fast, and its caption skips the result.** Lines at 12.4 units arrive every 0.5 s and jump between columns, and the caption never says "four frames".
   - Fix: a caption that names the contrast, e.g. "…Lined up again, they fall into four frames."
   - Space the lines 0.6 s apart, as in beat 4.
   - *Type:* too fast; the caption doesn't name what's shown.
7. **Sorting frames by first word teaches a shallow test.** Beat 8's Notice says the problems "sort by their first words". A teacher might take away "check who owns the first noun in the problem statement". THE COST is also a different kind of category from the three actors (rival, partner, us). A figure needs a readable shorthand, so this isn't fatal.
   - Fix: say on the page that the first word is the figure's shorthand, and that telling real frames apart takes reading.
   - *Type:* ambiguous concept.
8. **One AI box invites "just use different AIs".** The picture shows one shared tool. The evidence block says a second model isn't an independent check (Kim et al.; Jiang et al.), but the picture and captions don't. It's fine to leave this on the page, but it's the first objection a teacher will raise.
   - *Type:* likely objection.
9. **Red as the student colour.** Against the grey lines above, the replay's red first lines can read as red-pen corrections, as if the replay's papers had been marked up. The red dots and the red "DEFINE PROBLEM" label probably carry it, but I noticed.
   - *Type:* minor ambiguity.
10. **The AI panel's line sits over both runs.** "Each outline opened with: The rival's influence" also sits above the replay, so it says the replay's outlines opened with the rival too. If that's intended (same AI, different order), it's subtle and nice. If the replay students gave the AI their problem line, it's wrong.
    - Fix: decide which, and make a caption or Notice line say so.
    - *Type:* ambiguous.
11. **Claims I'd want sourced.**
    - From the essay: the Section II definition and quote; "Section VIII asks each student to name the problem frame before submitting"; "Section IX's pilot starts with a problem frame written without AI"; the kicker "Failure mode 6 of 7".
    - The three studies the essay cites: Deng, Brucks & Toubia 2026; Murthy, Ullman & Hu 2024; Vendrell & Johnston 2026.
    - The four workplace studies: Doshi & Hauser 2024; Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg 2025; Jiang et al. 2025; Terwiesch et al. 2026.
    - "The companion's failure-mode lab adds a fair objection."
    - These are checked below.

What didn't need fixing:
- Beats 1–6 are paced well, and every caption was readable before the change it describes.
- Nothing in the scenario blames a student or treats AI use as cheating. "All drafted with AI" in both runs is a good, fair touch.
- The replay keeps the rival as the largest frame, which is fair to the students who chose it.
