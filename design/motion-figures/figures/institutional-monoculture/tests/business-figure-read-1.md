## 2. The point, in one sentence

Ten plans can each pass review and still be one answer in ten outfits, because the AI gave every analyst the same opening frame; the manager's job is to line up the problem statements and ask what frames nobody considered.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"If I lined up the one-line problem statements from my team's last batch of AI-assisted proposals, how many distinct frames would I find? And which obvious ones (compete on cost, partner, license, walk away) did nobody put on the table?"

## 4. The final frame alone

It carries the idea. At 1280 wide I get: ten ticked plans, all drafted with AI, whose problems sit in one bracket labelled "ONE FRAME: DIFFERENTIATE", tagged "AI'S FRAME", and traced to "EVERY OUTLINE OPENED: Differentiate on value". Below that, the same team imagined, with three bracketed frames, one of them still differentiate. Then the definition and the check question. Without the animation it says: *your team's variety may be cosmetic; check how many frames are actually on the table.*

Weak spots in the still:
- The "AI" box at top left is empty and connected to nothing.
- "THE MANAGER ASKS ?" is an empty box. As the reviewer's prompt it reads well enough, but it's also an open loop.
- The top half is grey and labelled "AI's frame", while the bottom is navy and varied. On its own the still leans towards "top bad, bottom good". The caveat that a different frame isn't better by itself appears only in the page text below the figure.
- The source line has no "constructed" in it (see Problems, item 3).

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The manager's question goes nowhere in the picture.** Beat 7's caption lands about 2.6 s before its box. The box stays empty to the end, and the next beat cuts to an imagined rerun. I wasn't sure whether the manager's question went unanswered, or whether the rerun was meant to be its answer. The page text separates them clearly: the manager asks, drawing on their own market knowledge, and framing first is a separate proposal. The picture doesn't. On the phone, the "?" has no label at all.
2. **"How would I know that?"** Beat 6 gives the manager something a manager rarely has: sight of what every analyst's AI outline opened with. In practice I see finished plans, not the prompts or outlines behind them. A sceptical strategy lead will ask how they would learn this. The check question itself ("line up the problems") doesn't need it, so the figure could say that the lineup alone shows one frame, and that the AI-outline link is the explanation rather than something the manager can see.
3. **The source line in the final frame can travel without its caveat.** "Illustration based on Romasanta et al., HBR (2026), and Bottesch, ACIS (2025)." is clear, but if someone screenshots the final frame into a deck (business readers will), nothing in the picture says the team, plans and frames are constructed. The setup text says so, but it doesn't travel with a screenshot. Something like "Constructed illustration, informed by …" would hold up better. "Based on" slightly oversells, since neither study lined up a team's plans, as the page itself says.
4. **Military wording sits above the figure.** Right under the setup, on the Workplace edition, it says: "PME (professional military education): the example from *The Irreducible Officer*, the National War College essay." The edition toggle reads "Workplace · PME", and PME is unexplained jargon to me until that note. Below the figure, "military education" appears three more times (the provenance paragraph, "The original essay, written for military education", and the closing line). That's acceptable as provenance, but heavy. The meta description opens "A teaching figure:", which is classroom wording. I found no other classroom wording on the page (no faculty, students or papers).
5. **Beat 6's card is detached.** It slides out of the AI box in about 0.4 s, then sits apart from it. Once settled, the only connector runs to the list, so the AI box ends the figure empty and orphaned.
6. **A heading appears before its content.** "ONE IMAGINED SPREAD" shows at about 1:04 over blank space, about 17 s before the spread forms. On first sight I read it as "one imagined spread of… what?"
7. **Phone view (390).** Problem lines wrap into orphan words until bullets arrive. The manager's "?" is unlabelled. The final frame drops the ten problem lines and the AI-outline link.
8. **Realism of the set-up.** Ten analysts each answering the same CEO question on their own isn't how most strategy teams split work (you'd see workstreams, or two or three option teams). It's still recognisable. Nonprofit readers get no bridge: "competitor", "dealers" and "patents" are all corporate.
9. **Slight overstatements inside the example.** "*Every* AI outline opened with that frame" is stronger than the quoted finding ("almost uniformly"). The page's framing ("each plan holds up, and the plans look nothing alike") makes surface variety the premise, but the cited Bottesch abstract reports homogenisation in style *and* content. Both are labelled constructed, but a careful reader could notice the tension with Bottesch.
10. **Transient overlaps.** For about 1 s at 1:03 (labels colliding during the shrink) and about 1 s at 1:21 (tiny in-flight words over the first column). Cosmetic.

**Fairness.**
- *To the AI:* fair. It's the source of the shared opening in act one and a useful challenger in act two, and "ALL DRAFTED WITH AI" appears on both teams, so the figure isn't anti-AI.
- *To the analysts:* fair. Every plan holds up and is ticked twice, and nobody is shown as lazy.
- *On "differentiate":* the figure doesn't say differentiate is wrong. It survives as one of three frames in the imagined spread, and all ten original plans keep their ticks. The message reads as "considering one frame means you missed others". The only lean the other way is visual: the grey "AI's frame", and the first run dimming during act two.

## Source check (after the read)

*(Added after the cold read above was written.)*

The HBR article is paywalled, and I couldn't read its full text. I worked from the NYU Stern research highlight, the ACIS abstract page and secondary coverage.

**Bottesch (2025), ACIS 2025 Proceedings, article 290** ([aisel.aisnet.org/acis2025/290](https://aisel.aisnet.org/acis2025/290/)).
- All three quoted phrases appear word for word in the abstract: "99 knowledge workers from a multinational industrial corporation"; "that indeed significant homogenisation effects occur in terms of style and content when knowledge workers are supported by GenAI (versus when they are not)"; and "initial evidence that these effects might be mitigated through advanced prompting and (more) critical handling of GenAI output by knowledge workers".
- The paper has one author (Sven Bottesch, University of Ulm), its sample comes from one company, and the abstract gives no effect sizes. The page's caveats hold.
- Supported. One tension, already raised in Problems item 9: the abstract reports *style* homogenisation as well as content. The figure's premise that the plans "look nothing alike" is a device that runs against that half of the finding. It isn't a misquote, but the figure leans on the content half and quietly reverses the style half.
- The "99" figure is not in `sources/source-spine.md` or the essay; Bottesch appears in neither. It is supported by the abstract.

**Romasanta, Thomas & Levina (2026), HBR, "Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got 'Trendslop' in Return"** (NYU Stern highlight dated 16 March 2026, matching the page's "March 2026").
- Both quoted sentences appear word for word in the NYU Stern highlight: "Across thousands of simulations, we saw LLMs almost uniformly select the same trendy strategies, regardless of context." and "We call the propensity for AI to opt for buzzy ideas over reasoned solutions 'trendslop.'" Supported.
- **Likely inaccurate: "It doesn't report which strategies the models favoured. The figure's shared frame, differentiate, is constructed."** The NYU summary indeed names no strategies. But several sources say the HBR article does:
  - an EBSCO abstract of the HBR piece (seen as a search snippet; the page itself returned 403): "Across seven core business tensions, LLMs predominantly favored differentiation over commoditization, augmentation over automation, and long-term over short-term";
  - IBTimes UK coverage: answers clustering around "'differentiate,' 'collaborate,' 'think long term' and 'augment existing capabilities'";
  - this project's own `sources/source-spine.md`, which says the models defaulted "to options such as differentiation, augmentation and collaboration".
- As written, "It" reads as the work, not the summary, so the sentence probably understates the source. It also undersells the figure: the AI's frame "Differentiate on value" matches what the research reportedly found the models default to, so it isn't merely constructed. A safer wording would be: "The authors' summary doesn't name the strategies; the article reports defaults including differentiation over cost-type options, which this figure's AI frame mirrors. The plans and lines are constructed." (Ideally, confirm against the HBR text first.)
- Secondary reports of scale ("seven models … across 15,000 simulations", Fortune, April 2026) are consistent with "thousands of simulations". Fortune also says rewording prompts or asking for pros-and-cons didn't change the preference much. That supports "regardless of context" and the page's "sameness is the default" line.
- Secondary summaries say the authors recommend using LLMs for "option expansion, counterargument generation, and risk surfacing" while humans keep the choice. If the HBR text confirms this, it's close support for the imagined team's "AI challenges" step, which the page currently presents as coming only from the essay.
- Does the figure contradict the research? No. "*Every* AI outline opened with that frame" is a notch stronger than "almost uniformly", which is acceptable in a labelled construction. The research is about models' strategy *choices* across contexts, not about a team's plans converging. The page says so ("Neither study lined up a team's plans"), which is accurate.

**Source line** ("Illustration based on Romasanta et al., HBR (2026), and Bottesch, ACIS (2025).")
- Both citations are correct as to authors, venue and year.
- My earlier note stands: "based on" plus no "constructed" in the still is the one overclaim risk once the frame travels on its own.

**Not checked:** Terwiesch et al. (POM, 2026). It's in the source spine, but it wasn't one of the two sources I was asked to verify.
