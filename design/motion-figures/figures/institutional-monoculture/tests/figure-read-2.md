## 2. The point

Ten AI-assisted papers can each look different and each pass faculty review, yet share one problem frame that came from the AI's default opening, and a teacher sees that only by lining up the problem each paper sets itself and asking which framings nobody took.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"If I pull the one-line problem statement out of every paper in my section and put them side by side, how many genuinely different framings are there, and what reasonable ways of framing this task did nobody try?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Yes, it mostly carries the idea without the animation:

- The two process labels ("AI OUTLINE → WRITE" against "DEFINE PROBLEM → AI CHALLENGES → WRITE") make the contrast readable.
- The ten ticked, different-looking papers sit next to one bracket of ten "rival" lines tagged "AI'S FRAME".
- The lower half shows three groups, and the faculty question sits underneath.
- On its own the frame says: *the first seminar's variety is cosmetic, because one frame from the AI sits underneath it all. A seminar that writes its own frame first and asks the AI to challenge it might spread out. Go and look for what's missing.*

What the still loses:

- That the frame was revealed by lining up first lines. The list just sits there, and its link to the papers isn't visible.
- That the imagined students wrote their red line before using AI. You have to decode the red bar on top of each paper.
- The tan note under each red line is unexplained.
- On desktop the dashed "?" box beside "AI'S FRAME" reads as "missing frames". At 390 it sits under the papers and reads as a missing paper.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The lower half answers the question the figure ends on, and the spread it shows can't be read.** The faculty check asks "What frames are missing across the seminar?", but just below the "?" the imagined seminar has already supplied two frames: back the partner's goals, secure what we need. A teacher could leave thinking those are *the* missing frames, or that running the essay's process makes missing frames show up, rather than "you have to bring the other frames yourself". The imagined problems also travel and sit as unreadable navy bars, while the first seminar's ten problems are readable words. So the contrast is asserted by three labels, not shown the way the monoculture is. Smaller: "ONE POSSIBLE RESULT" is stacked over "COUNTER THE RIVAL" exactly like "ONE FRAME / COUNTER THE RIVAL" above it, and reads at first as "One possible result: counter the rival". (Ambiguous visual / misread risk.)
2. **"Same frame" is shown as "same word".** The only on-picture evidence that ten papers share a frame is that each line contains *rival*, underlined in turn. A teacher could take away a keyword test: scan for a repeated noun. That would miss papers that converge without sharing vocabulary, and flag papers that share a word but frame the problem differently. The beat-5 highlight of the differing details helps, but the underline is the move a viewer remembers. (Ambiguous visual.)
3. **The AI's "challenge" looks like the AI's "outline".** In beat 8 the note that comes back is the same tan-and-grey sheet as beat 2's outline, only smaller. Nothing marks it as a challenge or as alternative frames: no question mark, no strike-through, no forked arrows. A viewer could reasonably conclude that the only difference between the two seminars is order, or that the AI still supplied the content. The essay's mechanism (AI finds missed assumptions and proposes other frames) has no picture of its own. (Caption does more work than picture.)
4. **Is the AI blamed for what the task invited? (fairness).** The context card ends "a rival's loans", and several paper problems are other context items seen through the rival lens: "Block rival ports" from "its ports", "Contest rival mines" from "its minerals". A sharp teacher may object that the task text itself steered students toward the rival, so tagging it "AI'S FRAME" is unfair to the AI. The figure never connects the AI's frame to the one rival item among five. Highlighting "a rival's loans" when the AI card appears would make the argument rather than leave it open to that objection. The only counter-evidence on screen is the imagined seminar, which is labelled a possible result. To its credit, the figure is fair to students and AI elsewhere: three imagined papers still choose "counter the rival", and both seminars are "all drafted with AI".
5. **Autoplay and playing out of view (mechanical).**
   - Autoplay starts only once the bottom of the controls is on screen. At 1366×657 that leaves about 5 px of scroll where the whole picture and the controls are both visible. Parking the picture's top 8 px from the window top left the controls 6–10 px below the fold. The figure did not start and sat on its 0:28 poster (beat 3), at both 1280×720 and 1366×657. A small extra scroll started it.
   - Once started it keeps playing when scrolled away: it ran from 0:05 to 0:13 while I was 2,600 px down the page. A teacher who glances at the beat list below or the prose will come back mid-figure.
   - Keyboard: tabbing to Play scrolls the page so that, at 1280×900, the top ~340 px of the picture is above the window, and autoplay then starts with most of the picture out of view.
6. **390 px: the motion is lost and the "?" is misplaced.** Papers are glyph-sized, so beats 2, 3 and 8 can't be followed as movement, and the "?" sits under the papers rather than beside the frame (see the size-specific notes). Desktop is the primary target, but on a phone this is a sequence of before and after states.
7. **Beat 7 moves everything at once.** The whole first seminar shrinks and relocates while the "ONE FRAME" label jumps from below the bracket to above it. Readable because the caption is short, but it's the one moment I briefly lost my place.
8. **Small motion and polish items.**
   - The beat-2 "ask" marks are nearly invisible.
   - In flight, lines overlap list text (beat 4) and bars cross papers (beat 9).
   - `?t=0:30` shows "NaN:NaN / 1:30"; only plain seconds work. This is developer-facing.

Nothing was too fast. The shortest dwell is 7 s for a 7-word caption.

**Two wording issues against the essay**

1. **"…the essay offers the pilot as a way to find out."** In context this means finding out whether the pilot *spreads a seminar's frames*. The essay doesn't say that.
   - Section IX presents the pilot as a test of whether individual students "can own a problem frame before they scale judgment through AI". Its evaluation criteria are individual: who can explain purpose through frame, who can pressure-test a chosen frame, who can recover, who can defend.
   - The essay never links the pilot to institutional monoculture. "Monoculture" appears only in the Section II paragraph.
   - Suggest something like "a pilot like the essay's could be used to find out", so the essay isn't credited with an aim it doesn't state.
   - This matters because the figure's whole lower half rests on that link.
2. **"…the essay's proposal for the next assignment."** Section IX says the sequence "runs inside a single existing assignment where framing is the central demand". "Next assignment" is not wrong, but "an existing framing assignment" would be closer.

**One addition of the figure's own, not a misattribution**

The faculty move "line up each paper's problem across the seminar" is the figure's own way of applying Section VIII's per-student frame evidence at class level. The essay doesn't prescribe it, and the page doesn't claim it does. Still, a teacher may assume it's the essay's method, because the faculty check is presented as a practice the essay recommends.
