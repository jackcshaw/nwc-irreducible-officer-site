# Institutional monoculture: storyboard cold read, round 2

Tester: first-time viewer (an educator who hasn't read the essay). Material: `tests/storyboard-for-read.md` only (Intent removed). Read on October 28, 2026. I didn't open `storyboard.md`, `storyboard-read-1.md`, or any other figure's folder. Sections 1–6 were written before I checked any sources. Section 7 was added afterwards.

## 1. Beat by beat

**Beat 1 · A seminar, one task (0–10 s).** A task card, ten blank papers with red dots labelled "The seminar", then a five-item context list. It reads clearly: ten students, one open task. The caption is short, so there's time for the card. But the five context items fade in over 0.8 s (3.6–4.4) at top right, while my eye is still on the grid at left. I'd register "there's a context list" and probably not "a rival's loans is one of five". That matters later, because beat 6 calls back to this exact item. I also noticed that "a rival's loans" is the only context item that names an actor. The other four are topics. To me that makes it the most frame-shaped item on the card, which the "Why" (context that "doesn't lead with the rival") doesn't account for.

**Beat 2 · The outlines come back alike (10–18 s).** Clear action: requests go up, grey outlines come down. The caption says the outlines are "alike", and the picture can't really prove it. Grey placeholder bars always look alike, so "small differences in line lengths" reads as generic placeholder, not as shared content. I take "alike" on trust until beat 6. There is also one "AI" box. Did they all use the same tool? If so, of course the outlines match, and a teacher might decide the fix is "use different tools".

**Beat 3 · Their own papers (18–27 s).** Strong. Grey drains into ten distinct layouts, "All drafted with AI" appears, and the ring ticks each paper. I understood: real work, real variety, everyone passes. The short ink bar at the top of each paper doesn't mean anything yet, which is fine. One small question: the outline's structure disappears completely into each layout, so what did the student keep from it? Beat 6 answers that, but here it looks like they kept nothing.

**Beat 4 · Faculty line up the problems (27–41 s).** This is the best beat. The bars lift out and grow into words, and by the fourth "The rival's …" I'd seen the pattern before any caption named it. The pace (0.6 s a line, then a 5 s hold) felt right. Small wrinkle: "The rival's loans" is a noun phrase, closer to a topic than a problem statement. I read it as "the problem is the rival's loans", and it works as shorthand.

**Beat 5 · One frame (41–49 s).** Underline, bracket, "ONE FRAME: THE RIVAL". It confirms what I'd already seen. The last-word brightening at 42.0 is subtle enough to miss, but it's only a lead-in.

**Beat 6 · Where the frame came from (49–57 s).** This beat carries the causal claim, and it's the one I'd most likely half-catch. First the AI panel shows "EACH OUTLINE OPENED WITH / The rival's influence" (good, readable). Then at 53.0 three things change together in three places: the bracket turns grey and is relabelled "AI'S FRAME: THE RIVAL", grey bands slide under ten lines, and a grey underline appears on the context card. That leaves 3.2 s of hold for the most conceptually loaded beat. Because I skimmed the context card in beat 1, the underline sends me back to read the card now, while the bracket is changing.

The underline is also ambiguous. It could mean "the AI seized one item of five", which seems to be the intent. It could equally mean "the task card planted the rival", and then the frame is the task designer's, not the AI's. The label "AI'S FRAME" asserts an origin that a sceptical teacher could dispute: in a war college, a seminar might reach for the rival with no AI at all. "Each student kept it" is fair enough. Nobody is accused of copying.

**Beat 7 · Imagine one change (57–69.5 s).** It's busy but followable. The seminar shrinks into the top half, its list becomes a column, and new labels appear on it ("THE RIVAL", "AI'S FRAME", "ONE FRAME", "AI OUTLINE → WRITE") while it's dimmed to 35%. I wouldn't read those labels until beat 8. The imagined seminar arrives, red lines are written, red copies go up, outlines come back under them, and the papers get written. The sequence label building piece by piece ("DEFINE PROBLEM → AI OUTLINE → WRITE") is the clearest single device in the figure: it states the one change in words. "Imagine" is honest framing.

What I couldn't tell: did the AI's outline change because of the student's red line, or did it still open with "The rival's influence"? The panel's opening line has faded, and the returning outlines look like the beat 2 outlines. So I don't see what the AI did differently. I just see the red line on top.

**Beat 8 · Lined up the same way (69.5–82.5 s).** The ticks land first (good: "these pass too"). Then the lines lift, turn ink and drop into three labelled columns. Because they arrive in student order, the destination jumps (rival ×3, partner ×3, our interests, partner, our interests ×2), and my eye chased between columns. "THREE FRAMES" arrives at 80.5, which leaves 2 s before the caption changes. The board stays on screen into beat 9, so I'd recover, but the payoff is tight. Also, the caption says "the same way", yet these lines drop into pre-labelled columns. In beat 4 they formed one list that was bracketed afterwards, so sorting by frame looks like a different operation that presupposes the categories.

When I read the three columns, my first thought was: "Our port access" and "The rival's ports" are the same ports with a different possessive. "The partner's debts" are presumably the rival's loans seen from the other side. Is this three frames, or three grammatical subjects?

**Beat 9 · The faculty check (82.5–90 s).** The check reads cleanly in the caption band. Then the dashed "?" bracket appears across the two empty columns of the first board, directly above the two columns the imagined seminar filled with THE PARTNER and OUR INTERESTS. My honest reading: "the missing frames are the partner and our interests, and a good seminar has three". The Notice says the imagined seminar "doesn't fill in the question for a real class", but the layout fills it in. The fixed three-column board also implies a target count.

On the second line, "Line up each paper's problem, then ask what other frames it could take": I first read "it" as "each paper's problem", which means asking per paper. The final-frame description says "the problem", which means asking at the level of the seminar. These are different instructions.

## 2. The point (my own sentence)

Papers that each pass on their own can all quietly share one definition of the problem, here the one the AI's outline opened with, and you only see it when you line up every paper's problem statement side by side.

## 3. The question a teacher should ask afterwards

"If I lined up the one-line problem statements from my last set of papers, how many different ways did students define the problem? Which reasonable definitions did nobody take, and where did the one they all took come from?"

## 4. The final frame alone

Mostly yes, on desktop. The two sequence labels ("AI OUTLINE → WRITE" vs "DEFINE PROBLEM → AI OUTLINE → WRITE") and the two counts ("ONE FRAME" vs "THREE FRAMES") carry the comparison without the animation. Both grids look equally varied and both are fully ticked, so it's clear that grading each paper wouldn't catch this. Grey "AI'S FRAME" with the bands under "The rival's" tells me where the shared part came from. The check underneath tells me what to do.

What it says to me: "When students start from the AI's outline, the seminar collapses into one frame, the AI's. When they define the problem first, it spreads to three. Ask what's missing." Two cautions about the still. First, the "?" above two filled columns reads as the answer key. Second, the imagined seminar looks just as solid and real as the first one. Only the small red label "imagined" marks it as a thought experiment, so a glance reads it as a result. The text is dense (13-unit problem lines, 12-unit mono labels), so it needs close reading at desktop size.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The on-screen unit of "frame" is the possessive.** (Ambiguous visual / confusion.) The figure never says what a frame is. It shows frames by first word: "The rival's", "The partner's", "Our". "Our port access" vs "The rival's ports" and "The partner's debts" vs "The rival's loans" look like the same evidence relabelled. A sceptical educator will call it a word game, and a less experienced one will learn the wrong unit ("change the subject of the sentence"). The caveat ("sorts frames by their first words as shorthand. Telling real frames apart takes reading.") is only in the page copy below, not in the figure.
2. **The "?" bracket gives the answer and a target count.** (Caption/picture mismatch with the Notice.) The dashed bracket spans exactly the two columns the imagined seminar fills directly below, inside a fixed three-column board. It says "the missing frames are the partner and our interests" and "three is right", which undercuts "faculty ask". A free-floating dashed bracket, or one not aligned to the lower board's columns, would avoid this.
3. **Beat 6 is overloaded and its attribution can be disputed.** (Too fast / ambiguous.) Three simultaneous changes in three places with a 3.2 s hold, on the beat that carries the causal claim. The context-card callback depends on a list that flashed in 0.8 s in beat 1. "A rival's loans" is the only actor-framed item on the card, so the grey underline can read as "the task primed it", which contradicts the "AI'S FRAME" label.
4. **The imagined outcome reads as evidence.** (Factual claim/fairness.) Frame-first leading to three frames is constructed, but it's drawn with the same solidity, ticks and counts as the first seminar. A teacher could leave thinking "define first and you'll get diversity" is a finding. I'd want to know whether any source shows that frame-first increases frame diversity. The workplace studies cited are about prompting, personas and pooling vendors.
5. **"The outlines come back alike" is asserted, not shown** (beat 2). Grey placeholder bars always look alike. The single "AI" box also leaves open whether this is one tool, which invites "use different tools" as the takeaway.
6. **The imagined seminar's AI step is opaque** (beat 7). It's unclear whether the AI's outline changed in response to the red line or still opened with the rival. The opening line has faded, so the AI's side of the change is invisible.
7. **"…what other frames it could take":** "it" can mean each paper's problem (per paper) or the seminar's problem (across papers).
8. **The beat 8 payoff is tight and its "same way" is not quite the same.** Lines jump between columns in student order, and "THREE FRAMES" holds 2 s before the caption changes. The lines also go into pre-labelled columns, unlike beat 4's single list.
9. **Colour semantics (no on-screen legend).** The first seminar's students write in navy (also faculty's colour), while the imagined students' lines are red ("decided"). Read literally, the first seminar's students decided nothing, which is a little unfair given "each student did real work". Red `#b81b2b` vs navy `#0a2242` on small bars may be hard to separate for colour-blind viewers. The sequence labels carry the meaning in words, which helps.
10. **Narrow (390 px):** in beat 8, "three labelled groups side by side" gives about 119 px per column. Lines like "The partner's voters" at 13 px will likely wrap, which changes group heights and the fixed stage height.
11. **Factual claims I'd want sourced:** the HBR "thousands of simulations … same trendy strategy advice regardless of context"; Doshi & Hauser; Kim et al. and Jiang et al. ("strikingly similar" across vendors); Terwiesch ("close to human levels"); "Sameness is the default, and escaping it takes deliberate design" (an inference stated as fact); the Section VIII frame-evidence list; the Section IX pilot order; the failure-mode lab's objection; and the kicker "Failure mode 6 of 7" against the header's "figure 6 of 8".

## 7. Source check (after writing the read)

I checked against `nwc-irreducible-officer-companion/sources/source-spine.md`, `the-irreducible-officer.md` and `labs/failure-mode-lab/cases.md`.

- **The essay's Section II quote** matches the essay text (line 43), including the Deng, Murthy and Vendrell & Johnston citations. The setup's paraphrase ("shared systems and defaults narrow the range of frames") is faithful.
- **Romasanta, Thomas & Levina (HBR, 2026)** matches the spine word for word, including the practitioner-article caveat.
- **Doshi & Hauser; Kim, Garg, Peng & Garg; Jiang et al.** match the spine. The page omits the spine's "larger, more accurate models err more alike". That's fine to leave out.
- **Terwiesch et al.** matches the spine, including "Sameness is the default, and escaping it takes deliberate design." That sentence is the spine's own gloss, not a finding, so the page is repeating an inference as fact. Consider softening it ("The authors' results suggest…") or attributing it.
- **"Failure mode 6 of 7"** is correct. Section II lists seven failure modes, and institutional monoculture is sixth. The header's "figure 6 of 8" is an internal figure count, so it's not a conflict.
- **Section VIII frame evidence** matches the essay (line 137): problem frame, key assumptions, criteria for success, evidence standard and the role AI played, "a page, not a portfolio", before submitting.
- **Section IX pilot order** is right: step one is an unaided problem frame written without AI (line 161). But step two is an *AI challenge*: students ask the AI to "identify the assumptions I may have missed, generate alternative frames for this problem…" and record what they accepted and rejected (line 163). The figure's imagined seminar instead sends the frame with a request for an outline. The essay's own AI step goes straight at monoculture by asking for alternative frames, and the figure's version is weaker and doesn't show it. Saying the figure follows "the order the essay's foundation pilot uses" is accurate, but a reader who opens Section IX will see a different AI step.
- **Problem 4 holds.** Nothing in the spine or the essay shows that frame-first increases the diversity of frames across a seminar. The pilot evaluates whether ownership is visible, not frame diversity (line 171). Terwiesch is about prompting, personas and pooling vendors. The imagined seminar's three frames are an illustration, not a reported effect.
- **The failure-mode lab objection** matches `cases.md` line 75 ("convergence may reflect a justified shared purpose or strong evidence. Difference is not inherently better"). The same note also says "Three authored examples do not demonstrate the output distribution of any model or actual class". That line supports problem 4 and could be borrowed for the "About the example" note.
- **The figure shows no numbers.** "Ten students", "five items" and "three frames" are parts of the constructed scenario, not study figures, and the storyboard labels them as constructed.
