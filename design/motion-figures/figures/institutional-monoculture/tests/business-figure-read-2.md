## 2. The point (my words)

When a whole team drafts with the same AI, every plan can pass review on its own while the set quietly shares the AI's one answer to what the problem is. Line up the problem statements and ask what's missing.

## 3. The question a manager should ask afterwards

"If I line up the opening line of every plan my team handed me this quarter, how many different definitions of the problem are actually there? And which obvious one did nobody write: cost, a partnership, the competitor's own customers?"

## 4. The final frame alone

**Desktop (1280×900 and 1280×720): yes, it mostly carries the idea.** Top half: ten ticked plans, a ten-line list bracketed "ONE FRAME: DIFFERENTIATE", a grey card "Almost every outline opened: Differentiate on value" tied to it with an "AI'S FRAME" tag, and the manager's dashed "What frames are missing?" Bottom half: "Same team, imagined" with "DEFINE PROBLEM → AI CHALLENGES → WRITE", and three bracketed groups under "ONE IMAGINED ANSWER". To me it says: *the AI's default became everyone's frame. Framing first could have spread the team across several frames.* At 720 the list text is small (about 12–13 px) but readable.

One risk in the still: the AI card and "AI'S FRAME" tag are wired straight into the manager's lineup. Seen without the animation, the frame implies the manager can trace the frame back to the AI, which is exactly what beat 6 says they can't do.

**Mobile (390×844): only half the contrast survives.** The first run's ten-line list and the AI card are gone from the final frame. All that's left is "ONE FRAME: DIFFERENTIATE (AI'S FRAME)" above the three imagined groups. "Ten lines that look different but share one frame" no longer appears, so the final frame shows a label against a board.

## 5. Problems, ranked

1. **The lines called "problems" read as solutions (beat 4, beat 10, the close, the meta description).** "Outserve everyone", "Launch basic models" and "License our designs" are recommendations. To a strategist, a problem definition sounds like "How do we stop price-led buyers defecting?" or "How do we protect margin?" The caption "lines up each plan's problem" and the check "Line up the problems" don't match what's on screen. That makes "define the problem first" harder to follow, because the red "problem" line written first ends up lined up as another strategy. "About the example" defends it ("what the plan sets out to solve"), but most viewers won't read that far. *Fix to consider:* call them "each plan's opening recommendation" or "answer", and keep "frame" for the grouping. Or make the lifted lines read as problems ("Win back service revenue", "Stop losing price-led buyers").
2. **The remedy shown is "next time, run a different process". The manager's move on today's ten plans appears only in prose.** The animation goes straight from "What frames are missing?" to an imagined re-run. The text below says the manager can answer it now, from their own market knowledge or by having the team name frames. A practical manager's first question is "what do I do with these ten plans on my desk?" The figure gives that answer only in the text below it.
3. **"The manager doesn't see this" is only in the caption (beat 6, final frame).** The grey card and tag are drawn in the same space as the manager's lineup and tied to it, so the static final frame suggests the manager knows the source. *Fix to consider:* a small "(not visible to the manager)" on the card, or a visual separator for the viewer-only layer.
4. **Beat 5 is the shortest look at the figure's main claim.** 5 s, with the bracket label up for about 2 s before the next caption. Some lines are arguable as "differentiate" (uptime contracts and bundled maintenance could be read as a service-revenue play), and a manager will want a moment to agree. The list stays up, so this costs comprehension on the first pass only.
5. **Beat 2 says "Each asks *an* AI".** The page's point is a *shared* system ("the company's AI" in the intro). "An AI" lets a viewer picture ten different tools. "The same AI" or "the company's AI" would carry the mechanism in the caption.
6. **Ten analysts writing ten separate full plans for one CEO question is a bit artificial.** I recognized the situation, since several people or business units draft with the same Copilot or ChatGPT, but real strategy teams more often split one plan into workstreams. The industrial-equipment setting, the dealers, the uptime contracts and the warranties all ring true. The nonprofit gets one sentence in the intro and nothing on the stage.
7. **Beat 9 is busy, and the bubble's speaker is unclear on desktop.** Copies fly up through the dimmed first run, a bubble appears far from the AI box, mini-bubbles land, and plans get written, all in about 6 s. Mobile labels it "The AI asks:". Desktop could too. A smaller point: "asks an AI to find assumptions they missed and suggest other frames" (the prose) describes an AI that *answers*. The bubble shows an AI that asks questions back.
8. **The colour code is never stated on the stage.** Red means person or no AI, grey means AI, navy means manager. I worked it out, but beat 8's red "DEFINE PROBLEM" line only reads as "written without AI" if you've decoded the colours.
9. **Mobile-specific:**
   - The final frame drops the first-run list and AI card (see section 4).
   - In beat 4, list items wrap mid-phrase in two columns ("Bundle maintenance / in", "Brand our / engineering") before bullets appear. "in" and "engineering" briefly look like separate items.
   - The ten plan icons are about 20 px, so "each in its own layout" barely shows.
   - The navy outlines on plans being written in beat 9 look like the manager's review ring.
   - The source line sits below the controls, outside the picture, flush against the box edge.
   - The beat list describes desktop positions ("task card at the top right", "two rows of five") that don't match the mobile layout.
10. **Small things:**
    - The poster state before autoplay shows "0:26 / 1:31", with the scrubber a third of the way along, over a faded mid-figure picture.
    - In headless Chromium I saw main-thread stalls of about 0.55–0.65 s near 0:46 and 0:52, with no capture running. That probably doesn't matter on real hardware, but it's worth a check on a modest laptop.
    - Captions have no `aria-live` region. The full description in the figcaption covers screen readers.

**Fairness:**

- *To the AI:* fair. The AI's frame is a sensible strategy. The imagined answer keeps a "differentiate" group, the imagined team still drafts with AI, and the page includes Terwiesch's counter-evidence that design can restore diversity.
- *To the analysts:* fair. "These hold up too" and the ticks make clear the first-run plans weren't bad work.
- *Does it imply "differentiate" is wrong?* No, in content. The only nudge is visual: the first-run bracket turns AI-grey under an "AI'S FRAME" tag, which a skimmer could read as tainted. The text ("a different frame isn't better by itself. A team can compare several frames and still agree on one") handles this well.

## 7. Business-edition questions

- **Would a business or nonprofit reader see their own work?** A business strategy or planning reader would: the CEO question, the competitor context and the levers are credible. The parallel-plans setup is a stretch (problem 6). A nonprofit reader has to translate from one intro sentence. Nothing on the stage is nonprofit-shaped.
- **Does the manager use only what a real manager would have?** Yes. The manager uses the finished plans, their opening lines and their own question, and never touches the AI outlines. The page says so outright. The final still undercuts this slightly (problem 3). The navy line from the manager's question to the imagined board is read as an answer to the question, not something the manager ran. That's acceptable because the board is labelled "imagined".
- **Is the AI or any analyst treated unfairly? Does the figure imply "differentiate" is wrong?** No (see Fairness). The figure says one frame across the whole team means others went unconsidered, and keeps differentiate in the imagined answer.
- **Does it contradict or overstate its research?**
  - It mostly hedges well: "almost every", "Same team, imagined", "Whether framing first spreads frames across a team is untested", "Neither study lined up a team's plans".
  - The picture shows style *diverging* while content converges, whereas Bottesch found both converge. The page discloses this as a drawing choice. That understates sameness rather than overstating it.
  - The ten outlines in beat 2 are identical (10 of 10), while the card says "almost every". That's minor.
  - The on-stage claim depends on the reported "differentiation" default, which comes from secondary coverage. The page says so (checked below).
- **Is the source line clear, without overclaiming?**
  - Mostly. "Illustration based on Romasanta et al., HBR (2026), and Bottesch, ACIS (2025)" reads honestly, and the intro adds "The team, the question and the plans are constructed."
  - "ACIS" means nothing to a business reader; "a 2025 conference paper" would.
  - "based on" could suggest the picture shows their data. The notes deny that clearly ("The figure shows none of either study's data"), but the on-stage line doesn't.
- **Military or classroom wording still on the page:**
  - "See this with: Workplace · **PME**": the edition switcher uses an unexplained military-education acronym.
  - The header "Essay" link and "Go further" lead to *The Irreducible Officer*. That's labelled "written for military education", which is fine as disclosure.
  - The close's "**Reviewer's** check" sits next to "The manager asks". It's not military, but it's a second name for the same person.
  - None of these appear on the stage: student, faculty, class, course, officer or mission. "Reviewer's check" is the only role-word mismatch there.

## 8. Source check (done after the sections above were written)

**Romasanta, Thomas & Levina (HBR, 2026)**

- **Authors, title, venue and date: supported.** HBR's page lists Angelo Romasanta, Llewellyn D.W. Thomas and Natalia Levina, "Researchers Asked LLMs for Strategic Advice. They Got 'Trendslop' in Return.", March 16, 2026. The page's "(March 2026)" is right. The body is paywalled past the opening line, and I did not try to get around it.
- **The two quotes from the authors' summary: supported word for word.** The NYU Stern research-highlight page (Levina, March 16, 2026) has "Across thousands of simulations, we saw LLMs almost uniformly select the same trendy strategies, regardless of context." and "We call the propensity for AI to opt for buzzy ideas over reasoned solutions 'trendslop.'" The page's claim that "that summary names no strategies" is also correct; the Stern page names none.
- **"Coverage of the article reports that the models' defaults included differentiation over commoditization, along with augmentation over automation and the long term over the short term": supported in substance, but the exact pairing comes from only one source I could reach, and I couldn't open that source.**
  - IBTimes UK: the advice "tended to cluster around themes such as 'differentiate,' 'collaborate,' 'think long term' and 'augment existing capabilities.'"
  - Fortune (Apr 10, 2026): models deem "'commoditization' as outdated and negative, and 'augmentation' as progressive and positive". The tensions include "long-term versus short-term growth" and "automate versus augment". Fortune never uses the word "differentiation".
  - The exact phrase "favored differentiation over commoditization, augmentation over automation, and long-term over short-term" appears in a search-result snippet for the article's EBSCO record. That page returned 403, so I couldn't confirm it.
  - Suggest citing a specific coverage source rather than "coverage", or adding IBTimes and Fortune by name.
- **Where the edition's wording differs from the source spine.** Coverage lists collaboration ("collaborate") as a default too. The source spine's entry says "differentiation, augmentation and collaboration", while the page says "…augmentation over automation and the long term over the short term". Both are supported, but the two lists should agree.
- **Context the page could add.** Fortune reports 7 models, about 15,000 simulations, and seven binary tensions; the study was led from Esade. That's consistent with "thousands of simulations".
- **How well the figure fits the study.** The study put strategy *tensions* to models as binary choices. That makes "Differentiate on value" as an outline's *opening frame* a reasonable stretch of the finding, not a direct result. The page's "follows that reported default" and "Neither study lined up a team's plans" cover this. The CEO question pits differentiating against competing on cost, which is the very tension the study reports, so the example is well chosen.

**Bottesch (ACIS 2025)**

- **Title, author and venue: supported.** "Towards Generative Monoculture? Insights from a Field Experiment on Style and Content Homogenisation Effects in GenAI-Supported Knowledge Work", Sven Bottesch (University of Ulm), ACIS 2025 Proceedings, single author.
- **"99 knowledge workers from a multinational industrial corporation": supported word for word** (abstract).
- **"that indeed significant homogenisation effects occur in terms of style and content when knowledge workers are supported by GenAI (versus when they are not)": supported word for word.** The abstract goes on: "across three representative knowledge work tasks, namely knowledge acquisition, knowledge packaging, and knowledge creation". The page leaves that out. It's worth adding, because none of the three tasks is strategy planning.
- **"initial evidence that these effects might be mitigated through advanced prompting and (more) critical handling of GenAI output by knowledge workers.": supported word for word.**
- **"single-author conference paper from one company, and the abstract gives no effect sizes": supported.**
- **"Bottesch found that style converged as well": supported** ("style and content").

**Other checks**

- **"The imagined team follows a proposal from the original essay": supported**, but the proposal is in *Section IX* (Step one, "unaided problem frame"; Step two, "AI challenge": "identify the assumptions I may have missed, generate alternative frames"). The page's "Go further" link points to Section II, which is where the monoculture paragraph is (also correct). A reader looking for the frame-first procedure won't find it at that link.
- **The essay's AI challenge asks the AI to *produce* assumptions and alternative frames.** The figure's bubble shows the AI *asking* "What did you assume? What other frames fit?" That's a small mismatch (see problem 7).
- **Numbers check, completed: "99" and Bottesch appear in neither `sources/source-spine.md` nor the essay** (nor `essays/he.md` or `essays/k12.md`). Bottesch needs a source-spine entry. Trendslop and "thousands of simulations" are in the spine.
- **Terwiesch et al. (POM, 2026):** the publisher page returned 403, so I couldn't verify it beyond the source-spine entry, which matches the page's wording.

**Nothing on the page is contradicted by a source I could reach.** The only claims I couldn't fully pin down were the exact "differentiation over commoditization" pairing (one blocked source) and the Terwiesch summary (blocked).
